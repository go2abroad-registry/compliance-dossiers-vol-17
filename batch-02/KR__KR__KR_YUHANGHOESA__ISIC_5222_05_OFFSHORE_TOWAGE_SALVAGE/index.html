Registered Agent & Physical Address Licensed Agent Required

Under Korean commercial-registration practice, the registered office must be a fixed and identifiable local address capable of receiving service of process, registered correspondence, and tax notices. A pure email-only or mail-forwarding virtual office is rejected by the Korean court registry for initial incorporation and by National Tax Service tax registration. For the maritime towage and salvage industry, the competent maritime authority and financial institutions will expect evidence of an operational place of business, including a lease contract, office signage, a local contact person, and a corporate bank account with transaction activity. A serviced office or office-sharing suite with a physical desk, postal handling, and a management service agreement may be accepted by the registry but is likely to be regarded by banks and the tax office as insufficient unless the company demonstrates actual personnel, records, and operating assets. Therefore, a physical office or a fully equipped serviced office in the jurisdiction is strongly recommended.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

E-government filing portal

Foreign non-resident entities must apply for a Business Registration Number (BRN) via the HomeTax portal or in person at the district tax office with jurisdiction over the entity's Korean place of business. Required documents include the certificate of incorporation, articles of association, proof of foreign registration, and a power of attorney if a tax representative is appointed. The NTS issues the BRN within 3-5 business days. For entities without a Korean place of business, a Foreign Entity TIN is obtained through the same process with a designated tax representative.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of goods are zero-rated under Article 21 of the VAT Act. For services, zero-rating applies to services supplied to non-residents for use outside Korea, including international maritime transport and salvage services. The service must be performed outside Korea or directly connected to exported goods.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

South Korea maintains a largely liberalized foreign exchange system under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act, with the Korean won (KRW) freely convertible for most current account transactions and a broad range of capital account transactions subject to reporting, registration, or prior approval thresholds. Capital account liberalization is substantial but not absolute; foreign direct investment, portfolio investment, and borrowing transactions are generally permitted but require reporting to the Bank of Korea or designated foreign exchange banks, and certain transactions may trigger documentary verification including certificates of foreign currency deposit, tax clearance, and underlying contract evidence. For a Korean limited liability company engaged in offshore towage and salvage, the operational revenue stream will typically be denominated in US dollars or other foreign currencies, requiring the repatriation of export proceeds under the Foreign Trade Act and the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act. The company must deposit received export proceeds in a foreign exchange bank account, and although there is no mandatory conversion of export proceeds into KRW, the exporter must report the receipt to the designated foreign exchange bank and comply with the receipt-within-18-months rule for export proceeds. Profit remittance to non-resident shareholders is allowed through a dividend remittance process that requires audited financial statements, corporate registration certificates, tax payment receipts, and a remittance application with the designated foreign exchange bank proving the source of funds and the tax withholding obligations under the Korea tax law. Stringent anti-money laundering verification, including ultimate beneficial owner disclosure and evidence of legitimate business operations, is applied to all cross-border fund flows, and the Bank of Korea maintains close monitoring of suspicious transactions. Practical hurdles for a newly formed entity include mandatory foreign exchange business registration, potentially lengthy bank KYC reviews for a marine services company, and the need to establish a clear paper trail for vessel ownership, charter contracts, and salvage awards before banks will permit outward or inward remittances. The overall convertibility regime is best classified as managed convertibility with high transparency but meaningful administrative friction for non-resident owners of maritime service entities.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Export proceeds must be received in Korea within 18 months from the date of export declaration and deposited in a designated foreign exchange bank account; the exporter must submit a receipt report to the bank within 30 days of collection. There is no mandatory conversion ratio into KRW; the exporter may voluntarily convert export proceeds at the market rate, but any failure to receive the proceeds within the statutory timeline requires a pre-approval extension from the designated foreign exchange bank or the Bank of Korea.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Korea (BOK) and the Ministry of Economy and Finance, with the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act administered through the Bank of Korea's Foreign Exchange Inspection and the Korea Customs Service for declaration purposes

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Low viability for this entity because South Korea does not recognize unlicensed foreign fintech electronic money institutions as permissible banking counterparties for a domestic corporate entity; the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act requires cross-border payments and foreign currency accounts to be routed through a designated foreign exchange bank or a licensed financial institution registered with the Financial Services Commission and the Bank of Korea. Offshore digital EMIs such as Wise, Revolut Business, or Mercury cannot provide a legal KRW account or act as a recognized settlement institution for the export of marine towing and salvage services unless they hold a Korean electronic financial transaction license, and even then the EMI would only be an intermediary and not a substitute for the required deposit bank. The corporate treasury must therefore rely on domestic tier-1 commercial banks for all KRW and USD settlement flows.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

9% on taxable income up to KRW 200 million; 19% on taxable income over KRW 200 million up to KRW 20 billion; 21% over KRW 20 billion up to KRW 300 billion; 24% over KRW 300 billion, before the 10% local income tax surcharge (effective national-plus-local range: 9.9% to 26.4%)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

22% (20% national corporate income tax withholding + 2% local income tax surcharge) on dividends paid by Yuhan Hoesa to non-resident corporate or individual shareholders, unless reduced by an applicable bilateral Double Tax Treaty.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The annual corporate income tax return must be filed within three months after the close of the taxable year; for a 31 December fiscal year-end, the statutory deadline is 31 March of the following year. Where the deadline falls on a public holiday or Saturday/Sunday, it extends to the next business day. Prepayments are due within two months after the first six months of the fiscal year.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Master File: required when annual total revenue is at least KRW 100 billion and cross-border related-party transaction volume is at least KRW 10 billion; Local File: required when cross-border related-party transactions are at least KRW 10 billion; an Integrated Report of International Transactions is required when aggregate cross-border related-party transaction volume is at least KRW 5 billion. The National Tax Service may also demand contemporaneous transfer-pricing documentation earlier during an audit whenever the transaction amount or pricing risk justifies it, especially for intercompany bunker, tug-charter, salvage-subcontract and management-fee flows.

Economic substance scope: There is no UAE-style per-entity Economic Substance Regulation filing in Korea, but the combined effect of Article 14 of the Framework Act on National Taxes (substance-over-form), the Korean controlled-foreign-company regime under the International Tax Adjustment Act, and the limitation-on-benefits/principal-purpose-test provisions of Korean tax treaties imposes a substantive Core Income-Generating Activities (CIGA) requirement. For Yuhan Hoesa, CIGA must be exercised in Korea: vessel ownership or long-term bareboat charter, vessel flag and registration, operational dispatch of tugs, crew employment and seamens' tax records, salvage risk management, commercial negotiation and invoicing, and financial accounting. If those functions are relocated to an offshore principal or a letterbox parent, the NTS can recharacterise the arrangement, disregard the interposed entity, deny treaty benefits, and attribute the income directly to the beneficial owner.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Omission of Revenue

Statutory sanction: Article 3 of the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act: imprisonment up to 10 years and a fine up to ten times the tax evaded; Framework Act on National Taxes negligence tax of 10%-40% of underpaid tax and 20% late-filing penalty; automatic public disclosure of seriously non-compliant taxpayers by the National Tax Service; seizure of bank accounts, tug vessels, charter revenue, and port-use rights.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly execute or conceal a sham return are criminally liable as co-principals, not merely as corporate representatives. Korean courts will pierce the corporate veil where the Yuhan Hoesa is used as an instrumentality of the controlling foreign shareholder; directors and beneficial owners can be ordered to make full civil restitution of evaded tax, interest and penalties, may be blacklisted and subject to travel bans in Korea, and face imprisonment. The Commercial Act, Article 401, also exposes directors to third-party damages for gross neglect of duty.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation and Disguised Profit Repatriation

Statutory sanction: International Tax Adjustment Act Articles 4 and 5 authorise the National Tax Service to make an arm's-length adjustment, compute the taxpayer's tax base as if the non-arm's-length transaction had not occurred, deny expense deductions, and recharacterise the payment as a dividend or capital contribution; the understated tax then attracts negligence tax up to 20% and interest; failure to submit documentation can lead to additional penalties and obstruction-based criminal referral; the scheme is reportable to the National Tax Service as a cross-border tax avoidance transaction.

Director personal liability: Directors who design or sign related-party pricing that lacks economic substance may be jointly and severally liable for the corporate tax deficiency under the substance-over-form doctrine and Commercial Act Article 401, especially where the arrangement is fraudulent or grossly negligent. Foreign principals who control the sham structure can also be personally named in the assessment; the NTS can impose travel bans during the audit/collection phase and criminal prosecutions under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act can result in imprisonment of the directors and beneficial owners.
AML / Beneficial Ownership Concealment and Unlicensed Offshore Towage-Salvage Operations

Statutory sanction: Under the Act on Reporting and Use of Specific Financial Transaction Information (Articles 3 and 15), failure to report suspicious transactions or UBO information is punishable by imprisonment up to 5 years and fines of up to KRW 100 million; carrying on unlicensed towage/salvage under Korean maritime law can lead to criminal fines, administrative revocation of vessel registration, prohibition on port entry, and civil fines for pollution; the Marine Environment Management Act also imposes joint liability for oil-spill cleanup and environmental remediation costs.

Director personal liability: The corporate veil is pierced when the LLC is a façade for money laundering, unlicenced operations, or environmental harm; directors, managers, and beneficial owners may face confiscation of their personal assets, arrest and imprisonment, blacklisting of the individuals and the company, cancellation of the tug and salvage vessels' operating certificates, seizure of charter receivables, travel bans, and personal environmental clean-up liability under Korean maritime-pollution law. The signatories to the escrow and wire instructions can be prosecuted as accomplices for money laundering and financing of unlicensed maritime operations.
