Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

No statutory minimum paid-up capital is required for a Yuhan Hoesa under the Commercial Act of the Republic of Korea. Contributions must, however, be paid in full before or at the time of the incorporation registration, and each member's contribution must be accurately recorded in the Articles of Incorporation and the capital contribution schedule. No blocked escrow account is mandated by the Companies Act for ordinary incorporation. If the company will hold donated or endowed assets for charitable purposes, the competent public-interest supervisory ministry may require the endowment assets to be deposited into a dedicated bank account or trust account held in the name of YUHANG HOESA and maintained blocked until the public-interest establishment authorization is issued.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

National Tax Service (NTS) of South Korea; online portal: HomeTax (https://www.hometax.go.kr)

E-government filing portal

For a Korean domestic entity such as a Yuhan Hoesa, the procedure begins with obtaining a Business Registration Certificate. The company must submit a business registration application (Business Registration Application Form) to the local district tax office having jurisdiction over the registered place of business, either online through HomeTax or in person. Supporting documents include the Certificate of Incorporation, Articles of Incorporation, lease contract or proof of place of business, copy of the representative's identification, and any required corporate governance resolutions. For foreign non-resident entities with no domestic place of business but with business activities giving rise to Korean tax obligations, the same business registration application is filed through an authorized domestic tax representative; the NTS then assigns a 10-digit Business Registration Number (TIN/VAT ID). If the non-resident entity only has Korean-source income subject to withholding and no VAT obligation, a separate simplified TIN may be issued for withholding tax administration. For a domestic Yuhan Hoesa, the NTS verifies the application and issues the Business Registration Certificate, which simultaneously registers the entity for corporate income tax and Value Added Tax purposes.

Standard VAT / GST rate

10%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

No monetary threshold. Every person supplying taxable goods or services in South Korea is required to register as a VAT taxpayer. Simplified taxpayers with annual gross revenue below KRW 80 million remain registered but are subject to simplified VAT computation rules.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating is available under the VAT Act for the export of goods, where the goods are actually exported from South Korea under a customs export declaration. Services supplied to non-residents or foreign jurisdictions may also be zero-rated if the services are treated as supplied outside Korea or in connection with export goods. A charitable endowment entity claiming export zero-rating must prove that the supply constitutes an export transaction and that the consideration is received from outside Korea in foreign currency.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Export Declaration accepted by Korea Customs Service)
  • Commercial Invoice
  • Bill of Lading (or Air Waybill for airfreight exports)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / Foreign Exchange Receipt Confirmation issued by a Korean authorized foreign exchange bank

Electronic tax invoices are mandatory under the VAT Act. Every VAT-registered business must issue electronic tax invoices (Electronic Tax Invoice) for B2B supplies and transmit them to the NTS HomeTax system. Transmission must be completed by the day following the invoice issuance date, and the system validates invoices through the NTS electronic tax invoice infrastructure. There is no independent anti-fraud digital stamping requirement equivalent to Italian or Romanian fiscal clearance, but real-time integrity verification is performed by the NTS. Non-compliance attracts VAT penalties and electronic invoice submission penalties.

The NTS may retrospectively adjust any zero-rated export supply if the required evidence is not maintained or if the foreign exchange is not repatriated within the statutory deadline. Unsupported zero-rating gives rise to a VAT assessment equal to the unpaid VAT plus an additional tax of 10% to 30% depending on the nature and severity of the non-compliance. Fraudulent claims may result in criminal penalties under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act, including imprisonment or fines, and the statute of limitations for a false export claim is generally extended to 5 years. Repeated or materially false declarations may trigger customs export verification audits and disqualification from future zero-rating privileges.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

South Korea operates a floating exchange rate regime under current account convertibility. Current account receipts, including export proceeds, dividends, interest and unilateral transfers, are freely convertible at the market rate without quantitative limits, provided the transaction is reported to the licensed bank under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act. Capital account transactions, however, are subject to declarative control: foreign financial loans, equity contributions and derivative transactions require registration with the MOEF or delegated banks, and certain short-term external borrowings face ceilings. A private charitable foundation receiving foreign grants must classify them as current transfers or donations, which are reportable but not license-restricted, since the underlying purpose is nonprofit. Profit remittance hurdles are minimal but documentary: a liquidating or capital reduction remittance requires tax clearance certificates from the National Tax Service; any grant disbursement to foreign beneficiaries requires underlying award contracts and CRS/FATCA declarations. The KRW is not freely deliverable offshore; settlement of cross-border grants must occur through an onshore foreign currency account at a domestic licensee. As of the current statutory framework, Korea does not impose a surrender ratio on export proceeds; however, exporters must receive the full invoice amount within the prescribed collection period, typically six months from the export license date, and any uncollected balance must be reported as a claim. Overall, convertibility is high on the current account and conditional on compliance on the capital account.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Under Articles 19-21 of the FXT Act and Bank of Korea regulations, exporters must collect export proceeds within 6 months from the date of export declaration; an extension of up to 3 additional months is possible for vessel or aircraft construction, overseas works and other MOEF-approved cases. There is no mandatory conversion ratio to KRW; the exporter may retain the foreign currency receipts in a Foreign Currency Deposit Account (FCDA) at a licensed domestic bank, but must report the proceeds to the foreign exchange bank and surrender the full amount for conversion to KRW if the bank determines it is necessary for trade settlement balance. Reports are filed through the bank's electronic Foreign Exchange Information System.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Korea (BOK) in conjunction with the Ministry of Economy and Finance (MOEF) as the Foreign Exchange Administration under Chapter 4 of the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act (FXT Act)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Low — foreign fintech EMIs such as Wise or Revolut are not licensed to service South Korean corporations because the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act and the Electronic Financial Transactions Act require all foreign exchange business to be conducted through domestic licensed banks; these EMIs lack permissible foreign exchange brokerage licensing for non-resident entities and cannot operate as banks within the jurisdiction. They may only function as engineered referral partners or provide pre-funded debit cards through a licensed Korean bank partner, so they cannot serve as the primary treasury settlement rail for this foundation. All cross-border grant flows must execute via SWIFT through a commercial bank with a domestic reporting duty.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

9% to 24% progressive national corporate income tax: 9% on taxable income up to KRW 200 million; 19% on KRW 200 million to KRW 20 billion; 21% on KRW 20 billion to KRW 300 billion; 24% above KRW 300 billion, plus a 10% local income tax surtax on the national corporate tax (aggregate headline range from 9.9% to 26.4%).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

20% statutory withholding tax at the national level payable on dividends distributed by a Korean corporation to a non-resident corporate or individual shareholder, plus the 10% local income tax surcharge resulting in an aggregate statutory withholding rate of 22%, unless the rate is reduced under an applicable Double Tax Treaty.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Local File must be prepared and maintained where cross-border intercompany transactions with a foreign related party exceed KRW 5 billion; Master File is required where total cross-border related-party transactions exceed KRW 50 billion; a Country-by-Country Report is required when the ultimate parent group's consolidated revenue is KRW 1 trillion or more. Failure to maintain or submit such documentation can trigger penalties, evidentiary burdens, and transfer-pricing adjustments.

Economic substance scope: Korea does not apply a Gulf-style Economic Substance Regulations regime, but the Corporate Tax Act and general anti-avoidance rules enforce a substance-over-form doctrine. The Yuhan Hoesa must perform Core Income-Generating Activities in Korea, including asset custody, grant approval, investment management, risk control, and board oversight. A Korean charitable foundation that is merely a pass-through mailbox entity with decisions made offshore can be disregarded, re-characterized, or treated as having a foreign place of effective management, resulting in higher tax exposure, penalty taxes, and denial of nonprofit benefits.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Charitable Claim

Statutory sanction: Under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act and the Corporate Tax Act, the National Tax Service may impose additional taxes of 10% to 40% of the underreported income, a 20% non-filing penalty, and daily late-payment surcharges. Criminal tax evasion is punishable by imprisonment up to 3 years and fines up to 5 times the amount of tax evaded.

Director personal liability: Korean courts may pierce the corporate veil under the Korean Commercial Act, making directors jointly and severally liable for the corporation's tax obligations and damages caused to creditors and shareholders. Directors involved in the scheme face personal civil restitution, asset freezes, restrictions on departure from Korea through blacklisting and travel bans for substantial delinquencies, disqualification from charitable governance, and criminal imprisonment under the Criminal Act for breach of trust or fraud.
Transfer Pricing and Thin Capitalization Misconduct

Statutory sanction: The National Tax Service may recharacterize the transaction, adjust taxable income to the arm's-length result, deny non-arm's-length deductions, impose penalty tax under the Corporate Tax Act for international transaction violations, and levy daily late-payment surcharges. Documentation failures may additionally trigger information-reporting penalties and evidentiary burdens against the taxpayer.

Director personal liability: Directors who approve sham or mispriced related-party transactions are personally liable for the resulting corporate tax deficiencies and civil damages under Korean Commercial Act principles. The corporate veil may be pierced where the charity is undercapitalized, controlled solely by the shareholder, and used as a vehicle to avoid tax. Directors may be subject to criminal charges for unlawful diversion of assets, travel bans, blacklisting, and imprisonment where the transaction amounts to embezzlement or breach of fiduciary duty.
AML/CFT, Sanctions, and Illicit Grant Distribution

Statutory sanction: Under the Act on Reporting and Use of Certain Financial Transaction Information and the Korean criminal law, failure to report suspicious transactions or engaging in money laundering through a charitable foundation can result in imprisonment up to 5 years, penalties up to KRW 50 million, regulatory fines, asset forfeiture, license revocation, and secondary sanctions exposure from international sanctions regimes.

Director personal liability: Directors and compliance officers may be prosecuted for willful blindness and money laundering even where the corporate entity is used as a facade. The corporate veil can be pierced to hold directors personally liable for the repatriation of illicit funds, civil asset recovery, travel bans, blacklisting, disqualification from serving as directors, and criminal imprisonment. The foundation's banking relationships are placed at risk and its tax-exempt status may be revoked.
