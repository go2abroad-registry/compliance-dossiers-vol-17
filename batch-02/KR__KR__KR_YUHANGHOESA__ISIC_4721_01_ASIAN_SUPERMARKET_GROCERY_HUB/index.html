Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

National Tax Service (NTS) of the Republic of Korea; online electronic portal: HomeTax (Hometax) at www.hometax.go.kr; export and customs declarations are administered by the Korea Customs Service through its UNI-PASS electronic portal at unipass.customs.go.kr.

E-government filing portal

A foreign non-resident entity carrying on business in South Korea must apply for a ten-digit Business Registration Certificate (Sahop Deungnokjeung) within 20 days from commencing taxable business. The application is filed with the district tax office having jurisdiction over the domestic place of business, or, in the absence of a fixed place of business, with the director-designated tax office, and may be lodged through HomeTax or in person by a duly appointed domestic tax representative. The required documentation package includes the foreign company's certificate of incorporation or equivalent public registry extract, apostilled or consular-certified certificate of foreign registration, board resolution appointing the Korean in-country representative, written appointment of a tax representative for foreign non-resident taxpayers, evidence of the local business premises lease, passport copies of the controlling principals, and a power of attorney in favor of the domestic tax representative. Once NTS verifies the application, it issues the Business Registration Certificate, which simultaneously constitutes the Korean VAT registration, corporate income tax registration, and the statutory Tax Identification Number. The same registration number must be used on all VAT invoices, e-tax invoices, customs export declarations, and payroll withholding remittances; any material change in entity particulars must be reported to the district tax office within 15 days.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation: a Korean Yuhan Hoesa is mandatorily treated as a domestic corporation subject to Korean corporate income tax on its worldwide income, and the entity is the VAT taxpayer; there is no pass-through of income or tax attributes to shareholders under Korean domestic tax law.

Available election forms

None applicable in the sense of a US-style check-the-box election or elective hybrid mismatch treatment. Korean tax law imposes entity-level taxation without elective pass-through. The only material election available is the domestic consolidated corporate income tax return regime under the Corporate Tax Act, which may be elected by a resident parent company and its 100% wholly-owned resident subsidiaries; group taxation is subject to strict continuing conditions. Foreign tax credit, branch profit election, and certain accounting-period/consolidation timing elections may apply under the corporate tax framework, but no hybrid mismatch election is available and anti-avoidance rules apply.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

10%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

Zero registration threshold: every person, including a foreign non-resident corporation, engaged in any taxable business in South Korea must register for VAT from business commencement regardless of turnover. A simplified VAT taxable person status is available for small business operators with annual taxable supplies not exceeding KRW 80,000,000, but such simplified taxpayers remain fully VAT-registered and must comply with e-tax invoice obligations.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under Korean VAT law, goods exported outside South Korea are zero-rated, and certain services supplied to or used by a foreign non-resident outside Korea may also be zero-rated if the statutory conditions are satisfied. For the Asian supermarket grocery hub, zero-rating is available only where physical goods leave South Korea under customs export clearance, title and risk pass to an overseas non-resident buyer, the export transaction is supported by genuine commercial documentation, and foreign currency is received in respect of the export sale. Domestic retail sales in Korean premises remain taxable at 10% VAT.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Bill of Entry) filed with Korea Customs Service through UNI-PASS and approved export clearance confirmation
  • Commercial invoice and packing list matching the export declaration and shipment details
  • Sales contract, purchase order, or equivalent binding evidence of the overseas transaction
  • Bill of Lading for sea freight or House/Master Air Waybill for air freight evidencing physical export movement from South Korea
  • Certificate of origin or export food-safety/phytosanitary certificate where required by the destination jurisdiction
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (FIRC) issued by the handling foreign exchange bank
  • Foreign currency bank credit advice or statement evidencing receipt and repatriation of export proceeds
  • Zero-rated VAT export tax invoice issued to the overseas buyer
  • Proof of the overseas buyer's non-resident status, such as the buyer's foreign business registration certificate or equivalent official identifier

Electronic tax invoices are mandatory for all VAT-registered entities in South Korea, including a Yuhan Hoesa engaged in grocery retail and export. The seller must issue a Korean-standard VAT tax invoice through HomeTax or an NTS-approved Electronic Tax Invoice service provider, and the electronic invoice data must be transmitted to the National Tax Service no later than the day following issuance. South Korea does not operate a real-time fiscal clearance or anti-fraud digital stamping model similar to Latin American regimes; instead, the NTS performs post-transaction cross-verification by matching e-tax invoice data, bank payment information, cash receipt data, customs export declarations, and foreign exchange receipt records. Issuance of false, inflated, or unregistered electronic tax invoices carries penalty taxes and may trigger criminal liability under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act. For export supplies, the zero-rated export tax invoice must be retained together with the customs export declaration, transport documents, and proof of receipt of foreign currency.

The National Tax Service may audit export substantiation within the statutory tax assessment period, with longer periods for fraudulent underreporting. If zero-rated export treatment is not fully substantiated by customs evidence, transport documents, and foreign currency receipt records, or if the claimed export is found to be false, the NTS will claw back the 0% rate and assess VAT at 10% on the gross export supply value, together with past-due interest and statutory penalty taxes. Penalty taxes may include additional tax for unregistered turnover, false or unfiled tax invoices, underreported export sales, and improper electronic tax invoices; those penalties can be substantial and may exceed the underlying VAT. Criminal prosecution under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act and the Korea Customs Service's customs violation proceedings can result in monetary fines, imprisonment, confiscation of the export proceeds, and permanent loss of zero-rating eligibility. Exporters must therefore retain the complete documentation bundle for at least the statutory record-keeping period and ensure that export proceeds are repatriated within 180 days, subject to the extension procedures established under Korean foreign exchange and customs administration.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

South Korea maintains a predominantly liberal current-account convertibility framework. Korean won (KRW) is freely convertible for trade settlements, service payments, and routine business expenses, subject to the repository reporting obligations of residents through authorized foreign exchange banks. Capital-account transactions are more administratively controlled: foreign direct investment inflows require filing under the Foreign Investment Promotion Act with KOTRA or a designated foreign exchange bank, and the remitted funds must be credited to a Foreign Investment Capital Account before being converted into KRW for business use. The surrender requirement for export proceeds has been substantively abolished, so exporters may retain foreign currency in domestic foreign-currency accounts. However, profit remittance and dividend repatriation to non-resident shareholders require submission of audited financial statements, tax clearance certificates, and bank verification of the capital verification certificate, and the bank is required to report the remittance to the BOK via FEIS. In practical underwriting terms, the regime is transparent and predictable for a low-risk grocery importer, but any mismatch between the stated investment purpose codes, capital account movement, and actual trade invoices will trigger automatic bank compliance review.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no mandatory conversion ratio for export foreign currency receipts under South Korean law; the statutory surrender proportion is effectively 0%. Exporters must nonetheless receive export proceeds within a general statutory timeline of six months from the date of export declaration or the date the export goods are shipped, as specified under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act and related Presidential Decrees. Proceeds must be received through an authorized foreign exchange bank and may be held in a domestic foreign-currency deposit account. If the six-month collection deadline is not met, the exporter must submit an extension application or report explaining the non-collection to the relevant authority through the foreign exchange bank. The bank is obligated to record the receipt, verify the underlying export customs declaration, and transmit the transaction data to the Foreign Exchange Information System (FEIS) within the data submission windows established by the BOK.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Korea (BOK), acting with the Ministry of Economy and Finance (MOEF) as the responsible monetary and foreign exchange policy agency under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act and the Foreign Exchange Information System (FEIS)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

The viability of offshore fintech EMIs is low for the Korean entity. Fintech EMIs are not licensed as banks or foreign exchange banking institutions under the Korean Banking Act and Foreign Exchange Transactions Act, and they are legally barred from issuing domestic KRW account numbers, acting as settlement institutions for Korean tax payments, customs duties, utilities, or local POS/credit card merchant acquiring workflows. A foreign EMI could theoretically support a pre-funded multi-currency card for travel or minor expenses, but it cannot satisfy the statutory corporate banking requirements, foreign investment capital account opening, KRW clearing, or customs and tax settlement rails. The Korean subsidiary should use Tier-1 domestic commercial banks for all primary treasury functions and may use EMIs only as a supplementary procurement card for non-regulated cross-border purchases, never as a primary settlement rail.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: The viability of offshore fintech EMIs is low for the Korean entity. Fintech EMIs are not licensed as banks or foreign exchange banking institutions under the Korean Banking Act and Foreign Exchange Transactions Act, and they are legally barred from issuing domestic KRW account numbers, acting as settlement institutions for Korean tax payments, customs duties, utilities, or local POS/credit card merchant acquiring workflows. A foreign EMI could theoretically support a pre-funded multi-currency card for travel or minor expenses, but it cannot satisfy the statutory corporate banking requirements, foreign investment capital account opening, KRW clearing, or customs and tax settlement rails. The Korean subsidiary should use Tier-1 domestic commercial banks for all primary treasury functions and may use EMIs only as a supplementary procurement card for non-regulated cross-border purchases, never as a primary settlement rail.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

24% national corporate income tax maximum bracket plus 10% local income tax surtax, producing a 26.4% composite headline rate; progressive brackets are 9% up to KRW 200 million, 19% above KRW 200 million and up to KRW 20 billion, 21% above KRW 20 billion and up to KRW 300 billion, and 24% above KRW 300 billion

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

22% composite withholding tax on outbound dividends payable to non-resident shareholders: 20% national corporate income tax withholding plus 10% local income tax surtax on that withholding (20% x 1.10 = 22%).

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: KRW 10 billion aggregate cross-border related-party transactions in a fiscal year triggers the mandatory preparation and submission of Local File and Master File transfer pricing documentation to the Korean tax administration; lower documentation requirements may apply in tax audits, and contemporaneous TP documentation is required for transactions with specially related parties in low-tax jurisdictions

Economic substance scope: Korea does not apply Gulf-style Economic Substance Regulations, but the National Tax Service enforces substance-over-form doctrines through the Corporate Income Tax Act and Article 12 of the International Tax Adjustment Act to deny DTA benefits where obtaining treaty benefits is the principal purpose; for permanent establishment attribution, the tax office requires Core Income-Generating Activities (CIGA) to be identified in Korea, and intercompany arrangements between the Korean retail entity and offshore purchasing entities must show real inventory, management, employees, and risk assumption in Korea.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Under-Reporting

Statutory sanction: Article 10 of the Punishment of Tax Evasion Act (jo-sae-beom cheob-ryeo-beop): imprisonment up to three years and/or a fine up to KRW 100 million; in addition, the NTS imposes national tax penalties of up to 40% of the evaded corporate income tax under the Framework Act on National Taxes, plus a 10% local surcharge and late payment surcharge of 0.022% per day.

Director personal liability: Directors are jointly and severally liable for the corporate tax shortfall and penalties when corporate assets are insufficient, under Article 39 of the Framework Act on National Taxes; personal bank accounts, real estate, and vehicles may be seized; the NTS may impose a departure restriction/travel ban; if the evasion involves fraudulent acts, the director faces criminal imprisonment and disqualification from serving as a director or auditor.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance and Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: Article 5 of the International Tax Adjustment Act empowers the National Tax Service to re-determine income at arm's length and impose penalty taxes of 20% to 40% on the adjusted understatement; Article 12 denies double tax treaty relief where one of the principal purposes is treaty shopping; under the Commercial Act Articles 399 and 401, courts may pierce the corporate veil to hold directors jointly liable for damages caused by intentional or negligent abuse of the corporate form.

Director personal liability: Each director becomes personally liable for the full recomputed tax, additional charges, and civil damages to the company and creditors; personal asset restitution is enforceable through NTS collection proceedings; the director is blacklisted as a delinquent taxpayer and subject to travel ban; if the shell was used to defraud creditors or tax authorities, criminal charges for fraud and aggravated tax evasion may lead to imprisonment for up to five years.
AML, E-Tax Invoice Fraud, and Foreign Exchange Violations

Statutory sanction: The VAT Act imposes penalties of up to 2% of the supply value for each unissued e-tax invoice; the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act imposes administrative fines and criminal penalties for unregistered outbound payment transactions; the Financial Transaction Reports Act imposes fines for failure to report suspicious transactions; the tax administration also disallows the related expense deductions and imposes additional corporate income tax penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors are secondarily liable under the Framework Act on National Taxes for unpaid VAT and corporate income tax; personal assets can be restrained and a travel ban imposed for tax delinquencies; the use of personal bank accounts for business revenue may trigger embezzlement charges against the director; criminal conviction may result in imprisonment up to three years under the Punishment of Tax Evasion Act and additional liability under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act for unlicensed capital movements.
