Korean law does not impose a per se ban on virtual offices or commercial mailboxes for software developers, but registration of a Yuhan Hoesa with a virtual address is operationally risky. The district National Tax Service and financial institutions require the actual place of business and may reject home-office or post-office-box addresses unless supported by a written lease, physical reception service, and evidence that management, books, and data are accessible from that address. A virtual office is therefore legally permissible in form but substantially disfavored; a leased serviced office is recommended for commercial substance.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

National Tax Service (NTS) of the Republic of Korea, operating the electronic Home Tax Service (HTS) portal at hometax.go.kr; indirect tax registration and e-tax invoice reporting are lodged through that portal, while export customs clearance and export declaration are administered by Korea Customs Service (KCS).

E-government filing portal

A non-resident foreign company that supplies goods or services in Korea, or receives Korean-source income, must obtain a Korean Business Registration Number (the statutory TIN for corporate tax and VAT). Before applying, the foreign company appoints a domestic tax representative if it has no Korean place of business. The representative submits to the National Tax Service through Home Tax Service: (i) Certificate of Incorporation and Articles of Association, (ii) foreign business license or certificate of registration translated into English or Korean, (iii) power of attorney appointing the tax representative, and (iv) a statement of the expected Korean business scope and commencement date. The NTS issues the 10-digit business registration number, which is simultaneously used for corporate tax, VAT, and employee withholding; the registration is effective retroactively to the business commencement date. Digital-service-only businesses selling to Korean consumers are treated as registered taxable persons for VAT and must file VAT returns using the same registration number, with no requirement for paid-in capital.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

10%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

There is no statutory turnover threshold that exempts a foreign or domestic supplier from VAT registration; any person carrying on a business making taxable supplies in South Korea must register. A Simplified Taxpayer filing regime is available only for individual proprietors with annual VAT-exclusive revenue below KRW 80,000,000, but such taxpayers remain registered and must file semi-annual simplified VAT returns.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under the Korean VAT Act, goods and services exported from Korea are zero-rated at 0% when there is a real export of goods evidenced by Korea Customs Service clearance, or a supply of services to non-residents for use outside Korea. For the mobile utility software app, zero-rating is available when the app is distributed through Apple IAP and Google Play Billing to non-Korean account holders, the download or use of the app occurs outside Korea, and the proceeds are collected in foreign currency and repatriated. The supplier must obtain the app store settlement report, transaction-level logs with country codes, and, for B2B export invoices, the foreign purchaser business registration evidence. Zero-rating is not automatic: the VAT return must separately disclose zero-rated supplies and attach export settlement documentation.

  • Korea Customs Service electronic Export Declaration, which is the Korean equivalent of a shipping bill or bill of entry and evidences that the goods left Korea
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill, or for digital service exports the app store settlement report plus carrier transit receipt where a physical export exists
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (FIRC) or a Korean bank Foreign Exchange Earnings Certificate showing actual collection of export proceeds in hard currency

South Korea enforces an electronic tax invoice mandate through the NTS HomeTax e-invoice system. B2B suppliers with transactions subject to VAT must issue electronic tax invoices, transmit them to NTS by no later than the day following the supply, and use an NTS-issued invoice identification number. APIs and machine-to-machine ERP integration are available through approved e-invoice service providers and the HomeTax open interface; each submitted e-invoice receives immediate validation of buyer and seller registration numbers and format, although Korea generally validates rather than pre-clears in real time. Anti-fraud control includes automatic comparison of issued, transmitted, and received e-tax invoices, refusal management, cash receipt issuance obligations for consumer sales, and automatic audit flags for missing, delayed, or false digital stamps. The fiscal digital evidence is the NTS e-Tax Invoice acceptance record, not a physical stamp.

The NTS compares export zero-rating claims with Korea Customs Service clearance records, foreign exchange bank receipts, and digital platform settlement files. If the claimed export is false, overstated, or not supported by documentation, the zero rate is reversed, the unpaid 10% VAT is assessed with statutory negligence under the VAT Act, additional tax of up to 40% can be imposed for intentional evasion or fraudulent invoices, and interest under the Framework Act on National Taxes accrues from the original filing date. Criminal referral under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act is possible for deliberate fabrication of export documentation or circular foreign exchange arrangements. Claimed refunds discovered to be improper are clawed back with the same statutory additions before any refund setoff.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

South Korea operates a liberalized current-account convertibility regime under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act and its Enforcement Decree. The Korean won (KRW) is fully convertible for legitimate trade, service, dividend remittance, and salary transactions; current-account payments are generally processed without prior approval through authorized foreign exchange banks. Capital-account convertibility, however, is subject to notification or approval requirements for inward direct investment, outward investment, loans, and financial derivatives. Export proceeds must be collected into a resident foreign currency deposit account at a domestic foreign exchange bank within the statutory collection period, but there is no mandatory conversion ratio into KRW. Profit remittance, including dividends for foreign shareholders, is technically free for non-residents provided the company is classified as a foreign-invested enterprise and submits withholding tax certificates; however, practical hurdles include historical tax clearance confirmations, annual external audit reports, and the need to prove the source of funds through FEIS filing. The Bank of Korea and selected foreign exchange banks monitor large and unusual cross-border flows under the Foreign Exchange Information System; any non-compliance can result in administrative fines and delays. Overall, the KRW is assessed as freely usable on current account and substantially convertible on a notified, transaction-based capital account.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act, Korean exporters must collect proceeds from export declarations within one year from the date of exportation, extendable only with MOEF/BOK approval, and must deposit those proceeds into a foreign exchange bank resident foreign currency account. There is no statutorily mandated surrender conversion percentage; exporters may retain 100% of export proceeds in foreign currency, convert to KRW at the prevailing market rate, or apply the funds to eligible foreign currency payment obligations, all subject to bank-level FEIS reporting thresholds and anti-money laundering documentation.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Korea (BOK) as the central monetary authority, with the Ministry of Economy and Finance (MOEF) under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act and the Foreign Investment Promotion Act

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Very Low. South Korea imposes strict licensing under the Electronic Financial Transactions Act for electronic money, prepaid instruments, and payment services. Offshore digital EMI platforms that do not hold a Korean electronic financial business authorization cannot legally issue KRW accounts, FX settlement accounts, or provide cross-border treasury services to Korean domestic entities. A Yuhan Hoesa must use a locally licensed foreign exchange bank for operating cash and FX clearing; a non-bank EMI may be used only for ancillary outbound payouts if it is a registered overseas payment provider with a clear Korean contractual interface, but it cannot replace the primary clearing bank. The recommended route is a domestic foreign exchange bank with FEIS reporting capabilities.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

All inbound equity injections must follow the Foreign Investment Promotion Act when the Yuhan Hoesa is foreign-invested. Step one: file an investment notification with MOEF/KOTRA through the designated foreign exchange bank. Step two: open a foreign investor's foreign currency deposit account at that bank. Step three: remit the capital from the overseas investor's account via SWIFT, entering the appropriate Korean foreign exchange purpose code for inward direct investment and equity purchase. Step four: the bank registers the remittance in the Foreign Exchange Information System and issues a certificate of capital verification. Step five: funds may be converted into KRW at market rate and settled into the company's operational account. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Local File: required when cross-border connected transactions exceed KRW 50 billion (KRW 50,000,000,000) per fiscal year; Master File and CbC Report: required when the multinational group's consolidated revenue exceeds KRW 1 trillion and the Korean entity's cross-border connected transactions exceed KRW 10 billion.

Economic substance scope: Korea applies economic substance via the GAAR (Article 17, Framework Act on National Taxation) and established case law. For tax incentives (e.g., Free Economic Zones), the company must maintain actual business premises, hire residents, and perform core income-generating activities (CIGA) in Korea. A letterbox company will be denied treaty benefits and preferential tax rates.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Return

Statutory sanction: Violation of Article 3 of the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act; penalty of imprisonment up to 5 years and/or fine up to twice (or three times for aggravated cases) the amount of evaded tax; plus additional 20% (or 40% if fraudulent) surcharge on the tax due.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly engage in, instruct, or conspire to commit tax evasion are personally liable for the unpaid tax and penalties; may be subject to criminal imprisonment, asset seizure, and a travel ban (blacklisting) pending investigation.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Adjustment under Article 4 and 5 of the International Tax Adjustment Act; additional tax assessment plus an understatement penalty of 10% (40% intentional) and late-payment interest; failure to produce prescribed documentation attracts a KRW 5 million per document penalty.

Director personal liability: Directors who approved transfer prices without an economic basis may be jointly liable for the adjusted tax if they acted negligently; criminal liability may arise under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act if the transfer pricing arrangement constitutes willful tax fraud.
Shell Company / Sham Entity for Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: The NTS may deny treaty benefits under the Limitation on Benefits (LOB) clause and under the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (Article 17 of Framework Act on National Taxation); corporate tax is re-assessed at full statutory rates with a 40% penalty for abusive avoidance; the entity's approval may be revoked.

Director personal liability: Directors face personal liability for the corporate obligations if the court pierces the corporate veil for fraudulent or dishonest use of the entity; travel bans can be imposed, and criminal charges for tax avoidance may result in up to 5 years of imprisonment.
