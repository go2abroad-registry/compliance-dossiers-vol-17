Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

No statutory minimum paid-up capital for a Korean Yuhan Hoesa; the total amount of contributions stipulated in the Articles of Incorporation must be actually paid (cash) or delivered (in-kind) before registration. Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rated export treatment is available for goods physically exported from South Korea under an accepted Korean customs export declaration, and for services supplied to foreign customers in foreign currency that are treated as exports under the Value-Added Tax Act (including qualifying overseas franchise licensing and royalty services, provided the service is performed/consumed outside Korea and the consideration is received in foreign currency).

  • Customs Export Declaration (Korean customs Export Declaration Form with assigned export declaration number)
  • Bill of Lading or equivalent sea/air waybill evidencing physical export
  • Commercial invoice and packing list
  • Foreign exchange bank inward remittance certificate (Korean Foreign Exchange Earning Confirmation or Foreign Currency Deposit Certificate) evidencing receipt of export proceeds

Mandatory national e-tax invoice regime: all taxpayers must issue electronic tax invoices through the HomeTax e-tax invoice system and transmit them to the NTS within one business day after the supply date. No separate real-time fiscal stamping API exists; NTS clearance is achieved through mandatory digital invoice transmission, and franchise point-of-sale daily sales records must be reconcilable with electronic tax invoices.

The NTS and Korea Customs Service may disallow zero-rating upon audit if export documents are missing, false, or inconsistent. Unverified export zero-rating results in a retroactive VAT assessment at 10% plus interest and statutory penalty surcharges under the Framework Act on National Taxes; fraudulent export invoices or false foreign exchange certificates can trigger criminal prosecution under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act, restrictions on VAT refund claims, and disqualification from future zero-rating.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

South Korea maintains a liberalized yet carefully supervised foreign exchange regime. The Korean won (KRW) is fully convertible for current-account transactions, including royalties, franchise fees, dividends, and trade-related settlements, subject to mandatory reporting through licensed foreign exchange banks. Capital-account transactions are generally free but require a declaration or ex-post reporting; certain inflows such as foreign direct investment, securities acquisition, and overseas borrowing require notification to the designated foreign exchange bank or the Ministry of Economy and Finance. There is no broad surrender requirement for export proceeds, but foreign currency receipts must be repatriated into a domestic foreign currency account or a KRW account in a timely manner, typically within two years from the export declaration date, unless an extension is approved by the Foreign Exchange Bank. Profit remittances are permissible for non-resident shareholders under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act, provided that the entity has submitted its capital registration, filed necessary tax returns (including withholding tax on dividends), and obtained a tax payment confirmation from the Korean National Tax Service. Dividend remittances are usually processed on the basis of a resolution of the shareholders meeting, audited financial statements, and a certificate of tax payment. The practical obstacle for a newly formed Yuhan Hoesa with a Chinese-linked ownership structure is not the legal convertibility of the KRW but rather the need to demonstrate the legitimate origin of the inbound capital, the authenticity of the licensing and royalty arrangements, and ongoing compliance with cross-border reporting obligations. Overall, the won is freely usable for trade and investment settlement within Korea, but strict paper trails are required for every remittance to avoid violation of the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act, anti-money laundering regulations, and U.S. secondary sanctions screening.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Under Article 14 of the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act and the Enforcement Decree, exporters must receive export proceeds in foreign currency within the statutory collection period, generally no later than two years from the date of the export declaration, unless a shorter or longer period is established by the contract or approved by the Bank of Korea through a foreign exchange bank. Once collected, the proceeds must be physically deposited into a foreign currency deposit account maintained at a licensed Korean domestic banking institution or converted into Korean won at the prevailing market exchange rate. There is no mandatory conversion ratio to KRW; the exporter has the freedom to retain the foreign currency holding in the domestic account. However, the foreign exchange bank must be informed of the receipt, a foreign exchange sales confirmation must be obtained for the underlying transaction, and any extension beyond the deadline must be justified by exceptional causes such as force majeure or trade disputes. For the self-serve broth bowl licensing business, export proceeds would primarily arise from license fees, master franchise royalties, and the export of proprietary broth seasoning or machinery; all such foreign currency receivables must be collected and settled through a designated Korean foreign exchange bank and reported under the corresponding trade or service receipts codes.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Korea (BOK) in conjunction with the Foreign Exchange Administration under the Ministry of Economy and Finance, acting pursuant to the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act and the Foreign Investment Promotion Act

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

The viability of offshore fintech EMIs in South Korea is largely non-existent for a domestic Yuhan Hoesa seeking standard corporate banking services. A foreign EMI that is not licensed under the Korean Banking Act or the Electronic Financial Transactions Act cannot legally accept KRW deposits, maintain domestic bank account numbers, or provide corporate IBAN/KR-CDC clearing access in Korea. While global payment service providers such as Wise or Payoneer may offer ancillary cross-border settlement for outbound payments, they are not substitutes for a Korean won-denominated corporate bank account. Korean regulators mandate that all domestic businesses settle KRW obligations through a licensed bank, and any entity receiving foreign currency transfers must route those proceeds through a designated foreign exchange bank for Yuhan Hoesa. Furthermore, the Financial Services Commission and the Bank of Korea have repeatedly cautioned that unlicensed EMIs are barred from providing deposit-taking services to Korean residents. Even for travel-money or disbursement functions, the non-bank EMI would need to partner with a Korean bank, effectively preserving the bank as the account holder and subject to the same KYC and AML controls. Therefore, for this franchise licensing operation with regular inbound royalty wires, reliance on an offshore fintech EMI would be a regulatory red flag, likely triggering an automatic AML escalation and potentially invalidating the corporate entity's compliance posture. The optimal strategy is to use a Tier-1 Korean commercial bank as the primary operational and FX counterparty, with only limited and clearly designated utility payment or transit scenarios through a fully licensed money remitter if ever needed.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

The economically compliant route for inbound capital injection into the Korean Yuhan Hoesa is through a designated foreign exchange bank under the Foreign Investment Promotion Act. The non-resident shareholder or investor must first file a foreign investment notification with the transaction bank, specifying the nature of the investment, the amount, and the name of the Korean target company. Once the notification is accepted, the investor wires funds from an overseas bank account via SWIFT MT103 clearly referencing the purpose code 'investment in stock' and the assigned foreign investment notification number. The receiving Korean bank will credit the funds to a temporary foreign currency deposit account, then convert the USD or CNY to KRW at the market exchange rate and credit the entity's operating account only after all KYC and source-of-funds checks are cleared. For a cash contribution, the bank will also require the issuance of a capital contribution receipt and verification of the company's registered capital. For a non-cash contribution such as the licensing of the 'malatang' brand or self-serve flavor technology, the Korean authorities require a third-party valuation certificate and a report of the contribution through the Ministry of Economy and Finance. Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

24% national top marginal corporate income tax rate on taxable income exceeding KRW 30 billion; plus 10% local income tax surcharge on the national corporate tax amount, yielding an aggregate headline top rate of 26.4%

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

22% aggregate on outbound dividends paid by the Korean LLC to a non-resident corporate shareholder, composed of 20% national Corporate Tax Act withholding plus 2% local income tax surcharge, before any tax treaty reduction.
