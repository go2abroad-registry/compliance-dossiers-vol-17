Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

None under the Korean Commercial Act. There is no statutory minimum paid-up capital for a Yuhan Hoesa. However, before registration the founders must fully pay their subscription quotas. Cash contributions must be deposited into a contribution custody account opened in the name of the company to be formed at a Korean commercial bank, and the bank must issue a Certificate of Deposit of Contribution. These funds need not remain blocked after incorporation, but they must be specifically allocated and deposited prior to the registration application. Non-cash contributions require a licensed expert valuation report and must be described in the Articles of Incorporation.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

National Tax Service (NTS) of the Republic of Korea; electronic portal: HomeTax (hometax.go.kr); customs clearance authority: Korea Customs Service (KCS) via UNI-PASS electronic portal.

E-government filing portal

A Korean Yuhan Hoesa LLC, as a domestic legal entity, must complete its commercial registration with the relevant registry court and, within 20 days of business commencement, file an Application for Business Registration (Business Registration Certificate application) with the NTS district tax office having jurisdiction over its registered seat, or electronically through HomeTax. Attachments include certificate of incorporation, articles of incorporation, notice of business commencement, lease agreement or title evidence for the registered seat, proof of Korean bank account, registered director/shareholder identification, and authorization letter for the preparation agent. The NTS then issues a Business Registration Certificate with a 10-digit Business Registration Number, which serves as both the Corporate Taxpayer Identification Number and the Korean VAT registration number. If the entity is approached as a foreign non-resident business operator with no fixed domestic place of business, the foreign entity must appoint a Korean tax representative and file the simplified non-resident business registration via HomeTax; the tax representative submits the foreign certificate of incorporation, charter, passport, power of attorney, and evidence of Korean taxable supplies. No alternative federal entity election is available.

No monetary registration threshold; any person supplying goods or services in Korea in the course of business is required to register as a VAT business operator from the commencement of business. Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rated export VAT treatment is available for battery-grade lithium carbonate powder physically exported from Korea to a foreign destination under the Value-Added Tax Act. The Yuhan Hoesa must prove: (i) the goods were delivered into the Korean export customs channel through Korea Customs Service; (ii) sale to a foreign purchaser; (iii) the goods left Korean territory and customs territory; (iv) the settlement amount in foreign currency was deposited in a bank account; and (v) the export documents are retained for the statutory period. Qualifying digital services supplied to foreign users for consumption outside Korea may also be zero-rated if the supplier holds evidence of foreign user status, foreign use, and foreign currency payment; supply of such services to Korean recipients remains taxable at 10%.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Export Declaration) accepted by the Korea Customs Service through UNI-PASS
  • Bill of Lading (B/L) issued by the ocean carrier evidencing the lithium cargo shipped from Korea to the foreign consignee
  • Commercial Invoice and Packing List
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (FIRC) or equivalent Foreign Exchange Transaction Confirmation issued by a Korean bank demonstrating receipt of the export proceeds

The Korean mandatory e-invoicing regime requires all registered VAT businesses to issue electronic tax invoices (e-Tax Invoice) through the National Tax Service HomeTax system. E-tax invoices must be transmitted to the NTS by the day following issuance through the integrated e-invoice API system, allowing real-time data validation and automatic reconciliation with customs declarations in UNI-PASS. There is no separate crypto-tax stamping window; anti-fraud digital control is achieved through mandatory e-invoice central registration, continuous NTS system monitoring, and invoice-return matching on VAT returns. Export supplies must be documented with a zero-rated e-tax invoice.

The NTS and Korea Customs Service will audit export zero-rating substantiation and may use UNI-PASS and bank exchange transaction data to verify actual shipment and repatriation. If the export documents are false, the goods do not leave Korea, or the foreign exchange proceeds are not received within the prescribed period, the zero-rating is retrospectively cancelled: VAT becomes due at the standard 10% rate, the exporter must file an amended VAT return, any refund previously credited is clawed back with penalty tax surcharges (up to 40% of the underpaid VAT, plus interest), and criminal liability may be pursued under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act for fraudulent evasion.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

South Korea maintains a largely liberalized foreign exchange regime under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act. The Korean won is fully convertible for genuine current account transactions such as trade payments, operational expenses, and profit repatriation, but every inbound and outbound remittance must pass through an authorized foreign exchange bank and be reported in the Foreign Exchange Information System (FEIS). Capital account transactions, including shareholder capital contributions, foreign loans, equity subscription proceeds, and long-term debt instruments, are also permitted for foreign-invested companies, but they require a prior report or a registration certificate issued by the designated foreign exchange bank. The key operational hurdle is not a prohibition on convertibility but the documentary discipline: banks require the underlying trade contract, customs declaration, tax invoice, and correct foreign exchange purpose code, and they have a statutory duty to reject any remittance with inconsistent or incomplete supporting papers. For profit remittance, the entity must prove distributable profits via audited Korean IFRS financial statements and a board resolution, then deduct 22% corporate income tax and 10% dividend withholding tax, subject to treaty relief where available; only the after-tax dividend is freely convertible and can be sent through the bank as long as the dividend-related tax has been paid. In practice, for a battery-metals arbitrage company, the Bank of Korea and Financial Supervisory Service will also review actual commodity trade flows and may request evidence that the lithium carbonate imports are not a disguised speculative carry trade or layered transfer-pricing mechanism.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Under Article 5-2 of the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act, an exporter must collect its export proceeds within six months of the export declaration date and must deposit them into a foreign-currency deposit account at a licensed foreign exchange bank. The mandatory conversion ratio to Korean won is 0% for current export proceeds; the company may retain the proceeds in USD or other major currencies indefinitely for working capital, supplier settlement, or treasury hedging purposes. If the six-month collection deadline is missed, the exporter must file a delayed-receipt report through the managing bank with a written justification, and the Bank of Korea may order a settlement or impose administrative sanctions. Compliance requires reconciliation of the export customs declaration, the inward credit notice, and the FEIS transaction report within the prescribed window.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Korea (Central Bank), acting under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act, with delegated banking supervisory execution through the Financial Supervisory Service and policy coordination with the Ministry of Strategy and Finance

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Low viability. South Korean statutory banking regulations require every corporate foreign exchange transaction to be conducted through a bank licensed under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act. Offshore financial technology entities such as Wise, Revolut Business, Airwallex, or Currencycloud are not authorized foreign exchange banks in South Korea; they cannot issue a domestic KRW account number, cannot legally receive or disburse KRW, and cannot perform the mandatory FEIS reporting. They may only serve as an ancillary payment corridor for funds already converted through an authorized Korean bank, and even then, the final remittance must be executed through the licensed banks. For a medium-risk battery-metals trader handling million-dollar import settlements, reliance on an offshore digital EMI would create unrepairable compliance gaps, delay customs clearance, and compromise the auditor's capital verification process. The Korean receiving bank may also reject inbound wires from non-bank fintech accounts because the ultimate ordering customer does not satisfy the identity verification and financial information reporting requirements. Consequently, the treasury blueprint must use only tier-1 Korean commercial banks.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

For a foreign-invested Korean Yuhan Hoesa, the compliant routing is as follows: the foreign shareholder must submit a Foreign Investment Report through a designated domestic bank or KOTRA, receive the Foreign Investment Registration Certificate, and then remit capital by SWIFT MT103 into the dedicated Foreign Investment Capital Escrow Account under the entity name. The remittance must be declared through the authorized foreign exchange bank and entered into the FEIS with the precise foreign exchange purpose code corresponding to foreign direct investment in wholesale trade of minerals and battery materials. The bank will verify that the fund remitter matches the registered investor, that the beneficial ownership KYC file is complete, and that the remittance narrative references the underlying Korean entity and planned ISIC 4662 business activity. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Low viability. South Korean statutory banking regulations require every corporate foreign exchange transaction to be conducted through a bank licensed under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act. Offshore financial technology entities such as Wise, Revolut Business, Airwallex, or Currencycloud are not authorized foreign exchange banks in South Korea; they cannot issue a domestic KRW account number, cannot legally receive or disburse KRW, and cannot perform the mandatory FEIS reporting. They may only serve as an ancillary payment corridor for funds already converted through an authorized Korean bank, and even then, the final remittance must be executed through the licensed banks. For a medium-risk battery-metals trader handling million-dollar import settlements, reliance on an offshore digital EMI would create unrepairable compliance gaps, delay customs clearance, and compromise the auditor's capital verification process. The Korean receiving bank may also reject inbound wires from non-bank fintech accounts because the ultimate ordering customer does not satisfy the identity verification and financial information reporting requirements. Consequently, the treasury blueprint must use only tier-1 Korean commercial banks.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Master File required if aggregate intercompany transactions exceed KRW 100 billion in the fiscal year; Local File required if intercompany transactions with a foreign related party exceed KRW 5 billion per year; for a lithium carbonate procurement arbitrage entity, intercompany cross-border purchases or sales exceeding KRW 1 billion also trigger contemporaneous TP documentation requirement under the LCITA.

Economic substance scope: Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) do not apply in Korea as a standalone statutory regime, but a Korean limited liability company engaged in cross-border lithium carbonate procurement must demonstrate real business substance to avoid PE characterization, CFC attribution, and treaty abuse challenges. The Korean tax authorities apply the Substance-over-Form doctrine under Article 14 of the Framework Act on National Taxes and the general anti-avoidance rules (GAAR) under Article 4 of the LCITA. The Core Income-Generating Activities (CIGA) are procurement contract negotiation, purchase order execution, quality inspection, logistics coordination, and pricing decisions. If these CIGA are performed offshore by a foreign parent while the Korean entity is a pure shell, the Korean entity may be subject to penalty adjustments and the foreign parent may be deemed to have a Korean PE through the subsidiary.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Corporate Tax Evasion through Transfer Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: Article 4 (Adjustment of Tax Base) and Article 10 (Penalty Tax) of the Law for the Coordination of International Tax Affairs (LCITA): unfaithful filing penalty of 20% of the additional corporate tax assessed, and penalty tax up to 30% for inadequate TP documentation. Under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act, tax evasion is punishable by imprisonment up to 2 years and a fine up to KRW 20 million; aggravated evasion exceeding KRW 300 million carries up to 5 years imprisonment and a fine up to KRW 50 million.

Director personal liability: The corporate veil is pierced under the Framework Act on National Taxes and the Commercial Act: a representative director who intentionally or negligently caused the tax evasion is joint and severally liable for the unpaid corporate tax, penalties, and additional charges. Directors may face travel restrictions and blacklisting by the National Tax Service (NTS) for unpaid taxes exceeding KRW 50 million; criminal conviction results in imprisonment, and civil asset restitution may be enforced against personal property of the director if corporate assets insufficient.
Shell Company and Economic Substance Failure

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty benefits under Articles 90-91 of the Corporate Income Tax Act and anti-treaty-shopping provisions in Korea's DTAs; the NTS may recharacterize the shell as a mere agency of the foreign parent and assess corporate tax at 22-27% plus branch tax at 22% on the deemed distributable income. Administrative penalty of 30% of the understated tax under Article 47-3 of the Framework Act on National Taxes; criminal sanctions for fraud under Article 347 of the Criminal Act if intentional concealment of transactions is proven.

Director personal liability: Directors of a shell entity who sign false purchase orders, issue inflated invoices, or submit incorrect returns may be held criminally liable as co-principals. The Commercial Act imposes liability on directors for damage caused by gross negligence or willful misconduct, allowing creditors or the NTS to attach director personal assets. Foreign directors residing in Korea may be subject to departure bans, while non-resident directors may be individually pursued through Mutual Assistance procedures.
Outbound Profit Repatriation and Dividend WHT Evasion

Statutory sanction: Withholding tax recharacterization under Article 93 of the Corporate Income Tax Act and Article 6 of the LCITA: the disguised fee is reclassified as a constructive dividend and subjected to 20% (22% with local surtax) withholding tax plus a 10% (of the additional tax) unfaithful return penalty and 0.03% daily late payment penalty. Article 31 of the Framework Act on National Taxes imposes penalty surcharges up to 80% of the additional tax where fraudulent intentional revenue concealment is verified.

Director personal liability: Directors who approved the fake service fee structure have personal secondary liability for the unpaid WHT under Article 42 of the Framework Act on National Taxes and Article 401 of the Commercial Act. Criminal prosecution may occur for intentional evasion under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act, leading to imprisonment up to 5 years, fines, and asset seizure. The NTS may register the directors on the hard-core delinquent taxpayer public disclosure list for cumulative evasion exceeding KRW 50 million, severely impairing credit, travel, and business operations.
