Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

None. The Commercial Act imposes no minimum paid-up capital for a Yuhan Hoesa and no blocked escrow account is required. Subscribers may fix the stated capital in the Articles of Incorporation, and capital may be subscribed in cash or in kind. Foreign currency contributions must be received through an authorized foreign exchange bank; the bank must issue a certificate of deposit of capital for use in the commercial registration filing. In-kind contributions require an independent valuation when the stated capital is subscribed.

Funding & unlock path: Fire Safety Completion Certificate issued by the local Civil Defense and Fire Department after inspection of fire extinguishing systems, sprinkler coverage, fire alarms, emergency exits, and dangerous materials storage separation. E-government filing portal

A foreign non-resident entity must register under Article 8 of the Korean Value-Added Tax Act. Application for business registration (including assignment of a tax identification number) is made to the competent district tax office via the HomeTax portal or in person. Available election forms

None. Korea has no check-the-box or elective pass-through regime for LLCs; all domestic corporations and LLCs are subject to entity-level corporate income tax. Hybrid mismatch adjustments apply under Korean Corporate Tax Act implementing OECD BEPS Action 2, but there is no election mechanism.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

10%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

No mandatory registration threshold; all persons making taxable supplies must register. Simplified taxpayer (simplified VAT) applies to small businesses with annual revenue below KRW 80,000,000, but foreign non-resident entities engaged in taxable supplies are also required to register regardless of turnover.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating is available under Articles 21 and 22 of the Korean Value-Added Tax Act for (i) export of goods (actual delivery/transport out of Korea); (ii) services performed outside Korea or supplied to a foreign non-resident person; and (iii) international maritime or air transport. For DG (dangerous goods) forwarding, zero-rating applies to freight charges and customs brokerage services attributable to export cargo, provided the underlying movement of goods is lawfully exported under Customs Act procedures and supported by shipping/airway documents.

  • Export Declaration (Export Bill of Entry) filed with Korea Customs Service (UNI-PASS)
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill (AWB) evidencing international carriage
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or Foreign Exchange Earnings Certificate (foreign currency receipt proof)
  • Commercial Invoice
  • Packing List
  • Certificate of Origin (if required under the relevant FTA)

Since 2012, all VAT-registered persons must issue and transmit electronic tax invoices (e-Tax Invoice) through the HomeTax e-Invoice system in real time. Issuance must occur no later than the day following the supply, and the encrypted digital XML/JSON message must be authenticated under NTS-approved certification. There is no physical clearance stamping; compliance is enforced via real-time API reporting and automated cross-checking of invoice data against VAT returns.

False zero-rating claims are subject to penalty tax of 20% of the amount excluded and, in cases of fraud or negligence, 30% to 40% of the VAT evaded; criminal prosecution is possible under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act. The NTS may retrospectively claw back zero-rated input tax credits and assess additional VAT on the original supply, plus monthly 0.025% non-payment surcharge for up to 60 months. Documentary insufficiency triggers full disallowance of the export zero-rating benefit.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

South Korea maintains a largely liberalised current account, with foreign exchange transactions governed by the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act and delegated to foreign exchange banks. Capital account transactions, including inbound foreign direct investment, are generally free but must be reported through designated foreign exchange banks or KOTRA under the Foreign Investment Promotion Act. The Korean won (KRW) is convertible for trade and business-related current account flows, but onshore KRW remains restricted in offshore markets; non-residents may not freely open onshore KRW accounts without a permitted economic purpose. Profit remittances are allowed once the company demonstrates tax payment and audited distributable profits, but compliance with foreign exchange reporting, suspected terrorism financing checks, and documentary evidence from the foreign exchange bank is required. There is no surrender requirement for export proceeds, but exporters must collect proceeds within the statutory realisation period, generally 180 days from export declaration, and either deposit them in a foreign currency account with a domestic bank or sell them to a licensed foreign exchange bank. Stringent anti-money laundering screening applies to high-risk dangerous goods forwarding entities, making bank-level convertibility conditional on clean corporate and trade records.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No mandatory conversion ratio is in effect. Export proceeds must be received and realised through a licensed foreign exchange bank within 180 days from the date of export declaration; the period may be extended on application for justifiable trade reasons. The proceeds may be held in a domestic foreign-currency deposit account without compulsory KRW conversion, but the bank must be notified and the export collection must be closed in the Electronic Customs Clearance system.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Korea (BOK) and the Ministry of Economy and Finance (MOEF) as the foreign exchange authority under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Offshore digital EMI platforms, including multi-currency e-money wallets and virtual IBAN aggregators without a Korean foreign exchange business licence, are not legally permissible as the primary settlement rail for this entity. The Foreign Exchange Transactions Act restricts foreign exchange arbitrage and payment processing to licensed foreign exchange banks; an unlicensed EMI cannot perform cross-border KRW clearing, cannot file Korean foreign exchange reports, and would automatically fail the bank's approved payment account test during the high-risk DG forwarding review. An EMI may be used only as a secondary, low-value disbursement channel if the underlying funds are held with a licensed domestic bank and the EMI has a valid partnership with that bank; primary capital injection and trade payments must remain on SWIFT, MT103, and domestic won wire rails.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound investment must be structured under the Foreign Investment Promotion Act. The foreign investor files a foreign investment report with KOTRA or a designated foreign exchange bank using the prescribed FDI form; then the capital remittance is wired through SWIFT MT103 using the appropriate purpose code for foreign direct investment from the parent's account to the Yuhan Hoesa capital escrow account in Korea. The receiving bank performs foreign investment verification, accepts the foreign currency, and issues a Foreign Investment Registration Certificate. The company completes capital verification from the bank's foreign investment confirmation. The funds must be held in the capital escrow account and later converted to KRW through a licensed bank at the market rate for operational use. All onward transfers require matching underlying invoices or funding plans to avoid violation of foreign exchange verification.For outbound settlements from the DG forwarding business, the company should mainly use SWIFT MT103 for general freight invoice payments and agent commissions; commercial letters of credit (MT700/MT710) for overseas payables where bank documentary protection is required; and documentary collections (DP/DA) for smaller, trusted customer relationships. Domestic KRW payments to local carriers, port authorities, and customs duties should be settled on the Korean wire system through the same licensed bank, while foreign currency accounts at the Tier-1 bank handle USD/EUR/JPY clearing. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Local File and Master File are mandatory when international related-party transactions exceed KRW 5 billion per fiscal year; CbC reporting applies when the multinational group's consolidated revenue exceeds KRW 100 billion or the OECD threshold as implemented.

Economic substance scope: Korea does not operate a standalone ESR/CIGA self-certification regime, but economic substance is enforced through the substance-over-form principle, controlled foreign company rules, General Anti-Avoidance Rules, and treaty anti-abuse provisions; for a DG freight forwarding company, management, personnel, storage, and core income-generating activities must genuinely occur in Korea to preserve deductions and treaty benefits.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Intentional False Declaration

Statutory sanction: 40% fraudulent underreporting penalty under National Tax Basic Law; criminal prosecution under Punishment of Tax Evasion Act Article 3; imprisonment up to 5 years and fines scaled to the evaded tax; publication of severe tax delinquents.

Director personal liability: Directors who participated in fraud are jointly and severally liable for unpaid corporate taxes and penalties; assets can be attached personally; NTS can impose travel bans and blacklist non-compliant directors; criminal conviction may result in imprisonment and disqualification from corporate management.
Shell Company and Transfer Pricing Abuse

Statutory sanction: Transfer pricing adjustment to arm's length result; denial of deductions under Korean TP law; GAAR recapture of tax benefit; additional penalties up to 40% of the understated income; possible CFC inclusion of offshore retained profits.

Director personal liability: Veil piercing may attach civil liability to directors for confirmed tax deficiencies; directors can be sued for breach of fiduciary duty; overseas travel restriction and criminal exposure arise if the arrangement is treated as deliberate tax fraud.
Unlicensed Hazardous Goods Operations and AML Violation

Statutory sanction: Revocation of logistics license, trade restrictions, confiscation of illegal profits, administrative fines under the Dangerous Goods Safety Management Act and Foreign Trade Act; criminal referral for wilful non-compliance and financial crime.

Director personal liability: Directors face personal disqualification from regulated logistics activities, civil damages to shippers and third parties, travel bans, asset freezes, and criminal imprisonment; corporate officers cannot insulate themselves through the limited liability shield when they knowingly breached safety and anti-money-laundering statutes.
