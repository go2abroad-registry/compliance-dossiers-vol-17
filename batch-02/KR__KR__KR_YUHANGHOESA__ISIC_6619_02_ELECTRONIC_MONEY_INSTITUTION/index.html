Registered Agent & Physical Address Licensed Agent Required

A virtual office or commercial mailbox is categorically rejected for a licensed Electronic Money Institution in South Korea. The Financial Services Commission and the Bank of Korea require a physical, staffed office where the EMI's compliance officer, internal audit function, and independent directors can work, and where regulatory notices can be served. Korean tax authorities also refuse a virtual address for VAT and corporate tax registration because the office must be capable of storing accounting records, vouchers, and electronic contract files. Therefore, only a leasehold or owned physical office with exclusive access will satisfy bank and regulator due diligence.

Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

For a Yuhan Hoesa LLC under the Commercial Act, there is no statutory minimum paid-up capital threshold; however, for an Electronic Money Institution (pre-paid electronic payment means issuer) under the Electronic Financial Transactions Act, an operating license from the FSC requires paid-in capital of at least KRW 2 billion (approximately USD 1.5 million), and the capital must be held in an escrowed checking account with a Korean commercial bank under the FSC's 'paid-in capital verification escrow procedure' for at least 30 days prior to the license issuance, with an official verification letter from the escrow bank.

Funding & unlock path: Fire Safety Certificate (소방시설완비증명서) under the Firefighting Facilities Installation and Maintenance Act is required for the registered office if the floor area exceeds 100 square meters; the company must install fire extinguishers, an emergency evacuation map, a fire alarm system, and a fire-fighting water connection in compliance with Civil Defense Act Article 22. Structural load inspections are not required for a general office lease under a classified office building with a valid occupancy certificate; however, if the company intends to house its own IT equipment racks, a floor loading certificate of not less than kg/m² must be obtained from the building owner. Workplace safety approval under the Occupational Safety and Health Act is mandatory, and the company must appoint a safety and health manager if the number of employees exceeds 50, and must conduct an annual regular safety training and health hazard evaluation, and provide ergonomic workstations for its employees.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

10%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

KRW 0 for the domestic Yuhan Hoesa: VAT registration is mandatory as soon as business commences and no minimum economic turnover threshold can defer registration for a corporation. Foreign non-resident electronic-service suppliers may be subject to special deemed supply and registration rules under the Value-Added Tax Act, but the domestic entity must register regardless of turnover.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating under Article 11 of the Value-Added Tax Act applies to (a) exported goods physically cleared by Korea Customs Service and removed from Korea; (b) services rendered to non-Korean residents for foreign use, including cross-border payment-processing fees, virtual-IBAN issuance fees, and digital wallet reserve-management services supplied to offshore financial institutions; and (c) international carriage of goods or passengers. To claim 0% VAT, the entity must prove the supply occurred beyond Korean VAT territory: goods require a customs export declaration and transport document; services require a non-resident recipient, offshore consumption or contractual delivery outside Korea, and foreign-currency settlement. Domestic-facing services such as Korean card-scheme clearing and local central-bank settlement remain taxable at 10%, so the EMI must separate taxable domestic payment certainty/clearing services from offshore zero-rated services.

  • Customs Export Declaration under the Korea Customs Service UNI-PASS electronic clearance system (the Korean export declaration acts as the bill-of-entry/equivalent export clearance instrument),
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill (cargo receipt) showing the consignee outside Korea and actual shipping departure,
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / Foreign Exchange Transaction Confirmation issued by the Korean foreign-exchange bank evidencing that export proceeds were received within 180 days in the invoiced foreign currency,
  • For zero-rated offshore services and digital financial supplies: an executed export service contract or purchase order, evidence of the foreign recipient's non-resident status, invoice issued to the offshore counterparty, bank SWIFT or MT103 payment confirmation showing offshore funding, and internal records proving the service is consumed or performed outside Korea.

Every corporation, including the licensed EMI, must issue electronic tax invoices via the NTS HomeTax e-Tax Invoice system or through a licensed API-integrated ASP. The e-tax invoice must be issued no later than the day of supply or receipt of payment, whichever occurs first, and must bear the NTS-controlled invoice number. Bearer, buyer and seller Tax Identification Numbers and supply classification (zero-rated/taxable) must be transmitted electronically; the NTS performs real-time validation against VAT filings and mandatory matching in the VAT return clearance cycle. Since the removal of the supply threshold for mandatory e-invoicing, all general taxpayers are subject to the electronic issuance obligation. Korea does not use a physical fiscal digital stamp; anti-fraud control is achieved through API-based digital transmission, pre-issued NTS invoice serial numbers and reconciliation penalties.

If the NTS determines that zero-rating was claimed without the mandatory customs, transport, foreign-currency and non-resident-recipient evidence, it will reassess the supply as taxable at 10% VAT on the whole export consideration and impose statutory late-payment surcharges. Under the Framework Act on National Taxes, negligent underreporting triggers a 10% additional tax and fraudulent underreporting a 40% additional tax; failure to issue or transmit an e-tax invoice triggers a separate 1%-of-supply-value administrative penalty under the Value-Added Tax Act. Intentional export zero-rating fraud is prosecuted under the Punishment of Tax Offenses Act with imprisonment up to 3 years or a fine up to KRW 30 million. The normal five-year collection prescription is extended to ten years for fraudulent evasion, allowing retrospective reopening and clawback of unlawful VAT refunds.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Korean won (KRW) is fully convertible for current account transactions, including trade in goods, services, and routine profit remittances, subject to documentary verification and reporting under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act. Capital account transactions, including inbound equity injections, intercompany loans, and certain portfolio investments, require a declaration to a designated foreign exchange bank or, in larger or sensitive cases, prior notification to the MOEF. There is no mandatory surrender of export proceeds into KRW; however, exporters must repatriate foreign currency receipts to Korea within a statutory period and may hold them in foreign currency accounts, effectively allowing retention of export earnings in USD, EUR, JPY, or KRW. For a licensed electronic money institution (EMI) classified under ISIC 6619, profit remittance to foreign shareholders is generally permitted after statutory external audit and tax clearance, provided the remittance is executed through a bank with a Foreign Exchange Business License and the underlying source-of-funds documentation (e.g., audited financial statements, tax payment certificates, board resolution) is presented. In practice, Korean banks apply heightened due diligence to fintech entities, especially those with virtual IBAN issuance and digital wallet reserve management, requiring full transparency of the reserve account structure, segregation of customer funds, and compliance with the Electronic Financial Transactions Act and Act on Registration of Loan Business. Any transaction involving cross-border movement of digital assets or virtual IBAN facilities will be scrutinized under the Specific Financial Information Act (anti-money laundering / countering the financing of terrorism, AML/CFT) and the Financial Services Commission's virtual asset guidelines, which may treat the entity as a Virtual Asset Service Provider for certain activities and impose additional reporting and real-name verification obligations.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No mandatory conversion of export proceeds into KRW exists. Under Article 20 of the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act and MOEF notification, exporters must collect and repatriate export proceeds within 18 months from the date of export declaration, and they may retain these proceeds in foreign currency accounts at domestic banks. The full amount of export proceeds may be retained in foreign currency without surrender; however, any conversion into KRW requires a transparent record of the underlying transaction and is processed at market rates by the designated foreign exchange bank. Failure to repatriate within the statutory timeline without an approved extension triggers administrative fines and potential AML review.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Korea (BOK) and the Ministry of Economy and Finance (MOEF) via the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act; operational enforcement through the Korea Customs Service and designated foreign exchange banks

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Offshore digital EMI services (e.g., non-bank e-money institutions domiciled in the EU or UK) are legally barred from providing core deposit-taking or domestic KRW settlement services to Korean entities unless they hold a license under the Electronic Financial Transactions Act or a Specialized Credit Financial Business License. Such EMIs cannot directly access the Bank of Korea's won clearing system (BOK-Wire+ and the Cheque and Clearing House). Their viability is limited to auxiliary roles such as issuing virtual IBANs for inbound GBP/EUR settlement or providing multi-currency accounts outside Korea, with funds converted to KRW via a licensed Korean bank. Therefore, the treasury blueprint must rely on Tier-1 domestic Korean banks for any onshore KRW liquidity, capital held in escrow, and customer reserve safeguarding. Cross-border payment initiation can be supported by a licensed Korean electronic financial intermediary (e.g., Naver Pay or Kakao Pay if partnering with a bank) but the underlying settlement rail must ultimately be a domestic bank clearing account.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound foreign direct investment (FDI) capital injection must be routed through a designated foreign exchange bank in Korea. The investor files an investment declaration with the bank or MOEF under the Foreign Investment Promotion Act, after which the bank issues a Certificate of Foreign Investment. The wire transfer should use the acceptable purpose code 'Foreign Direct Investment' under the Bank of Korea's reporting system, and the counterparty bank should receive funds in KRW (or convertible major currencies) with SWIFT MT103 messages clearly referencing the company name, tax ID, and the Certificate of Foreign Investment number. For an EMI, the invested capital should initially credit a Capital Escrow Account; after the paid-in capital verification by the external auditor and issuance of a capital verification certificate by the registered accountant, the funds can be fully transferred into the operating won account. If the investment includes convertible notes or loans, an additional preliminary declaration under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act is required, and interest repatriation is subject to withholding tax. Any delay or misreporting of the investment declaration may result in administrative fines and potential recharacterization as a non-permitted loan.Outbound trade settlements from the Korean EMI will primarily use SWIFT MT103 for direct operational payments, corporate-to-corporate transfers, and vendor disbursements. For larger value transactions, a Commercial Letter of Credit (L/C) or Documentary Collection (D/P and D/A) may be used for physical goods inputs; however, for digital services and licensing fees, bank guarantees and plain SWIFT wire transfers are preferred. Korean banks require a 'Transaction Confirmation Sheet' and supporting underlying invoices for each outward payment, citing the correct Korean Economic Transaction Classification Code (e.g., Incidental Service Fees, Royalties, or Software Development). For disbursements to domestic e-commerce merchants, the EMI may interface with the Bank of Korea's BOK-Wire+ settlement system via its partner bank for KRW-denominated instant transfers. Cross-border disbursements in USD/EUR are executed via the bank's correspondent network, with the designated bank performing the mandatory sanctions screening at both the transactional and beneficiary levels.Onshore KRW/USD forward contracts for up to 12 months with a Korean commercial bank, permissible when there is a real export/import contract or a streaming digital service revenue contract, subject to a bank-provided hedging line and underlying exposure verification.Non-deliverable forwards (NDFs) on KRW with offshore counterparties are permitted for Korean financial institutions only under limited circumstances; for non-financial corporate entities like the EMI, NDFs may be allowed to hedge cross-border trade or capital exposure if routed through a domestic bank's offshore branch, but underlying trade documents are mandatory.Cross-currency swaps for converting USD debt into EUR or KRW liabilities are permissible when the structure is aligned with the actual obligations of the EMI (e.g., investor loans or digital asset reserve funding); these require an economically substantive asset-liability match and must be reported to the designated foreign exchange bank.FX options are not automatically available to all corporate treasuries; Korean commercial banks typically restrict plain vanilla options to entities with strong balance sheets and require a board-approved hedging policy, additional margin, and a clear risk absorption statement.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Offshore digital EMI services (e.g., non-bank e-money institutions domiciled in the EU or UK) are legally barred from providing core deposit-taking or domestic KRW settlement services to Korean entities unless they hold a license under the Electronic Financial Transactions Act or a Specialized Credit Financial Business License. Such EMIs cannot directly access the Bank of Korea's won clearing system (BOK-Wire+ and the Cheque and Clearing House). Their viability is limited to auxiliary roles such as issuing virtual IBANs for inbound GBP/EUR settlement or providing multi-currency accounts outside Korea, with funds converted to KRW via a licensed Korean bank. Therefore, the treasury blueprint must rely on Tier-1 domestic Korean banks for any onshore KRW liquidity, capital held in escrow, and customer reserve safeguarding. Cross-border payment initiation can be supported by a licensed Korean electronic financial intermediary (e.g., Naver Pay or Kakao Pay if partnering with a bank) but the underlying settlement rail must ultimately be a domestic bank clearing account.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

25% national headline CIT rate on taxable income exceeding KRW 300 billion, plus 10% local income tax surtax, producing a combined top effective headline rate of 27.5%; lower national brackets are 10% on income up to KRW 200 million, 20% on KRW 200 million to KRW 20 billion, and 22% on KRW 20 billion to KRW 300 billion, each subject to the 10% local surtax.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

22% combined statutory withholding on dividends paid by the Korean LLC to non-resident shareholders, comprising 20% national withholding under Corporate Tax Act Article 98(1) plus 2% local income tax surtax; bilateral tax treaties may reduce the combined rate to 5-10% on qualified dividends, typically subject to minimum shareholding and beneficial-ownership requirements.
