A virtual office or commercial mailbox is not permissible as the registered office for a Korea Customs Service licensed customs brokerage entity. The registered office must be a fixed physical place within the customs control district, evidenced by a lease agreement for at least one year, a visible door sign matching the registered Hangul corporate name, dedicated office space suitable for storing customs declarations and bonded transit records, telephone and utility connections, and availability for service of process and unannounced inspections. Korean commercial banks will reject a virtual office address when opening corporate operating accounts because the Real Name Financial Transactions Act requires in-person verification of officers and a physical business address consistent with the employment contract and tax invoice registry; local tax authorities similarly reject virtual office addresses for business registration under the Value-Added Tax Act. For a non-resident-owned LLC, the registered office must be maintained through a resident physical office manager or an authorized compliance agent in Korea, and the office must remain continuously open during ordinary business hours.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under Value-Added Tax Act Article 21 and its Presidential Decree, exported goods are zero-rated when the goods are physically exported from Korea under an accepted Korea Customs Service export declaration, are delivered to a foreign buyer, and the exporter retains evidence of export clearance and inward repatriation of export proceeds. For services supplied by this licensed customs brokerage and bonded transit management company, zero-rating applies only where the service is supplied to a non-resident recipient, is part of the international transport or export clearance chain, and is used outside Korea; domestic customs clearance services for local importers remain standard-rated at 10%. The zero-rated supply must be reported on the VAT return, issued as an export tax invoice, and supported by the full documentary bundle. The input VAT attributable to zero-rated export supplies may be recovered through the ordinary VAT return or an early VAT refund application to the NTS.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Export Bill of Entry / export declaration receipt accepted by Korea Customs Service through the UNI-PASS system, showing the exporting business registration number and HS classification)
  • Bill of Lading (or Air Waybill for air cargo) issued in the Korean exporter's name
  • Commercial Invoice and export sales contract identifying the overseas buyer and the precise services or goods supplied
  • Packing List and cargo manifest as filed by the licensed customs broker
  • Licensed Customs Brokerage engagement record and broker's Customs Act filing log evidencing the export clearance mandate
  • Foreign Exchange Settlement Certificate / Inward Remittance Certificate (FIRC) issued by an authorized Korean foreign exchange bank showing receipt of export proceeds in convertible foreign currency
  • Bank confirmation or foreign exchange transaction record proving repatriation of export proceeds within the statutory period

Electronic tax invoice issuance is mandatory under the Value-Added Tax Act through the NTS HomeTax Service. Tax invoices for taxable supplies are transmitted to the HomeTax e-Tax Invoice system upon issuance, and each valid electronic tax invoice receives an NTS issuance/approval number that functions as an anti-fraud digital control. The system operates as a real-time transmission and cross-matching model rather than a pre-approval fiscal clearance model. Registered businesses may transmit via the NTS open API or use the HomeTax web interface; anti-fraud controls include public key certificate authentication, unique NTS-assigned invoice numbers, issuer/receiver cross-matching, and automatic reconciliation against VAT returns and customs export declarations.

During audit, the NTS cross-references VAT returns and e-tax invoices with Korea Customs Service UNI-PASS export declarations and bank foreign exchange receipts. If the mandatory export documentation and foreign exchange repatriation evidence are not produced, export proceeds are not collected within 180 days without approved extension, or the export is determined to be a fabricated or circular transaction, the zero-rating is retroactively denied, 10% VAT is reassessed on the supply value, and statutory additional taxes apply: 20% for underreporting and 40% for fraudulent or malicious non-reporting under the Framework Act on National Taxes and the Value-Added Tax Act, together with late-payment interest and clawback of any VAT refund already paid. Submission of a false export declaration or false electronic tax invoice also triggers criminal liability under the Punishment of Tax Offenses Act and customs fines/penalties under the Customs Act, including imprisonment and monetary penalties for the responsible officers and the licensed customs broker.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

South Korea operates an open yet prudently regulated foreign exchange regime under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act. The Korean won (KRW) is fully convertible for current account transactions such as trade settlements, remittance of service fees, dividends from foreign investment, and ordinary customs brokerage payments. Capital account transactions, including equity injections, cross-border loans, and real estate acquisitions, are subject to prior declaration, registration, or reporting to a licensed foreign exchange bank or the Bank of Korea. There is no broad surrender requirement for export proceeds; exporters are generally permitted to retain foreign currency in licensed foreign currency accounts, although all receipts must be repatriated through a domestic foreign exchange bank and reported via the Foreign Exchange Information System (FEIS). Profit and dividend remittance out of Korea is permitted if the original foreign investment was duly registered under the Foreign Investment Promotion Act and the payout is supported by audited earnings; however, the banking institution will conduct source-of-funds and tax clearance checks. In practice, authority remains concentrated in the Korean financial regulatory ecosystem, requiring a resident domestic bank relationship for any significant KRW clearing, and non-residents seeking to establish treasury operations must navigate the requirement for local corporate registration, tax registration, and a foreign investment certificate for capital verification.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Export proceeds must be received through a registered domestic foreign exchange bank and must be reported to the authorities via the Foreign Exchange Information System (FEIS). The statutory collection deadline is 15 months from the date of the export declaration, unless prior approval for extension is obtained from the Foreign Exchange Administration. There is no mandatory conversion ratio for ordinary export foreign currency receipts; exporters may hold 100% of proceeds in foreign currency accounts. However, upon any conversion into KRW, the transaction must be executed at a licensed bank and coded under an approved foreign exchange purpose code.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Korea (BOK) and the Foreign Exchange Administration under the Ministry of Economy and Finance (MOEF)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Limited to non-feasible. Non-resident fintech electronic money institutions (EMIs) and virtual banking entities such as Revolut Business and Wise Business do not hold a domestic banking charter in South Korea and therefore cannot provide KRW-denominated local clearing, IBAN-equivalent KRW accounts, or access to Korean domestic payment rails (e.g., KFTC / HOFOS). They also cannot act as the primary foreign exchange bank for export proceeds surrender reporting or Foreign Investment Promotion Act capital registration. Such EMIs may be used solely as auxiliary payment gateways for trivial cross-border USD transfers outside the KRW clearing system, but all regulated treasury functions must be routed through a licensed commercial bank in Korea.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

All inbound foreign direct investment must be routed through a licensed Korean foreign exchange bank. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Limited to non-feasible. Non-resident fintech electronic money institutions (EMIs) and virtual banking entities such as Revolut Business and Wise Business do not hold a domestic banking charter in South Korea and therefore cannot provide KRW-denominated local clearing, IBAN-equivalent KRW accounts, or access to Korean domestic payment rails (e.g., KFTC / HOFOS). They also cannot act as the primary foreign exchange bank for export proceeds surrender reporting or Foreign Investment Promotion Act capital registration. Such EMIs may be used solely as auxiliary payment gateways for trivial cross-border USD transfers outside the KRW clearing system, but all regulated treasury functions must be routed through a licensed commercial bank in Korea.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Local File and Master File are mandatory when the total annual amount of cross-border related-party transactions with foreign related enterprises exceeds KRW 10 billion; a simplified contemporaneous transfer pricing report is sufficient where the total transaction amount is at or below KRW 5 billion; customs brokerage and freight-forwarding transactions to foreign group entities must be priced on an arm's length basis and the benchmark study must separately analyze the brokerage service margin and the customs duty valuation treatment

Economic substance scope: Korea enforces a substance-over-form doctrine through anti-abuse rules in the International Tax Adjustment Act, controlled foreign company rules, and judicial disregard of artificial corporations. A customs brokerage Yuhan Hoesa claiming treaty benefits or reduced withholding must perform its core income-generating activities in Korea: licensed customs brokerage operations, economic staffing with qualified customs brokers, business premises, risk management, contract negotiation, and board decision-making. A passive non-resident-owned shell with no local substance will be treated as a conduit or sham, and the foreign beneficial owner may be treated as the taxable taxpayer

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Corporate Income Tax Evasion and False Invoice Chain

Statutory sanction: Article 116(1) of the Corporate Tax Act: imprisonment up to two years or a fine equal to twice the evaded tax; Article 66(3) imposes a 40% unjust underreporting surcharge; Article 60 of the Value-Added Tax Act imposes false-tax-invoice penalties and criminal sanctions for entering a fictitious electronic tax invoice into the VAT system

Director personal liability: Under Article 399 of the Korean Commercial Code, a director is liable to the company and creditors for damages caused by fraudulent or grossly negligent management. Tax authorities may pierce the corporate veil and impose joint and several liability on the director for the evaded corporate tax, penalties, and interest. The director is also blacklisted as a serious delinquent taxpayer, subjected to a travel ban, asset seizure, and public disclosure, and may receive imprisonment up to two years
Abuse of Shell Company and Treaty Shopping for Repatriated Profits

Statutory sanction: The National Tax Service will deny treaty benefits under the anti-abuse provisions of the International Tax Adjustment Act, recharacterize the income as Korean-source income of the actual beneficial owner, impose 22% withholding tax plus a 20% additional withholding penalty, and order corrected withholding filings for all prior open tax years

Director personal liability: Directors who approved the treaty-favorable remittance and signed the withholding declarations are jointly and severally liable for the withholding shortfall and penalties. A nominee director who merely stamped the forms is civilly liable for the misrepresentation to the tax authority, faces blacklisting and departure restrictions, and may be prosecuted as an accessory to tax fraud, carrying imprisonment up to two years
AML and Foreign Exchange Violations in Cross-Border Profit Repatriation

Statutory sanction: The penal provisions of the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act impose imprisonment up to three years or a fine up to KRW 200 million; Article 14 of the Financial Information Act requires mandatory suspicious-transaction reporting and failure to report carries administrative fines and criminal referral; trade-based money laundering can result in confiscation of the transferred property and blacklisting by the Korea Financial Intelligence Unit

Director personal liability: Directors who design or approve the undisclosed remittance are personally liable for liquidated damages and repatriation costs, face civil asset seizure, travel bans, and are prohibited from serving as officers of a licensed customs brokerage. In aggravated cases, the director is prosecuted as a principal money launderer rather than a corporate representative, with imprisonment up to three years and personal forfeiture of the laundered proceeds
