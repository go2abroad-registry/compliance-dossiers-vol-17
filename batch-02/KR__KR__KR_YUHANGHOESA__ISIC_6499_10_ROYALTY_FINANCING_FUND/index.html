Registered Agent & Physical Address Licensed Agent Required

A pure virtual office or commercial mailbox, without a dedicated physical suite, lease, and full-time or part-time managerial presence, is commercially and administratively rejected for a revenue-based financing fund in the Republic of Korea. The National Tax Service requires an actual business place for business registration and will refuse a P.O. box or virtual-only address; the courts registry will likewise require a domestic physical seat for Yuhan Hoesa; and Korean banks, operating under the Financial Services Commission's customer due diligence rules, will refuse to open an operating account for a company whose registered office cannot accommodate service of process, record keeping, or on-site inspections. A premium serviced office with dedicated desks, signage, meeting rooms, telephone answering, and a one-year lease may be accepted if the company actually uses it and keeps records there. However, for the financing industry, a serviced office alone is still a yellow flag; the safer strategy is a physical office in Seoul or another major city with at least one resident non-executive manager and a clearly identified compliance officer.

Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

No statutory minimum paid-up capital applies. The Korean Commercial Act amendment abolished the former statutory capital floor, so the Articles of Incorporation may fix the capital amount. Each member must fully pay cash contributions or fully perform in-kind contributions before the registration of incorporation. Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

No turnover threshold for foreign entities with a domestic place of business; any taxable supply of goods or services in Korea triggers mandatory VAT registration. For domestic small businesses, the annual revenue threshold for mandatory registration is KRW 10 million, but this does not apply to foreign entities.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of goods are zero-rated under Article 21 of the VAT Act if the goods are shipped out of Korea and the export is evidenced by customs export declarations. Qualifying offshore digital services (e.g., software, digital content, and royalty income from foreign licensees) are zero-rated if the services are supplied to a non-resident and the consideration is received in foreign currency. The service must be 'used or consumed' outside Korea, and the supplier must retain evidence of the non-resident status of the recipient.

  • Customs Export Declaration (수출신고필증) issued by Korea Customs Service
  • Bill of Lading (B/L) or Air Waybill (AWB)
  • Foreign Exchange Remittance Certificate (FIRC) or bank statement evidencing receipt of foreign currency
  • Commercial Invoice and Packing List
  • Contract or agreement with the foreign buyer/licensee

All VAT-registered businesses must issue electronic tax invoices (전자세금계산서) through the HomeTax system for all B2B transactions. The e-invoice must be transmitted to the NTS in real-time via the HomeTax API or the NTS-approved EDI system. For B2C transactions, cash receipts (현금영수증) must be issued and reported. There is no separate fiscal clearance stamping, but the NTS uses real-time data matching to detect discrepancies. Failure to issue e-invoices or cash receipts results in penalties of 1% of the supply value per violation.

If the NTS determines that the export zero-rating was claimed without proper substantiation (e.g., missing FIRC, false export declarations, or failure to repatriate foreign exchange within 180 days), the VAT becomes immediately payable with interest. Penalties include: (1) a 40% penalty on the unpaid VAT amount for fraudulent claims, (2) a 20% penalty for negligent errors, and (3) additional daily interest of 0.025% on the overdue amount. In severe cases, criminal prosecution may be pursued under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act, leading to imprisonment up to 5 years or fines up to KRW 50 million. The NTS may also conduct a retrospective audit of up to 5 years and claw back any input VAT credits claimed in connection with the zero-rated supply.

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

South Korea maintains a liberalised but strictly supervised foreign exchange regime. The Korean won is fully convertible for current account transactions, including trade in goods, service fees, royalties and distributable profits, under a simple declaration-based reporting system. Capital account transactions, including inbound equity subscriptions, foreign currency borrowings and outbound investments, generally require bank intermediation or prior declaration through the Foreign Exchange Information System, but do not require routine central bank pre-approval unless they fall under special restrictions such as non-resident won borrowing or real estate acquisitions. The surrender of export proceeds is not mandatory in the form of a forced conversion into won, but all export receivables must be repatriated into a domestic foreign exchange bank account within the statutory six-month period from the date of export declaration, and any foreign currency held in Korea must remain in accounts maintained at licensed foreign exchange banks. Profit remittance hurdles are minimal after tax clearance, withholding tax certificates and standard foreign exchange bank verification, but dividend or royalty outflows must be supported by board resolutions, audited financial statements and valid tax residence certificates to avoid bonus withholding or interest deduction disallowance. Non-resident convertible debt or quasi-equity inbound instruments need additional reporting under Article 8 of the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act, and any use of an offshore fintech aggregator to bypass the domestic banking channel is not recognised for statutory capital verification or tariff/tax refund purposes. Overall, the Korean regime is not a surrender-based or approval-heavy system, but it imposes granular documentary discipline, real end-user validation and strict beneficial ownership identification on every cross-border funding and payment leg.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Exporters must collect and repatriate all foreign currency export proceeds to a licensed foreign exchange bank in Korea within six months from the date of export declaration; an extension of up to six additional months may be granted by the bank or the Korea Customs Service in justified trade credit circumstances. There is no fixed percentage of export proceeds that must be compulsorily converted into Korean won, but any conversion, sale or transfer of the foreign currency must be executed through a licensed domestic bank and reported in FEIS with the appropriate trade settlement classification code. If the export claim is partially unpaid or the receivable is written off, the exporter must file a trade settlement delay or non-collection report, otherwise the bank may suspend subsequent settlements and report the matter to the National Tax Service and the Financial Supervisory Service.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Korea (BOK) in conjunction with the Ministry of Economy and Finance (MOEF) and the Foreign Exchange Information System (FEIS) operated under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Offshore digital EMI providers have a Low viability rating for primary treasury operations in South Korea. They are not licensed by the Financial Services Commission to open domestic won-denominated settlement accounts, cannot access the FEIS foreign exchange reporting network, and are not eligible counterparties for mandatory export collection or statutory trade settlement verification. A foreign-owned Korean LLC must maintain a physical account relationship with a domestic Tier-1 bank for all inbound capital subscription wiring, trade settlement, VAT refunds and tax payments. Offshore EMIs may be used only as supplementary payment sensors for non-Korean e-commerce routing, but they cannot serve as the main treasury rail or as the bank of record for corporate verification, and the regulator will treat any attempt to route royalty inflows through an EMI without a domestic FEIS report as a potential violation of the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

All inbound capital injections must be wired by the foreign shareholder using SWIFT MT103 to the Korean LLC's capital escrow account maintained at a Tier-1 domestic bank, with the remitter name matching the equity investor's name stated in the board resolution and shareholder registry. The purpose of payment must reference the Korean Foreign Exchange Transactions Act capital transaction code, typically code 62001 for inbound equity capital or code 62002 for non-resident loan/deposit, and the Korean receiving bank will prepare the FEIS foreign exchange purchase/settlement declaration. For a domestic limited liability company, there is no minimum paid-in capital amount, but the bank will require a capital verification certificate or accountant's confirmation letter if the inbound funds are to be treated as subscribed capital for entity registration and tax purposes. The bank will block or return inflows where the source is a personal account of an unrelated third party or where the originator is a shell entity without a clear economic link to the Korean operating company, and the funds must not mix with capital of other group companies. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: KRW 50 billion in aggregate cross-border related-party transactions per fiscal year for mandatory Local File and Master File; Country-by-Country Report is due where the ultimate parent group's consolidated revenue is at least KRW 2.5 trillion, and the Korean entity receives a CbC notification.

Economic substance scope: Korea does not employ the Gulf-style Economic Substance Regulation, but its substance-over-form doctrine, the OECD BEPS Main Purpose Test, and the Danish beneficial-owner jurisprudence operate identically. A Yuhan Hoesa engaged in royalty financing must evidence Core Income-Generating Activities (CIGA) performed in Korea, namely strategic asset acquisition, credit and market risk decision-making, negotiation of royalty and financing contracts, ongoing portfolio monitoring, treasury and liquidity management, and board-level assumption of risk. If the entity is merely a conduit receiving Korean royalties and passing dividends to foreign investors without staff, premises and meeting record, Korean tax authorities will not only deny treaty benefits but also ignore the legal form under the Abuse of Legal Entity doctrine, imposing corporate tax at the full 22% rate plus local surtax and penalties.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Shell Company / Economic Substance Failure

Statutory sanction: The NTS will disregard the corporate entity under Article 23 of the Framework Act on National Taxes and Article 43 of the Corporate Tax Act. The royalty income is re-characterised as Korean-source dividend actually attributable to a permanent establishment, resulting in a 22% withholding assessment on the full remitted amount, plus a 40% negligence penalty for gross intentional underreporting, plus daily overdue interest at 0.03%, and rejection of the treaty claim under the Principal Purpose Test.

Director personal liability: Creditors and the National Tax Service may pierce the corporate veil under the Korean Commercial Act and Civil Act joint liability rules. Directors, shadow directors, and de facto managers are jointly and severally liable for unpaid corporate income taxes, the 40% negligence penalty, and collection charges. Criminal referral is mandatory when intentional evasion exceeds KRW 300 million; the responsible director faces imprisonment of up to 5 years and a fine of up to 5 times the tax evaded. The Ministry of Economy and Finance places the director on the immigration departure prohibition (blacklist) until the entire tax delinquency is settled.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: The NTS adjusts the transaction under Articles 88 and 89 of the Corporate Tax Act. The excessive deduction is added back to taxable income, re-computation of CIT and local surtax is made, a transfer pricing additional tax of 10% (or 20% for serious non-disclosure) is applied under Article 167-3, and a 0.03% daily overdue interest applies from the original due date.

Director personal liability: The representative director who signs the false return is jointly liable for the adjusted tax, penalties and interest. Where the transfer-pricing scheme is criminal, the director is exposed to imprisonment of up to 5 years and a fine of up to 5 times the avoided tax. In the recovery phase, the NTS attaches the director's personal bank accounts, vehicles, and real estate; unresolved delinquency above KRW 500 million also imposes a travel ban and blacklisting of the director and the company's ultimate beneficial owner.
Withholding Tax Non-Compliance and Unlawful Repatriation

Statutory sanction: The withholding agent is liable for the unwithheld tax and additional tax of 10% of the unwithheld amount (or 20% of the unremitted amount if collected but not paid), plus 0.03% daily overdue interest. The NTS may assess the representative as the withholding taxpayer under the joint liability doctrine. Criminal prosecution for embezzlement or unlawful non-remission results in a fine up to KRW 50 million and imprisonment up to 5 years.

Director personal liability: The managing director and the finance director become personally liable for the unpaid withholding tax and penalties, and the NTS can execute immediately against personal assets. If the amount unpaid is especially high, the directors are registered on the immigration departure restriction (blacklist) and cannot leave Korea without posting a bond or providing a tax clearance certificate. Repeated infringement also triggers disqualification management under the Commercial Act and leads to revocation of visas of foreign directors, with expulsion from Korea.
