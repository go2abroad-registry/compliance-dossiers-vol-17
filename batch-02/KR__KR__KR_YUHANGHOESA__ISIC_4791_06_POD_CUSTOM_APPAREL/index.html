Registered Agent & Physical Address Licensed Agent Required

A virtual office or commercial mailbox is formally permissible for initial registration under the Korean Commercial Act and court registry practice, but it is conditionally rejected by the National Tax Service and corporate banks in the Print-on-Demand online retail industry. The National Tax Service requires a demonstrable place of business for VAT and business registration purposes, and a virtual office may lead to assessment that the registered address is not the actual business site, causing tax notices and invoices to be sent to an address at which the company cannot physically receive them. Banks generally reject corporate current accounts when the sole registered office is a mail-forwarding venue, particularly for e-commerce merchants using online payment rails such as Stripe and PayPal, unless the company produces a fixed-term lease, evidence of local management presence and operational records. Therefore, a virtual office may be used only in the short term and should be upgraded to a serviced office lease before bank account onboarding and tax registration confirmation.

Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

None, no statutory minimum paid-in capital is required for a Yuhan Hoesa under the Commercial Act. However, at least part of each contribution must be paid or delivered to the initial directors before registration, and the amounts received must be deposited in a bank account in the name of the company under formation or a provisional subscription account. The deposit certificate is required by the registry court and the funds become unrestricted company assets after successful incorporation registration. A typical practical working capital for an e-commerce LLC is KRW 10,000,000.

Funding & unlock path: Not Applicable – Civil Defense fire certification under the Fire Prevention, Installation, Maintenance, and Safety Control of Fire-Fighting Systems Act, structural load inspection, and factory workplace safety approval are not required for an office-only e-commerce retail entity with no physical inventory storage. The company must, however, satisfy the general occupational safety duties under the Occupational Safety and Health Act for office workers, including providing safety training and complying with workplace stress and ergonomic standards, and must prepare a written workplace safety plan when it regularly employs five or more workers.

E-government filing portal

For a foreign non-resident entity establishing a Yuhan Hoesa (LLC) in South Korea, the statutory Tax Identification Number (TIN) is the Business Registration Number (사업자등록번호). The procurement procedure is as follows: (1) Prepare the required documents, including the certificate of incorporation, articles of association, a copy of the foreign entity's registration certificate, the passport of the foreign director or legal representative, and a power of attorney if a local agent is appointed. (2) Submit the application to the district tax office (세무서) having jurisdiction over the registered place of business in Korea, or file electronically via the HomeTax portal. (3) The NTS will review the application and issue the Business Registration Certificate, which simultaneously serves as the VAT registration number. (4) For VAT purposes, the entity must also complete a VAT registration application (부가가치세 사업자등록) if it will make taxable supplies; this is typically integrated into the business registration process. (5) The entity must also register for the electronic tax invoice system (전자세금계산서) through HomeTax to comply with mandatory e-invoicing.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

South Korea operates a liberalized current account and capital account regime under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act, yet the system retains procedural friction rather than blanket prohibitions. The Korean won (KRW) is freely convertible for current account transactions such as trade settlements, service fees, and profit remittances, subject to mandatory reporting through designated foreign exchange banks when transaction amounts exceed USD 500,000 or the KRW equivalent of 500 million won for cross-border settlements. Capital account transactions, including foreign direct investment inbound equity injections, foreign currency loans, and overseas direct investments, require prior notification or license from the Bank of Korea or the delegated foreign exchange bank, though approval is generally granted without substantive restriction for compliant structures. Export proceeds must be surrendered or deposited into a foreign exchange bank account within a statutory period, typically within 18 months from the date of export declaration, and exporters are required to convert foreign currency receipts into KRW as part of the settlement process unless a waiver is obtained for operational purposes. Dividend remittances to non-resident shareholders are permitted after corporate tax settlement and are subject to withholding tax of 22 percent (including resident surtax) unless reduced by an applicable income tax treaty, and the remitting bank requires evidence of tax payment, audited financial statements, and board resolution approving the dividend. For a private limited liability company (Yuhan Hoesa) engaged in print-on-demand retailing with transactions routed through Stripe and PayPal, the practical challenge lies not in convertibility refusal but in the banks' insistence on underlying documentary evidence that each remittance corresponds to real service or trade activity, preventing the appearance of disguised loan repayments or capital flight. The overall assessment classifies Korea as an OECD member with high capital mobility, but compliance with reporting thresholds, settlement timelines, and tax clearance certificates remains mandatory; failure to observe these procedures can result in administrative fines and delayed repatriation of funds.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Exporters must file an electronic export declaration with the Korea Customs Service and receive foreign currency proceeds into a designated foreign exchange bank account within 18 months from the export declaration date. The proceeds must be either converted into Korean won or deposited in foreign currency with a licensed bank; if conversion is elected, the bank applies the standard market rate and reports to the Bank of Korea under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act. No mandatory percentage conversion ratio applies, as the regime permits exporters to retain foreign currency in designated accounts, but all foreign exchange transactions must be routed through a licensed foreign exchange bank and accompanied by the exporter's Foreign Exchange Transaction confirmation and underlying trade documents such as commercial invoices, packing lists, and bills of lading.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Korea (BOK) and the Ministry of Economy and Finance (MOEF) via the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act; operational FX oversight is delegated to the Bank of Korea and the Korea Customs Service for trade-related foreign exchange declarations.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Offshore digital EMI platforms such as Wise, Payoneer, and Airwallex have limited legal viability as primary corporate bank accounts for a Korean-resident Yuhan Hoesa. The Foreign Exchange Transactions Act mandates that all cross-border capital flows and trade settlements be channeled through domestic licensed foreign exchange banks or entities registered with the Financial Services Commission (FSC). While these EMIs can be used as downstream payment aggregation tools for collecting customer payments from Stripe and PayPal, they cannot serve as the statutory settlement account for capital account transactions, dividend remittances, or export proceeds surrender. Furthermore, the Financial Supervisory Service (FSS) applies strong anti-money laundering scrutiny to corporations that route significant sales through unregistered foreign payment intermediaries, which may trigger enhanced due diligence and potential reporting to the Korea Financial Intelligence Unit (KoFIU). A hybrid architecture is advised: the Yuhan Hoesa should maintain a primary KRW and USD account at Shinhan Bank or Kookmin Bank for statutory trade settlement, tax payments, and capital verification, while using licensed payment gateways that have local partnerships with Korean acquiring banks for daily e-commerce settlement. Pure offshore EMI concentration is incompatible with the statutory requirement that any transaction exceeding USD 5,000 in foreign exchange must be reported with a purpose code through a licensed bank.

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

9%-21% progressive national Corporate Income Tax on taxable income (KRW 0-200 million: 9%; KRW 200 million-20 billion: 19%; over KRW 20 billion: 21%), plus a 10% Local Income Tax surcharge that yields an aggregate headline top marginal rate of 23.1%

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

22% aggregate absent a tax treaty: 20% national withholding tax under the Corporate Income Tax Act on non-resident dividends plus 2% Local Income Tax surcharge (10% of the 20% national WHT). The effective tax rate on an outbound dividend is therefore 22% of the gross distribution; a tax treaty may reduce the national portion (e.g. 5%, 10%, or 15% depending on the beneficiary's residence and shareholding)
