Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

National Tax Service (NTS), Republic of Korea; online electronic portal: Home Tax Service (HTS) at https://www.hometax.go.kr. Export customs clearance is administered by Korea Customs Service through UNI-PASS.

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Determine if the foreign non-resident entity has a Korean place of business or makes taxable supplies in Korea (including remote e-commerce sales). Step 2: If no domestic establishment, appoint a Korean tax representative as required by Korean national tax law and the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act. Step 3: Submit the Business Registration Application to the competent district tax office (or Seoul Regional Tax Office in certain cases), attaching a certified Korean translation of the foreign registration/certificate of incorporation, articles of incorporation, power of attorney, the representative's proof of identity, and evidence of the domestic place of business. Step 4: File online via the Home Tax Service portal or file in person. Step 5: The NTS will verify the application and issue the statutory 10-digit Business Registration Number, which serves as the TIN and the Korea VAT registration number. For a newly incorporated Yuhan Hoesa (LLC), the domestic company must apply for its own Business Registration Number within 20 days after commencement of business; a foreign parent/investor's home-country tax ID cannot be substituted.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

10%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

No general turnover threshold. VAT registration is mandatory from the commencement of any business that supplies goods or services in Korea. The Simplified Taxpayer turnover thresholds (KRW 30,000,000 for certain services and KRW 48,000,000 for retail/wholesale trade) apply only to individual traders and do not exempt a Yuhan Hoesa LLC from VAT registration.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Domestic supplies in Korea are standard-rated at 10%; export zero-rating applies only to qualifying exports. Goods physically exported from Korea qualify for zero-rated VAT when the export is evidenced by a Korea Customs Service export declaration, the goods are actually shipped to a foreign consignee, title passes to the overseas buyer, and the export proceeds are received in foreign currency. Qualifying digital and offshore services supplied to non-residents are zero-rated when the place of supply is outside Korea (services are used or consumed abroad); the exporter must retain proof of the non-resident status of the buyer, foreign-currency remittance, and offshore usage/consumption evidence. The Korean exporter files zero-rated returns and may claim a refund of any excess input VAT through the Home Tax Service.

  • Customs Export Declaration (export bill of entry) accepted by Korea Customs Service through UNI-PASS
  • Ocean Bill of Lading or Air Waybill naming the foreign consignee and showing the Korean port/airport of export
  • Inward Remittance Certificate issued by a Korean foreign-exchange bank, or the equivalent Foreign Exchange Transactions Act export-proceeds settlement record
  • Commercial invoice and packing list matching the export declaration

Mandatory electronic tax invoice regime administered by the NTS through the Home Tax Service e-invoice platform. General taxpayers must issue electronic tax invoices for business-to-business supplies and transmit the e-invoice data to the NTS within one day after issuance. For sales to end consumers, cash receipts are issued through the approved cash receipt system. There is no private sector fiscal stamping; the NTS performs data matching, cross-audit, and anti-fraud monitoring using the e-invoice database, cash receipt system, and pre-filled VAT returns.

The National Tax Service may demand all export evidence within the statutory assessment period (generally 5 years, extended to 7 years for fraudulent tax evasion). If zero-rating is not substantiated, the export sale is reclassified as a domestic taxable supply, VAT at 10% is assessed, statutory additional taxes and daily interest are applied, and any prior VAT refund is clawed back with interest. Deliberate false documentation or concealment can trigger aggravated additional tax up to 40% of the unpaid or unduly refunded VAT and criminal liability under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Korean won (KRW) is broadly convertible on the current account, and Korea has historically accepted IMF Article VIII obligations. Current-account settlements, including trade goods, services, royalties, and dividend remittances, are generally permitted without prior licensing provided the transaction is supported by underlying documentation and reported through an authorized foreign exchange bank to the Korean FX Information Network (FEI.NET). Capital-account transactions are more administratively controlled. Inbound foreign direct investment into the Yuhan Hoesa must be preceded by a Foreign Investment Report under the Foreign Investment Promotion Act, filed through MOEF, KOTRA, or a delegated foreign exchange bank. The bank then issues a Foreign Investment Report Certificate that underpins capital verification. Profit remittance to a foreign shareholder is lawful but will require board approval, audited financial statements, tax clearance evidence, and proof of distributable reserves; the paying foreign exchange bank will screen for round-tripping and window-dressing. There is no blanket constitutional prohibition on capital repatriation, but the practical hurdles are evidentiary rather than convertibility-based. Korea also retains supervisory restrictions on offshore won settlement and speculative short positions, so multinational treasuries must maintain a lawful FX purpose code for each economic flow. Overall, convertibility is high for genuine business transactions, but purpose coding, reporting, and foreign-investment registration create an unavoidable administrative matrix.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act, export proceeds must be collected through an authorized foreign exchange bank within six months from the date of export declaration. There is no mandatory surrender ratio requiring conversion of foreign currency receipts into KRW; exporters may retain the foreign currency or convert as commercially required. The deadline can be extended by prior approval from MOEF/BOK on justified grounds such as deferred payment arrangements, letter-of-credit discrepancies, or trade disputes. All export proceeds must be reported through FEI.NET and matched to the underlying export declaration to avoid administrative fines.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Korea (BOK) as the central bank, with the Ministry of Economy and Finance (MOEF) acting as the overall Foreign Exchange Control Authority under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act; authorized domestic foreign exchange banks are delegated as reporting and settlement institutions.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Legal viability is LOW for primary treasury or KRW clearing. Offshore digital EMIs such as Wise and Payoneer are not licensed under the Korean Electronic Financial Transactions Act to hold Korean-won balances or operate as a principal settlement bank for a resident Yuhan Hoesa. They may be acceptable only as auxiliary non-KRW collection or payout gateways for cross-border marketplace receipts, provided the company maintains a full domestic banking relationship for statutory Korean won settlements, tax payments, capital verification, and FX hedging. Using a foreign EMI as the sole operational bank would also create an unauthorized foreign-exchange-business concern under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act because the EMI would be effectively intermediating KRW conversion without a domestic foreign-exchange banking license. Therefore, the blueprint requires all primary treasury liquidity, capital injection, and regulatory reporting to remain inside an authorized Korean commercial bank.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

All inbound equity capital for the Yuhan Hoesa must be remitted in the foreign investor's name via SWIFT to a designated authorized foreign exchange bank in Korea. Before the transfer, the foreign investor must file a Foreign Investment Report with MOEF/KOTRA or the delegated bank under the Foreign Investment Promotion Act and obtain the Foreign Investment Report Certificate. The wire instruction must reference that certificate and carry the appropriate foreign-exchange purpose code assigned by the receiving bank for investment in a domestic corporation; trade remittances and shareholder loans must never be commingled in the same MT103. Upon receipt, the foreign exchange bank converts eligible foreign currency to KRW, records the foreign investment, and issues or verifies the capital subscription documentation. The accountant then uses the bank krucial foreign investment verification statement and paid-in capital evidence to complete statutory reporting in the commercial register and audited financial statements.For cross-border supplier payments and trade-related service disbursements, the treasury should use SWIFT MT103 wire transfers with a correct FX purpose code and attached underlying invoice and contract evidence. For bulk inventory imports into the TikTok Shop retail model, a Commercial Letter of Credit issued by a Korean foreign exchange bank provides payment security and documentary compliance. For lower-value, open-account transactions with established suppliers, Documentary Collections (D/P and D/A) are acceptable and offer moderate risk mitigation while preserving liquidity. Domestic KRW supplier and logistics payments should use the Korean financial clearing infrastructure, including KFTC and HOFINET settlement rails, rather than non-bank digital channels. Therefore, the blueprint requires all primary treasury liquidity, capital injection, and regulatory reporting to remain inside an authorized Korean commercial bank.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Under Articles 4-3 and 4-4 of the International Tax Adjustment Act (ITAA) and Presidential Enforcement Decree Article 7: a Korean entity is required to prepare and submit (a) a Local File, (b) a Master File, and (c) a Country-by-Country Report if its total intercompany transaction value with foreign related parties in a fiscal year is KRW 10 billion or more (approximately USD 7.5 million) for the Local File obligation; threshold for Master File is KRW 50 billion; CbC Notification threshold: KRW 100 billion in consolidated group revenue. For foreign investors, a minimal annual intercompany transaction threshold of KRW 50 million applies to simplified documentation ('Summarised Intercompany Transaction Report') under ITAA Article 4-3(4); all cross-border related-party transactions (PSAs, cost-sharing, management fees, IP licence fee transactions, and online platform payout flows) must be disclosed in a separate schedule to the corporate income tax return.

Economic substance scope: The Korean tax authorities apply the Authorised OECD Approach and the substance-over-form doctrine under the Corporate Tax Act Article 4 and the Framework Act on International Taxation. Under the Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) adopted in connection with Korea's compliance with the EU Code of Conduct Group and the OECD BEPS standards, a Korean resident business must prove the real management and control functions – i.e., Core Income-Generating Activities (CIGA) – are performed in Korea through strategic decision-making, employees with technical expertise, offices and physical infrastructure. For the TikTok Shop retail entity, the following CIGA factors are critical: (a) warehouse and fulfilment staff in Korea; (b) local marketing and creator management decisions; (c) risk underwriting functions for consumer returns and refunds; (d) payment reconciliation with TikTok Shop Singapore and local payment gateways; and (e) the principal's inventory ownership and supply chain control. Having a single-member Korean LLC wholly owned by a non-resident with no local employees and all board decisions made abroad may be deemed a shell company, triggering denial of treaty benefits, penalty tax under ITAA Article 7-2 (disallowance of deductions and imputation of income to the non-resident), and administrative sanctions under the Foreign Investment Promotion Act if the entity misrepresents its foreign investment status.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion via Under-Reporting or Sales Suppression

Statutory sanction: Article 9 of the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act (조세범처벌법): imprisonment for up to 5 years and a fine of up to KRW 50 million for unlawful tax evasion; where the amount of evaded tax exceeds KRW 500 million, imprisonment for 2 years or more and a fine of up to 5 times the tax due; where it exceeds KRW 5 billion, imprisonment for up to 10 years and a fine of up to 5 times the tax paid. Administrative penalties: National Tax Basic Act Article 47-3: 40% negligence penalty for intentional underreporting, plus a 0.022% daily late payment interest and unfaithful return penalty of 3% cap. In addition, failure to issue tax invoices under the Value-Added Tax Act Article 49 imposes a penalty of 2% of the unreported supply value.

Director personal liability: Under the Corporate Tax Act Article 109 and the National Tax Basic Act Article 39, the representative director, financial director, and any de facto controlling shareholder of the Yuhan Hoesa are jointly and severally liable for unpaid corporate taxes, penalties, and surcharges where the corporation's assets are insufficient; personal assets, bank accounts, and vehicles can be seized after a demand notice. For criminal evasion, directors who engaged in the concealment or diversion of sales proceeds face criminal imprisonment of 5 years (or 10 years if the evasion exceeds KRW 5 billion), travel bans (passport revocation and immigration blacklisting) are imposed under Article 2 of the Immigration Act and the NTS 'significant taxpayer' regime, and directors may be named in NTS criminal referrals to the Seoul Central District Prosecutors' Office. The veil is pierced when courts find the entity is a dummy/paper company or where the director signed false tax declarations; corporate civil damage claims by minority shareholders for breach of fiduciary duty can also arise under the Commercial Act Article 401.
Shell Company and Substance Failure (BEPS / ESR)

Statutory sanction: Article 4 of the International Tax Adjustment Act (ITAA) and Article 7-2 (adjustment of income between related entities) empower the National Tax Service to impute income to the Korean entity, disallow intercompany charges, and apply a 10% thin-capitalisation penalty; OECD/EU Global Anti-Base Erosion (GloBE) Pillar Two and Local Substance Rules can impose an effective minimum tax of 15%, in addition to the 22% withholding tax not being reduced under a DTA when the main purpose test fails. The Korean tax authorities may also cancel the registration of the 'head office' under the Commercial Act Article 636 and order dissolution if it is a sham. For foreign-owned SPVs, Article 9 of the Foreign Investment Promotion Act imposes sanctions for false foreign-investment registration, and the NTS may deny issuance of a Tax Residency Certificate for the Korean entity.

Director personal liability: In shell-company cases, courts pierce the corporate veil under the Civil Act Article 3 and Commercial Act Article 401, making directors and ultimate beneficial owners jointly liable for unpaid corporate taxes, including imputed income and penalties. Directors could be subject to criminal charges under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act Article 10 (making false declarations) with imprisonment up to 3 years. In addition, entrance/exit restriction (up to 2 years at the request of the National Tax Service) and blacklisting of overseas travel by the Ministry of Justice apply under Article 26 of the Foreign Travel Administration Act and Article 2 of the Immigration Act; the director's overseas assets may be traced under the Act on International Mutual Assistance in Tax Matters, and fund freeze orders are possible under Korea's AML/TF framework. Civil liability to creditors of the dissolved shell company arises under Article 171 of the Commercial Act, allowing creditors to claim against the directors' personal estates.
TP Documentation and Cross-Border Digital Commerce Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Article 4-3(2) of the ITAA: failure to submit TP documentation may result in the tax authority's audit assessment using internally available comparables; a documentary noncompliance penalty of 2% of the value of the implicated intercompany transaction applies, and a maximum of 30% of the tax base may be additionally assessed; Article 4-4(3) CbC Report penalties: up to KRW 20 million per violation, plus daily surcharge of KRW 5,000 per day (maximum KRW 10 million) for failure to submit CbC or Master File. In addition, illegal profit-shifting can be deemed tax evasion and subject to a 40% intentional understatement penalty and criminal sanctions as tax fraud if the amount evaded exceeds KRW 500 million.
