Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

National Tax Service (NTS) of the Republic of Korea; online electronic portal: HomeTax (www.hometax.go.kr). Export-related customs records are filed with the Korea Customs Service through UNI-PASS.

E-government filing portal

A domestic Chusik Hoesa must file a Business Registration Application with the competent district tax office within 20 days of incorporation, submitting the corporate registration certificate and articles of incorporation; the NTS issues a 10-digit Business Registration Number. A foreign non-resident entity that becomes liable for Korean VAT or withholding tax must appoint a Korean domestic tax representative where required, file the same Business Registration Application through HomeTax, and attach constitutional documents, proof of overseas registration, representative authorization, and an overseas address. Registration is completed without minimum capital or a local place-of-business requirement and produces a Business Registration Certificate / TIN for the foreign entity for Korean tax purposes.

No general VAT registration exemption threshold; every business operator must register with the NTS from commencement of business. A simplified taxpayer small-business classification applies only to annual taxable turnover below KRW 80,000,000, but it does not remove the registration obligation.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating is available under Article 11 of the Value-Added Tax Act for exported goods and for services provided to foreign customers, including offshore 3D art and character-rigging services supplied to non-resident game publishers, where the service is performed or economically consumed outside Korea and the consideration is received in a freely convertible foreign currency. A Korean seller must retain the contract, commercial invoice, e-tax invoice or zero-rated invoice, and foreign-currency remittance proof.

  • Korea Customs Service UNI-PASS Electronic Export Declaration / Certificate of Export Declaration
  • Commercial invoice issued by the exporting Chusik Hoesa
  • Packing list
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill (when physical delivery exists)
  • Foreign Exchange Repatriation Certificate issued by a Korean foreign-exchange bank, equivalent to an inward remittance certificate, confirming receipt of export proceeds in convertible foreign currency

All VAT-registered businesses must issue electronic tax invoices for taxable supplies to other businesses and transmit them to the NTS HomeTax system in real time, either directly or through an NTS-approved electronic tax invoice service provider using machine-to-machine submission. There is no separate physical fiscal clearance stamp; the NTS-assigned electronic tax invoice transaction record embedded in HomeTax is the controlling anti-fraud record. Failure or late transmission triggers penalty tax. Export declarations are submitted to the Korea Customs Service via UNI-PASS and matched with VAT zero-rating claims.

The NTS cross-matches HomeTax zero-rated invoices, UNI-PASS export declarations, contracts, and bank foreign-currency receipt records. If a zero-rating claim is unsubstantiated or fraudulent, the supply is reclassified as taxable at 10% VAT, the refunded input VAT is clawed back, and underpaid tax plus interest is assessed. Under the Framework Act on National Taxes, non-reporting or under-reporting penalties reach 10% of the unpaid tax, rising to 40% for intentional false returns; the Value-Added Tax Act also adds a penalty of 2% of the supply value for missing or late e-tax invoice issuance. Criminal prosecution may follow if forged customs declarations or false foreign-currency receipts are used. The general assessment and clawback period is five years, extended to ten years for fraudulent evasion.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Korean won (KRW) is fully convertible on the current account, covering trade in goods, services, royalties, and ordinary business remittances, which may be executed through licensed foreign exchange banks without prior approval provided the transaction is truthfully declared under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act. The capital account, however, remains regulated under a negative-list system: inward foreign direct investment, loans, debt securities, and overseas payments for capital purposes require prior reporting, delayed reporting, or approval depending on the specific nature and amount. For a Chusik Hoesa engaged in 3D game art outsourcing, the principal operational need is to repatriate receivables from overseas clients for sculpting, rigging, and art production services. These receivables qualify as current-account service revenues and are freely receivable in foreign currency, but the beneficiary bank must verify the underlying contract and assign an appropriate trade/service purpose code. Profit remittances to non-resident shareholders are permitted subject to corporate tax clearance, audited financial statements, and a withholding tax certificate; the remittance is classified as a dividend under the capital account and requires either prior declaration or confirmation through the domestic foreign exchange bank. The most significant operational hurdle is not the existence of licensing, but the documentary burden: every incoming foreign currency receipt must be matched to a real underlying transaction and a declaration must be filed within the statutory period, otherwise the bank may treat the receipt as an unclassified capital inflow and suspend disbursement. There is no mandatory conversion of foreign currency into won for service exporters, but once foreign currency is settled into a domestic foreign currency account, the entity may hold it indefinitely; however, any won conversion must be executed through a licensed bank at the market exchange rate. The regime therefore supports a multinational outsourcing treasury if the entity maintains disciplined trade documents, contracts, invoices, and shipping/artifact delivery proofs for every cross-border digital asset delivery.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Under Article 12 and related provisions of the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act and its Enforcement Decree, export proceeds from goods or service transactions, including digital art and software outsourcing deliverables, must be collected within six months from the date of the export permit, customs clearance, or the date of service completion, whichever is applicable. The exporter may apply for an extension of the collection deadline from the Ministry of Economy and Finance or the delegated foreign exchange bank in cases of unavoidable delay, such as long payment cycles in the gaming industry. While there is no statutory requirement to convert export proceeds into KRW, the bank will require that all foreign currency receipts be reported through the Foreign Exchange Information System within 30 days of receipt if the receipt is not directly paired with a trade declaration. For digital service exports without customs clearance, the service provider must maintain a Service Export Declaration (via the Korea Trade Information Service or a licensed bank) and surrender the supporting contract, scope-of-work, and evidence of digital delivery to prove that the incoming wire is a bona fide service remittance. Failure to do so may trigger a retroactive classification as a capital flow, with penalty charges up to 20% of the transaction amount under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Korea (BOK) with supervisory enforcement jointly carried out by the Ministry of Economy and Finance (MOEF) through the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act and the Korea Customs Service for trade-related foreign exchange declarations

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Offshore digital EMI providers, including global multi-currency wallet platforms, are not viable as the primary banking rail for a Korean-domiciled Chusik Hoesa. Under the Korean Foreign Exchange Transactions Act and the Electronic Financial Transactions Act, a foreign EMI must obtain a domestic e-money license or partner with a licensed deposit-taking bank to hold client funds in KRW. The Bank of Korea's pervasive direct participant rule means that overseas EMIs cannot directly access the Korean won clearing system (BOK-Wire+); they can only route KRW through a domestic bank as an agent, which introduces settlement risk and KYC gaps. Moreover, Korean banking regulations under the Real Name Financial Transactions Act mandate verified face-to-face or proxied verification of the entity's controlling parties before an account is opened, and foreign EMIs cannot legally maintain a corporate account for an operating entity without a Korean banking license. A pragmatic compromise is to use a global EMI as a downstream collection vehicle for invoice payments from foreign game studios, but all funds must then be swept into the Korean Tier-1 bank account via SWIFT to satisfy tax, foreign exchange declaration, and capital verification requirements. The treasury blueprint therefore recommends a domestic Tier-1 bank as the sole legal account repository and treats offshore EMIs as ancillary payment collection tools, never as depositories of statutory working capital or as vehicles for settling trade disbursements.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Foreign equity capital for the Chusik Hoesa must be routed through a licensed Korean foreign exchange bank via SWIFT MT103 with the payer's name matching the foreign shareholder in the Foreign Investment Report. The remittance should include a purpose message consistent with the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act: for equity investment, the designated purpose code is 'Acquisition or Increase of Capital by a Foreign Investor' under the Foreign Investment Promotion Act. Before remittance, the entity must obtain a Foreign Investment Registration Certificate from KOTRA or the local branch of a foreign exchange bank by filing the Foreign Investment Report, and the inflowing funds must be deposited into a temporary or capital escrow account in the name of the Chusik Hoesa. Upon receipt, the bank will issue a Certificate of Capital Verification confirming the foreign currency amount; the entity then has 30 days to certify the capital injection for corporate registry and foreign investor status purposes. For operational receivables from overseas game clients, the inbound wires must be coded as service exports under the appropriate current transaction purpose code (e.g., 'Software/IT Services' or 'Business Services'), and the entity must retain the underlying invoice and contract to satisfy the bank's trade declaration desk.For paying overseas subcontractors, freelance 3D artists, engine licensors, or software subscription vendors, the preferred clearing rail is SWIFT MT103 for direct wire transfers under USD 500,000 per transaction, accompanied by an FX declaration for service imports and an approval invoice. For larger purchases, the entity should use a Commercial Letter of Credit (L/C) only when transacting with a manufacturing vendor or a distributor requiring documentary assurance, although for digital art services the L/C is rare and documentary collections (D/P or D/A) are equally uncommon because the service is electronically delivered. The standard operational route is an MT103 via the corporate online banking platform with a mandatory reference to the purpose code 'Payment for Service/Intangible Rights', plus a remittance remark matching the invoice number and currency. The treasury should always centralize outbound payment requests to a single designated approver to avoid breaching the bank's 21-day audit threshold and to keep the foreign exchange reporting exact.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Offshore digital EMI providers, including global multi-currency wallet platforms, are not viable as the primary banking rail for a Korean-domiciled Chusik Hoesa. Under the Korean Foreign Exchange Transactions Act and the Electronic Financial Transactions Act, a foreign EMI must obtain a domestic e-money license or partner with a licensed deposit-taking bank to hold client funds in KRW. The Bank of Korea's pervasive direct participant rule means that overseas EMIs cannot directly access the Korean won clearing system (BOK-Wire+); they can only route KRW through a domestic bank as an agent, which introduces settlement risk and KYC gaps. Moreover, Korean banking regulations under the Real Name Financial Transactions Act mandate verified face-to-face or proxied verification of the entity's controlling parties before an account is opened, and foreign EMIs cannot legally maintain a corporate account for an operating entity without a Korean banking license. A pragmatic compromise is to use a global EMI as a downstream collection vehicle for invoice payments from foreign game studios, but all funds must then be swept into the Korean Tier-1 bank account via SWIFT to satisfy tax, foreign exchange declaration, and capital verification requirements. The treasury blueprint therefore recommends a domestic Tier-1 bank as the sole legal account repository and treats offshore EMIs as ancillary payment collection tools, never as depositories of statutory working capital or as vehicles for settling trade disbursements.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

24% top national corporate income tax bracket on taxable income exceeding KRW 300 billion, plus a 10% local income tax surtax on the national tax, producing a combined top effective rate of 26.4%; lower brackets are 9% up to KRW 200 million, 19% over KRW 200 million up to KRW 20 billion, and 21% over KRW 20 billion up to KRW 300 billion.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

South Korea imposes a 20% national withholding tax on dividends paid to non-resident corporations or individuals, plus a 2% local income tax surtax, creating a combined domestic rate of 22%. A bilateral tax treaty may reduce this to as low as 5% for qualified substantial shareholdings or 10% to 15% for portfolio investment, subject to beneficial ownership and holding-period conditions.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: KRW 50 billion in foreign-related party transactions for the Local File, KRW 100 billion in group revenue for the Master File, and a Country-by-Country Report where consolidated group revenue exceeds KRW 1 trillion; every cross-border related-party transaction must also be disclosed in the International Tax Adjustment Statement, with simplified disclosure for smaller intercompany flows.

Economic substance scope: Although South Korea is not itself an Economic Substance Regulations jurisdiction, Korean tax law applies substance-over-form, beneficial ownership, and conduit-company anti-abuse doctrines when granting treaty benefits, permanent establishment determinations, and foreign tax credits. A Chusik Hoesa must demonstrate real employees, actual management, genuine game-art creation and delivery functions, asset ownership, and risk-bearing capacity in Korea; the absence of such Core Income-Generating Activities will result in denial of DTA relief, transfer pricing adjustment, withholding recharacterization, and penalty exposure. Where the foreign parent is resident in an ESR jurisdiction, the entity must also satisfy the ESR/CIGA test in that jurisdiction.
