Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

National Tax Service (NTS) of the Republic of Korea, acting through the HomeTax electronic tax portal (www.hometax.go.kr); export customs clearance and export declaration functions are handled by the Korea Customs Service (KCS).

E-government filing portal

A foreign non-resident entity that commences VAT-taxable supplies in South Korea must obtain a 10-digit Business Registration Number (TIN/VAT ID) from the district tax office having jurisdiction over its Korean place of business. The application is filed before or within 20 days after commencement of business via the NTS HomeTax portal or in person. Required documents include: (i) the standard business registration application, (ii) certificate of incorporation or equivalent corporate proof from the home jurisdiction, (iii) certificate of foreign company registration or notarized foreign business license, (iv) evidence of a Korean place of business such as a lease agreement or registered domestic office address, (v) foreign investment report certificate under the Foreign Investment Promotion Act if the foreign shareholder meets or exceeds the statutory investment threshold, and (vi) a tax representative appointment letter if the foreign entity has no domestic fixed place of business. The NTS typically issues the Business Registration Number within three business days after review, and the registration certificate is available for download through HomeTax. For a domestic Chusik Hoesa, the same Business Registration Number is issued upon incorporation reporting and commencement of business.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation. The Korean Chusik Hoesa is treated as a domestic corporation under the Corporate Tax Act, taxed on its worldwide income at the corporate entity level; no pass-through regime applies.

Available election forms

None. South Korea does not offer U.S.-style check-the-box or elective pass-through classification for a Chusik Hoesa. Korean tax law contains hybrid mismatch rules that may adjust the tax treatment of cross-border arrangements where another jurisdiction characterizes the entity or instrument inconsistently for tax purposes.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

10%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

No monetary VAT registration threshold. Every person making taxable supplies of goods or services in South Korea is required to register as a taxable business within 20 days of commencement. A small business with annual taxable turnover not exceeding KRW 80 million may be classified as a simplified taxable person under the simplification regime, but registration is still mandatory for any person carrying on a business.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rated export VAT treatment under the Value Added Tax Act applies to goods, including desktop 3D printers, resin filaments, and consumables, when legal title to the goods passes to a foreign buyer and the goods are physically exported from South Korea to a foreign destination under an export declaration accepted by the Korea Customs Service. Zero-rating is substantiated by the actual export, not by mere invoicing to a foreign party. For offshore digital services, zero-rating would apply only where the services are supplied to a foreign non-resident and are actually used or consumed outside South Korea in accordance with the statutory place-of-supply rules.

  • Export Declaration Certificate (Export Customs Clearance Certificate) issued by the Korea Customs Service (KCS)
  • Bill of Lading (ocean export) or Air Waybill (air export) evidencing physical removal of goods from South Korea
  • Commercial invoice
  • Packing list
  • Purchase order or export sales contract
  • Foreign Exchange Settlement Certificate (inward remittance / export proceeds receipt confirmation) issued by a Korean foreign exchange bank evidencing receipt of export proceeds in foreign currency

All VAT tax invoices issued by registered businesses for taxable supplies must be issued as electronic tax invoices through the NTS HomeTax system where the recipient is another business or where electronic issuance is required by law. The electronic tax invoice must be transmitted to HomeTax by the end of the day following the earlier of the invoice issuance date or the payment date. HomeTax automatically cross-checks transmitted invoices against VAT returns, customs export data, and counterparty filings. There is no pre-clearance physical stamping requirement, but each transmitted electronic tax invoice is authenticated with a transmission receipt and digital validation record. Late transmission, non-transmission, or transmission of false electronic tax invoices triggers statutory surcharges generally ranging from 1% to 2% of the supply value and may attract criminal liability under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act for fraudulent invoicing.

The NTS may audit any zero-rated export claim and require production of the export declaration, transport documents, payment records, and foreign exchange settlement evidence. If the export or foreign exchange receipt cannot be substantiated, the supply is recharacterized as a domestic taxable supply and the NTS assesses 10% VAT on the supply value, plus statutory addition taxes and interest for late payment. Fraudulent zero-rating claims, false electronic tax invoices, or fictitious export documentation are subject to criminal prosecution under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act, with penalties including imprisonment and fines, and the statutory reassessment period is extended to 10 years in cases involving false or fraudulent documents, allowing retrospective clawback of improperly claimed refunds.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

South Korea operates a liberal but prudently monitored foreign exchange regime under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act (FETA). The Korean won is fully convertible for bona fide current-account transactions, including trade, services, royalties, dividends and ordinary remittances, which may be executed through authorized foreign exchange banks without case-by-case conversion approval. Capital-account transactions, however, are governed by a negative-list system: foreign direct investment (FDI) into Korea is permitted after filing a prior FDI notification with the Ministry of Economy and Finance via the Foreign Exchange Information System (FEIS), while speculative outbound transfers, non-trade banking loans and offshore KRW settlements are restricted. There is no statutory surrender requirement and no mandatory conversion ratio; an exporter may hold export proceeds in a foreign-currency deposit account or voluntarily convert them into KRW at the prevailing market rate. Profit remittance in the form of dividends is treated as a current-account item if the invested capital was lawfully registered, but banks enforce documentation discipline: audited financial statements, final tax payment certificates, shareholder meeting minutes, a board resolution and a foreign exchange purpose code are required. A mismatch between declared distributable reserves and the remitted amount is the main practical blockade. Therefore, KRW is not fully externally deliverable; all local won flows must settle through a Korean licensed bank.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Export proceeds must be repatriated to a licensed domestic foreign exchange bank within 5 years from the date of export declaration/loading date under the Enforcement Decree of the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act, unless an extension is granted by the Minister of Economy and Finance. The mandatory conversion ratio is 0%; Korean law does not require exporters to sell foreign currency to the Bank of Korea or to convert any specified portion to KRW. The exporter may retain 100% in a foreign-currency deposit account at an authorized bank, but the receipt must be reported through the Foreign Exchange Information System (FEIS) via the bank, and supporting export documents such as commercial invoice, bill of lading, packing list and customs declaration must be retained. Failure to receive within the statutory period, or evidence of unrepatriated proceeds, triggers regulatory attention, reporting obligations and potential penalties.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Korea (BOK), with the Ministry of Economy and Finance (MOEF) exercising foreign exchange policy authority under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act and supervising the Foreign Exchange Information System (FEIS).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Viability: Low. Under Korea's Foreign Exchange Transactions Act, only foreign exchange banks authorized by the MOEF may legally execute foreign-exchange transactions, issue FEIS purpose codes, process inward FDI reporting, and maintain resident Korean-won settlement accounts for domestic corporations. An offshore digital EMI cannot provide a direct local KRW account for the Chusik Hoesa, cannot perform FEIS declaration, and cannot issue a valid foreign-exchange certificate to satisfy capital verification, tax audits or corporate banking due diligence. If e-commerce revenue is settled into a foreign EMI, the export proceeds would be held offshore and outside the statutory Korean reporting perimeter, creating a serious regulatory violation. The correct treasury architecture is to use Stripe/Shopify Payments only as acquiring and payment-gateway front ends, with final KRW settlement wired directly to the Chusik Hoesa's Tier-1 Korean bank account.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

The Chusik Hoesa must first open an FDI-capable foreign-currency deposit account and a KRW operating account at a Tier-1 Korean bank. The foreign investor then initiates a SWIFT MT103 from its own corporate banking account, using the FEIS foreign-exchange purpose code for non-resident direct investment / equity acquisition and the exact legal name of the investor. The receiving Korean bank checks sanctions, denies-payment, CRS/FATCA self-certification and FEIS reporting, then credits the foreign-currency deposit account and issues a foreign-currency deposit certificate or inward remittance notification. The company may convert the funds to KRW at the market rate, but paid-in capital verification requires the foreign remitter to remain identical to the shareholder in the registry. After share subscription and issuance, the company files the capital increase with the Korean courts, updates the commercial registry, and obtains a certified public accountant's paid-in capital verification report. Transactions routed through third-party personal accounts, cash deposits, or unlicensed EMIs cannot be accepted as valid FDI.For routine supplier payments under USD 100,000, use corporate SWIFT MT103 telegraphic transfer with a legitimate trade purpose code and the supporting bundle of invoice, purchase order, packing list, courier/airway bill and customs declaration. For recurring resin/filament bulk purchases, an irrevocable commercial Letter of Credit (sight or usance) is recommended to secure the proforma invoice compliance under defined Incoterms. For stable long-term suppliers, Documentary Collections with Documents against Payment or Documents against Acceptance can reduce bank charges while preserving title control. Every outbound disbursement must align with an underlying Korean customs import declaration and a Korean tax invoice to avoid trade-flow mismatches. Stripe and Shopify Payments settle the e-commerce revenue in KRW through the local acquiring bank; they are not cross-border clearing rails and cannot be used to execute outbound wires or capital repatriation.Deliverable KRW/USD and KRW/EUR forward contracts with a licensed Korean foreign exchange bank, limited to the notional amount of genuine purchase orders, accounts payable or accounts receivable.Cross-currency interest-rate swaps matched to long-term foreign-currency equipment or inventory financing, provided the amortization schedule follows the underlying loan and the swap is documented in FEIS filings.Simple over-the-counter FX options, collars or zero-premium forwards used to hedge anticipated e-commerce receivables or resin import payables; naked option selling and speculative trading are prohibited for non-financial Korean corporations.Onshore non-deliverable forwards (NDFs) are generally reserved for authorized Korean banks or non-resident counterparties with specific real-demand approval; this operating company should therefore use deliverable KRW forwards, because KRW is locally deliverable and the underlying trade documentation is easier to evidence to the bank and tax authorities.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Viability: Low. Under Korea's Foreign Exchange Transactions Act, only foreign exchange banks authorized by the MOEF may legally execute foreign-exchange transactions, issue FEIS purpose codes, process inward FDI reporting, and maintain resident Korean-won settlement accounts for domestic corporations. An offshore digital EMI cannot provide a direct local KRW account for the Chusik Hoesa, cannot perform FEIS declaration, and cannot issue a valid foreign-exchange certificate to satisfy capital verification, tax audits or corporate banking due diligence. If e-commerce revenue is settled into a foreign EMI, the export proceeds would be held offshore and outside the statutory Korean reporting perimeter, creating a serious regulatory violation. The correct treasury architecture is to use Stripe/Shopify Payments only as acquiring and payment-gateway front ends, with final KRW settlement wired directly to the Chusik Hoesa's Tier-1 Korean bank account.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Under the International Tax Adjustment Act, a Local File and related-party transaction schedule are mandatory when the annual value of transactions with any foreign related party exceeds KRW 10 billion; the Master File is required when the multinational group's aggregate worldwide related-party transactions exceed KRW 50 billion; contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation is strongly recommended even below those thresholds because the National Tax Service may request a TP study within 60 days after an on-site inquiry.

Economic substance scope: South Korea does not apply UAE-style Economic Substance Regulations, but it enforces substance-over-form, beneficial ownership, and anti-avoidance doctrines. For the Korean Chusik Hoesa selling desktop 3D printers and resin filaments online, the core income-generating activities (CIGA) such as procurement, inventory ownership, pricing strategy, customer contracting, marketing, logistics risk and after-sales warranty support must be performed by the Korean entity. If those CIGA are deliberately conducted by the non-resident parent to obtain treaty benefits or strip Korean profit, the National Tax Service can recharacterize the arrangement, deny treaty relief, impose transfer pricing adjustments and assert penalties.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Shell Company and Economic Substance Abuse

Statutory sanction: The National Tax Service may disregard the corporate form under substance-over-form and the anti-abuse provisions of the International Tax Adjustment Act; treaty benefits are denied; amounts remitted to the non-resident investor are recharacterized as taxable deemed dividends subject to 22% WHT; and the Framework Act on National Taxes imposes negligence penalties of up to 40% of the understated tax plus daily overdue interest.

Director personal liability: Directors and the representative director who knowingly operate the entity as a shell face piercing of the corporate veil; they are personally liable for unpaid corporate taxes and withheld taxes, may be subjected to civil asset restitution and attachment of personal bank accounts and real estate, placed on the National Tax Service bad-faith taxpayer blacklist, subjected to travel bans/departure restrictions, and criminally prosecuted under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act with imprisonment up to 10 years and fines equal to twice the evaded tax.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation and Royalty Stripping

Statutory sanction: The National Tax Service exercises its transfer pricing adjustment power under Article 5 of the International Tax Adjustment Act, recomputing taxable income using the most appropriate arm's-length method, disallowing overstated cost of goods sold and IP royalties, and imposing a 20% TP negligence penalty on the underreporting; if the act is fraudulent, the penalty rises to 40% and daily overdue interest accrues on the deficiency for the entire open statute period.

Director personal liability: The representative director and financial officers who sign false transfer pricing disclosures and corporate tax returns are exposed as co-offenders in tax evasion; they can be held jointly and severally liable for the additional corporate tax, WHT and penalties; personal assets may be seized by the NTS, directors can be blacklisted as serious tax delinquents, overseas travel may be prohibited, and criminal imprisonment of up to 10 years plus monetary fines of twice the evaded tax can be imposed.
Willful Tax Evasion and Failure to Withhold on Non-Resident Dividends

Statutory sanction: Under the Framework Act on National Taxes, the non-filing/underreporting penalty for willful malpractice is 40% of the unpaid tax, with daily overdue interest on all late amounts; under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act, the entity and its responsible officers face criminal fines of up to roughly twice the evaded tax and imprisonment ranging from 2 years to 10 years where the evaded tax exceeds KRW 500 million; the NTS may seize corporate assets, revoke tax clearances, and publicly list the entity as a willful tax offender.

Director personal liability: The representative director and the officer in charge of tax affairs are personally liable for the uncollected withholding taxes because they are statutory withholding agents; the corporate veil can be pierced where funds are diverted or concealed, allowing creditors and the NTS to seek civil restitution from the directors' personal property; directors will face asset attachment, blacklisting and travel bans, and, upon a criminal conviction, actual prison confinement including terms of up to 10 years in aggravated tax fraud cases.
