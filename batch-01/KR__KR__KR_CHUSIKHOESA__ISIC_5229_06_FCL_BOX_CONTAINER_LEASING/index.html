Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

None under the Korean Commercial Act. A Chusik Hoesa may be incorporated without any statutory minimum paid-in capital, because the minimum capital requirement was abolished in 2012. However, the entire initial share subscription price must be actually paid and deposited into a blocked capital subscription escrow account at a commercial bank in the Republic of Korea, and the bank certificate of paid-in capital deposit must be submitted with the incorporation registration. If the subscription is paid with a contribution in kind, an appraiser appointed by the court must verify the contribution under Article 299. For a notional registered capital of KRW 100,000,000, the court registration tax, local education surtax, and judicial registration fee are approximately KRW 520,000, equal to about USD 390; notarial fees are additional.

Funding & unlock path: Fire Safety and Civil Defense Clearance: prior to startup of any physical depot, office, warehouse, charging room or paint booth, the operator must obtain a Fire System Completion Inspection Certificate and a Civil Defense and Fire Safety Permit from the local fire station under the Fire Services Act. The fire authority will verify access roads, fire lanes, hydrants, fire extinguishers, emergency lighting, evacuation routes, storage spacing and the compatibility of container stacking materials with fire safety regulations. Occupational clearance: under the Occupational Safety and Health Act, file a Workplace Safety and Health Initiation Report with the Korea Occupational Safety and Health Agency (KOSHA), obtain a structural load inspection for any container stacking racks and cranes, implement lockout/tagout and fall protection protocols, train workers in hazardous chemical handling, and secure a local workplace safety approval certificate before deploying staff. For port-located depots, the local harbor authority must also approve the operational layout and emergency evacuation plans.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

South Korea maintains a liberalised current account but a selectively controlled capital account. Under FETA, current-account settlements—trade, services, cross-border leasing income, and ordinary operational expenses—can be completed through authorised foreign exchange banks without central bank licensing. A Chusik Hoesa receiving container lease payments from non-resident lessees may credit either a KRW or a foreign currency account, with supporting leases, invoices, and cargo movement evidence retained for bank reporting. Profit remittance is a capital-account event: dividends require board approval, audited financial statements, tax clearance, and FEIS purpose-code filing, with a 20 percent withholding tax reduced by an applicable treaty. The KRW is non-deliverable offshore in the interbank market, so a Korean resident undertaking a hedge must settle in KRW with a licensed onshore foreign exchange bank. Capital and financial account items such as shareholder loans, acquisitions of domestic securities, or repatriation of principal require prior declaration and often a certificate of foreign investment. Thus, current account convertibility is high; capital account convertibility is conditional, transaction-level, and reliant on domestic bank intermediation.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No mandatory surrender ratio applies in South Korea. A Chusik Hoesa may retain 100 percent of foreign currency proceeds from cross-border container leases in a designated foreign currency account. The statutory settlement timeline is the contractual due date shown in the lease invoice or equivalent schedule; for goods export sales, the FEIS platform requires the foreign exchange bank to confirm receipt within the approved trade credit period. Overdue balances must be reported to the processing FX bank and the counterparty filing must be completed before the sanction of an extension. Any conversion of foreign currency to KRW must be executed through a bank licensed as a foreign exchange bank and cannot be executed through an offshore EMI.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Korea (BOK), with the Foreign Exchange Administration under the Ministry of Economy and Finance (MOEF) as the policy-controlling authority; licensed foreign exchange banks execute the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act (FETA) and report transaction-level data through the Foreign Exchange Information System (FEIS).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low; offshore EMIs are not legally licensed as banks in South Korea and cannot maintain local KRW accounts, participate in KFTC clearing, or accept deposits under the Banking Act and the Electronic Financial Transactions Act. They may route cross-border payments through partner banks but cannot serve as the qualifying treasury account holder or fulfil capital-account reporting, capital verification, or FEIS purpose-code requirements. Use them only as an ancillary card or payment-initiation channel; all primary clearing, FX execution, escrow, and capital-verification workflows must remain with a Tier-1 Korean bank.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Before any remittance, the Chusik Hoesa or the foreign shareholder must file a foreign investment report under the Foreign Investment Promotion Act, or a FETA capital transaction declaration, with the designated foreign exchange bank. The remittance is wired from the shareholder's account to a temporary Foreign Investor Capital Escrow Account at a Tier-1 Korean bank. The bank will verify the remitter identity and the FEIS purpose-code; after inward receipt, the bank issues the foreign investment registration certificate and converts the proceeds into KRW if required. Funds are then transferred to the company's operating account only after registration of the share issuance and deposit into the paid-in capital account. No operating payments may be made directly from the escrow account; each step requires the board resolution, share subscription agreement, and registry filing.For container procurement and trade settlement, use SWIFT MT103 for regular cross-border payments, commercial letters of credit issued by a Tier-1 Korean bank for large fleet purchases, and documentary collections when title-transfer documents are released against payment or acceptance. Domestic KRW disbursements use the Korean CD/EFT automated settlement system through the operating account. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

9%, 19%, 21%, 24% (progressive brackets on the corporate tax base) plus 10% local corporate income surtax, producing an aggregate headline top statutory rate of 26.4%

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

22% aggregate for outbound dividends paid by the Korean Chusik Hoesa to a non-resident corporate shareholder, comprised of 20% national withholding tax plus 2% local surtax (10% of the national withholding). If no tax treaty applies, this 22% burden is final for the shareholder and no foreign tax credit is available in Korea.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: The annual international related-party transaction report is mandatory for cross-border transactions exceeding KRW 100 million; a Local File is mandatory where the annual foreign related-party transaction amount equals or exceeds KRW 10 billion; a Master File is mandatory where the annual foreign related-party transaction amount equals or exceeds KRW 50 billion. International container leasing and intercompany management fees must be accumulated on a consolidated entity basis for these thresholds, including transactions with the ultimate parent, sister affiliates, and any special-purpose vehicle in the lease chain.

Economic substance scope: South Korea does not apply UAE-style Economic Substance Regulations by that name to a Chusik Hoesa, but the Korean tax authorities apply an equivalent substance-over-form and general anti-avoidance doctrine under the Framework Act on National Taxes and the Law for Coordination of International Tax Affairs. For an ISO shipping container leasing entity, the Korean company must demonstrate that its Core Income-Generating Activities (CIGA) are actually performed in Korea: real employees negotiating and managing container leases, active maintenance and re-positioning decisions, physical container utilization control, treasury and risk-management functions, and accounting records located in Korea. If those activities are performed from a foreign low-tax jurisdiction and the Korean entity is only an invoicing or title-holding vehicle, the National Tax Service will deny treaty benefits, recharacterize the intercompany lease, assess Korean tax on the economic substance, and apply fraud penalties.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Wilfull Non-Declaration of Korean-Source Container Lease Income

Statutory sanction: Under Article 3 of the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act, the responsible director or representative is subject to imprisonment of up to five years and a fine not exceeding five times the evaded corporate tax; the unpaid tax is collected with the statutory unfaithful return penalty of up to 20% under the Framework Act on National Taxes, plus daily late-payment interest. The National Tax Service can redetermine the taxable income for all open years, deny tax treaty benefits, and issue aggravated penalties for fraudulent omission.

Director personal liability: Korean courts may pierce the corporate veil where the company is an undercapitalized alter ego or where the corporate form is a sham used for wrongful tax purpose. The director who signed the false return is personally liable under Commercial Act Article 401 for damages caused to creditors and the tax authority by intentional or grossly negligent conduct. The director can also be placed on the public delinquent taxpayer blacklist if unpaid taxes exceed the statutory threshold, and the National Tax Service may issue a departure prohibition (travel ban) to prevent flight; a criminal conviction results in imprisonment and restitution of the evaded tax plus penalties.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance and Fabricated Intercompany Documentation

Statutory sanction: Under the Law for Coordination of International Tax Affairs Article 4, the National Tax Service can adjust the intercompany price to arm's length and assess corporate tax on the revised Korean taxable income. Statutory sanctions include a transfer pricing penalty of up to 10%-20% of the adjusted tax base, the unfaithful return additional tax, underpayment interest, and denial of deductions for undocumented intercompany services. If the fabrication is criminal fraud, the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act applies with imprisonment up to five years and a fine up to five times the evaded tax.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly sign false transfer pricing records are treated as having violated the duty of care and loyalty under the Commercial Act. They are jointly and severally liable for the additional corporate tax, local surtax, transfer pricing penalties, and legal enforcement costs. The same directors may be charged with obstruction of tax collection or tax evasion, and the National Tax Service can block international departure and publicly blacklist the taxpayer. Civil asset restitution and forfeiture of funds moved through the artificial lease chain are available remedies.
Shell Company Abuse / Lack of Economic Substance / Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: Under the Framework Act on National Taxes and the Law for Coordination of International Tax Affairs, the National Tax Service may apply the general anti-abuse rule and deny the DTA reduced withholding rate, recharacterize the transaction as taxable in Korea, and impose the full 22% dividend withholding or 27.5% interest withholding. Penalties include an additional tax of up to 30% of the underpaid tax for dishonest documentary abuse, the unfaithful filing penalty, and the complete disallowance of treaty claims. The entity may also be subject to deregistration or strike-off from the commercial registry if it is found to lack genuine business activity.

Director personal liability: A court can pierce the corporate veil and impose personal liability on directors where the company is a pure shell and the corporate form is used to circumvent Korean corporate tax. Directors are liable for the full unpaid corporate tax, surtax, interest, and penalties; they may be ordered to personally restore diverted lease profits to the Korean tax authority. The director's participation in treaty shopping is treated as gross negligence or fraud, leading to criminal prosecution, imprisonment up to five years, confiscation of personal assets, travel restriction or departure ban, and inclusion on the National Tax Service delinquent taxpayer blacklist.
