Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

No statutory minimum paid-up capital is prescribed under the Commercial Act, and the object business may be a virtual event platform. Nevertheless, the promoters must fully subscribe and pay the entire issue price for all issued shares before registration. Subscription funds must be deposited into a blocked escrow account opened at a domestic Korean bank in the name of the company-to-be; the bank must issue a certificate of deposit evidencing the subscription payment, and the funds cannot be withdrawn or released to the operational account until the court has completed the incorporation registration. Non-resident promoters must record inbound capital remittances under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act and, where required, file a foreign investment report with KOTRA or the designated Foreign Exchange Bank.

Funding & unlock path: Not Applicable for the digital business itself; no Civil Defense fire permit is required for hosting virtual concerts. For goods: export of goods is zero-rated if the goods are physically removed from Korea and the export declaration is filed. Mandatory electronic tax invoice (e-Tax Invoice) issuance for all B2B transactions via the HomeTaxi system. Invoices must be submitted within 7 days of the supply. Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

South Korea maintains a fundamentally open current account for goods, services, and dividend remittances, but retains a regulatory gatekeeping regime for cross-border capital flows under the FTA. The Korean won is convertible onshore for bona fide trade and corporate treasury purposes; however, the currency is not freely deliverable offshore, and non-resident counterparties in the metaverse and digital-content sector face reduced access to NDF markets unless they transact through authorized domestic banks. Capital account inflows, including equity injections into a Chusik Hoesa, require advance or post-hoc reporting through the Foreign Exchange Information System, and each remittance must be tagged with a standard purpose code. Profit remittance and dividend outflows are permitted when supported by audited financial statements and a certificate of foreign currency deposit receipt, but banks will scrutinize the underlying source of profits, especially in digital-asset-adjacent businesses. A virtual concert business generating USDC-denominated revenue faces heightened classification risk: the Korean authorities generally treat virtual assets as neither fiat nor explicit current-account receipts, so routing through a regulated domestic bank account and converting to KRW via a licensed virtual asset service provider (VASP) is mandatory. Overnight open positions in foreign currency are allowed for resident entities but must be reconciled against the original declared trade purpose, and any alteration in the settlement currency or timing requires a prior re-declaration at the foreign exchange bank.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Regulations, exporters of services (including digital performance ticketing) must receive foreign-currency proceeds within one year from the date of the service export declaration. There is no statutory mandatory conversion ratio into KRW, but the full amount must remain in a domestic foreign-currency deposit account with an authorized foreign exchange bank, and the bank must file a monthly FEIS report identifying the receipt and its purpose code. If the invoice is settled in USDC or another virtual asset, the proceeds must first be converted to fiat US dollar or KRW through a licensed VASP and then reported as an export receipt, or the transaction will be reclassified as a virtual-asset transfer subject to independent AML review.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Korea (BOK) with the Ministry of Economy and Finance (MOEF) as the designated Foreign Exchange Administration authority, operating through the Foreign Exchange Information System (FEIS) under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act (FTA) and subordinate regulations issued by the Financial Services Commission (FSC) for anti-money laundering enforcement.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

National headline corporate tax rate of 9% to 24% on taxable income brackets for fiscal years beginning on or after 1 January 2023: 9% up to KRW 200 million; 19% over KRW 200 million up to KRW 20 billion; 21% over KRW 20 billion up to KRW 300 billion; and 24% over KRW 300 billion. In addition, a 10% Local Income Tax surtax applies on the national corporate tax, producing aggregate effective headline rates of approximately 9.9% to 26.4%.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

For dividends paid by a Korean Chusik Hoesa to a non-resident shareholder, the statutory withholding rate is 20% national corporate tax/income tax plus 2% Local Income Tax surtax, making an aggregate statutory dividend withholding tax of 22%, unless reduced by an applicable bilateral Double Tax Agreement. Treaty rates often range from 5% to 15%, subject to shareholding and beneficial ownership conditions.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Korean transfer pricing law requires preparation of a Local File when the amount of cross-border related-party transactions with foreign related parties during the fiscal year reaches or exceeds KRW 10 billion, with a lowered threshold of KRW 5 billion for transactions with tax-haven jurisdictions. A Master File and Country-by-Country Report are required for multinational groups with consolidated global revenue of KRW 100 billion or more. The NTS may also request transfer pricing documentation for transactions as low as KRW 500 million when gross margins or comparability screening indicate a potential mispricing.

Economic substance scope: Korea does not apply Gulf-cooperation-council style Economic Substance Regulations under that exact name, but economic substance is an operative legal doctrine through Korean GAAR, abuse-of-rights principles, controlled foreign company rules, and OECD BEPS-aligned CIGA analysis. The Core Income-Generating Activities of a virtual concert metaverse business are Korean product development, local user acquisition, ticket tokenization and access control, metaverse event operations, content moderation, payment settlement risk, and customer support. If these functions are performed by the Korean entity, the resulting profit must be booked in Korea. A mere set-up in Korea with nominal staff and a separate offshore IP shell that receives most of the economic profit will be disregarded, and the payment may be denied or recharacterised under the economic substance doctrine.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion through Unreported Domestic Revenue and Cryptocurrency Settlements

Statutory sanction: This constitutes tax evasion under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act Article 3. The offender is subject to imprisonment with labor of up to 5 years or a fine not exceeding five times the amount of tax evaded. The NTS additionally imposes the unpaid corporate tax, VAT, and Local Income Tax, along with 10% to 20% negligent return penalties, a 0.022% daily overdue surcharge, and 10% to 40% additional penalties for unjustified underreporting. If the unpaid tax exceeds substantial thresholds, the taxpayer is publicly disclosed as a major tax evader.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly approved a false crypto receipt protocol or who knowingly withheld information from the tax preparer can be personally assessed and held jointly and severally liable with the corporation for the unpaid tax under the joint collection rules. Through piercing the corporate veil, the NTS may attach the director's personal bank accounts, vehicles, and real estate. Criminal prosecution may result in imprisonment; a departure restriction and travel ban are also imposed under the National Tax Collection Act while the tax is unpaid.
Transfer Pricing Mispricing and Offshore Royalty Stripping

Statutory sanction: Under Articles 4 to 6 of the International Tax Coordination Act, the NTS may re-determine the arm's-length consideration and compute tax based on the adjusted income. The deduction for the overstated royalty is denied, and the taxpayer is liable for additional corporate tax plus Local Income Tax, interest, and an underreporting penalty of up to 30%. If the offshore company is classified as a controlled foreign company, its undistributed income may be included in the Korean controlling shareholder's taxable income.

Director personal liability: Directors who signed the intercompany license, directed the royalty payments, or prepared false transfer pricing documentation are exposed to civil joint liability for the additional tax and penalties. The corporate veil may be pierced when the Korean entity is found to be a pure shell and the real management is conducted by the directors offshore. Criminal liability arises if the transfer pricing transaction is accompanied by false invoices, false accounts, or intentional concealment, resulting in imprisonment of up to five years and a fine of up to five times the evaded tax. The responsible director may also be blacklisted and placed on the departure-restricted persons list.
Beneficial Ownership Treaty Abuse and False DTA Relief Declaration

Statutory sanction: Treaty benefits are denied under the Korean International Tax Coordination Act and the Framework Act on Taxation general anti-abuse rule. The payer becomes liable for the additional withholding tax, interest, and 40% dishonest underreporting penalty. False tax documents submitted to a tax authority constitute fraud under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act, and the taxpayer may also be punished for document falsification.

Director personal liability: The director of the Korean corporate payer is jointly and severally liable for unpaid withholding tax and penalties because the director caused the company to make the payment without lawful withholding. The court can pierce the corporate veil and hold the director personally liable for restitution of the tax, interest, and penalties. Criminal sentencing may include imprisonment without labor of up to three years or a fine for document falsification, and imprisonment of up to five years for evasion. The NTS will impose a travel ban to prevent departure until all tax liabilities are settled.
