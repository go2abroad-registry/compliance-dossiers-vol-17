Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

No statutory minimum paid-up capital is required for a Chusik Hoesa under current Korean law, and no blocked escrow account is statutorily mandated. However, the total par value of all issued shares must be fully paid in cash before incorporation registration is filed, and a bank-issued capital deposit certificate evidencing such payment must be submitted to the District Court Registry. The funds may not be withdrawn for business use until registration is complete. The official filing fee includes a court registration fee of approximately KRW 40,000 plus registration tax at 0.4 percent of stated capital and a local education surcharge equal to 20 percent of the registration tax.

Funding & unlock path: Not Applicable to a factory-level Civil Defense fire permit, structural load inspection, or special industrial workplace safety approval because no manufacturing, chemical, assembly, or warehousing facility is established. General workplace duties under the Korean Occupational Safety and Health Act remain applicable to the office environment, and the building owner must maintain fire extinguishers, emergency exits, and civil defense fire safety systems. The company should maintain basic fire extinguishers, employee safety training records, and an emergency evacuation plan for its office employees.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

National Tax Service (NTS) of the Republic of Korea, operating through the Hometax electronic tax portal at www.hometax.go.kr; cross-border trade and customs processing are handled by the Korea Customs Service (KCS) through the UNI-PASS electronic customs portal at unipass.customs.go.kr.

E-government filing portal

For a resident Chusik Hoesa, the company must commence business registration within 20 days of business opening by filing an application with the competent district tax office through Hometax. The statutory tax identifier is the 10-digit Korean Business Registration Number (BRN) issued under Article 8 of the Value-Added Tax Act and the Framework Act on National Taxes. The required documentary bundle includes the certificate of incorporation, certified corporate articles, lease or ownership evidence for the registered business premises, nominee or representative director identification, and where applicable the foreign investment registration certificate under the Foreign Investment Promotion Act. Upon NTS acceptance, Hometax issues the BRN, which serves as the company's unified tax account for corporate income tax, VAT, withholding, and customs-adjacent tax filings. For a foreign non-resident entity without a Korean domestic place of business, the entity must appoint a Korean tax representative, register through Hometax using an accredited public-joint certificate, and submit the foreign certificate of incorporation, a foreign business license or equivalent registration extract, a board resolution appointing the tax representative, a power of attorney, and a declaration confirming the absence of a Korean domestic business place. NTS will then issue a foreign corporation BRN or equivalent Korean taxpayer identifier enabling VAT return filing, electronic tax invoice issuance, and payment of Korean VAT where the foreign entity supplies electronic services or other taxable Korean-source services. The entire registration flow is completed electronically; no physical visit is generally required.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

None for entity classification. The Republic of Korea does not provide a check-the-box or hybrid entity election under its domestic tax law; a Chusik Hoesa is per se a corporate income taxpayer taxed at the entity level on worldwide income. The only relevant elective regimes are group consolation and special tax incentive elections, none of which alter the entity classification or VAT registration framework.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

10%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

No economic turnover threshold for mandatory registration: any business making taxable supplies of goods or services in Korea, including a Chusik Hoesa operating an email newsletter hosting platform, must register for VAT from its first taxable supply. A simplified taxpayer regime exists for small suppliers with annual taxable turnover below KRW 80,000,000, but such taxpayers remain legally required to hold a Business Registration Number and issue VAT invoices.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of goods are zero-rated under Article 21 of the Value-Added Tax Act when the goods are exported and supported by customs export documentation. The supply of services to foreign customers is also zero-rated when the recipient is a non-resident and the service is deemed supplied or consumed outside Korea under the place-of-supply rules. For the email infrastructure SaaS scope, bulk transactional email, newsletter hosting, API delivery, and related digital platform services supplied to foreign subscribers or foreign businesses without a Korean place of business and with actual use outside Korea meet the statutory offshore service criterion. The supplier may claim full input VAT credit on costs directly attributable to zero-rated export activities and may file a VAT refund when input VAT exceeds output VAT; the refund is subject to NTS documentation review.

  • Korean Customs Export Declaration filed through the UNI-PASS system for physical goods movements
  • Commercial invoice and packing list
  • Bill of Lading or airway bill evidencing physical export to a foreign destination
  • For pure digital services: signed service agreement with the foreign customer, monthly usage and performance records generated by the email infrastructure platform, foreign-currency service invoice addressed to the non-resident customer, and bank records showing receipt of the export consideration
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (FIRC) or equivalent bank-issued foreign exchange receipt confirming receipt from overseas in KRW or foreign currency

Korea mandates electronic tax invoices for business-to-business taxable supplies. Every registered business must issue a Korean tax invoice through the NTS Hometax Electronic Tax Invoice system, either directly through Hometax or via an NTS-certified electronic tax invoice service provider. Invoice data must be transmitted to NTS in the form and within the timeline prescribed by the NTS Commissioner. NTS does not pre-approve or digitally stamp individual invoices before issuance; anti-fraud integrity is achieved through mandatory transmission of e-invoice data into the Hometax database, real-time cross-matching against filed VAT returns and buyer credit claims, risk-based audit analytics, and statutory penalty surcharges for false or omitted invoice data.

NTS and Korea Customs Service may jointly review zero-rating evidence. If the claimed export is not substantiated by customs clearance, foreign consumption evidence, or foreign exchange repatriation within the statutory 180-day deadline, the 0% rate is denied retroactively, the VAT becomes payable with statutory interest, and the related input VAT credits are clawed back. Additional penalty surcharges are imposed under the Value-Added Tax Act and Framework Act on National Taxes, including penalties for false tax invoices, unreported or underreported VAT, failure to transmit e-invoice data, and fraudulent refund claims. Fraudulent or wilful zero-rating claims may constitute criminal tax evasion under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act and can result in imprisonment, fines, publication of taxpayer information, and extended statute-of-limitations assessment periods.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

South Korea maintains a liberalised but supervised foreign exchange regime. Current account transactions are generally free: legitimate cross-border payments for trade, software subscriptions, cloud services and newsletter infrastructure fees can be settled in foreign currency or KRW through a designated Foreign Exchange Bank (FX Bank). The old fixed export surrender ratio has been replaced by a monitoring-based system; proceeds must be received through an FX Bank and reported, but the bank may hold them in a retained foreign currency account or convert to KRW at market rates. Capital account items are subject to a positive-list declaration regime: non-resident equity subscription, inter-company financing and profit remittance require advance filing under the Foreign Investment Promotion Act and the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act. Dividend repatriation from a Chusik Hoesa is permitted after capital verification, audited financial statements and withholding tax payment, but the bank will require the final shareholders resolution and confirmation of no unresolved tax liabilities. A foreign investor must complete a foreign investment report before inward remittance; the KRW conversion is then recorded and monitored. Service revenue from email newsletter platform operations is transactionally convertible, but any suspicious or unsubstantiated flow will be blocked by bank compliance committees under the Act on Reporting and Using Specified Financial Transaction Information. Overall, the KRW is largely convertible on current account and selectively convertible on capital account, with procedural rather than absolute restrictions.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No mandatory conversion ratio applies; under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act, export and service proceeds must be received through a domestic FX bank within one year of the transaction unless extended. The exporter or service provider reports the withdrawal and can either keep foreign currency in an FX account or sell it to the bank. Non-repatriation within the statutory timeline requires prior approval and triggers a report to the Bank of Korea.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Korea (BOK) with the Ministry of Economy and Finance, operating under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act and Foreign Exchange Regulations.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

24% national top CIT bracket plus 10% local income tax surtax (aggregate top rate 26.4%); progressive lower brackets apply up to KRW 200m (10%), KRW 20bn (20%), and KRW 300bn (22%).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

22% aggregate statutory dividend withholding tax for non-resident shareholders (20% national withholding under Article 98 of the Corporate Income Tax Act plus 2 percentage points from the 10% local income tax surcharge). Reduced treaty rates, generally 10% for qualifying substantial shareholders and 15% for portfolio shareholders under major Korean DTAs, apply only after treaty relief is approved.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Local File is required when cross-border related-party transactions exceed KRW 10 billion in the fiscal year; Master File is required when annual turnover exceeds KRW 100 billion; Country-by-Country Report is required for consolidated groups with total revenue of KRW 5 trillion or more.

Economic substance scope: Korea does not operate a UAE-style Economic Substance Regulation, but NTS applies the economic substance doctrine and substance-over-form principles; a Korean Chusik Hoesa must perform the Core Income-Generating Activities (CIGA) with local employees, active management, office/network infrastructure, and strategic decision-making. Absent substance, profits may be recharacterized as distributions to non-residents, and the Korean CFC rules can attribute offshore passive income to controlling Korean shareholder-directors.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Return

Statutory sanction: Under Article 3 of the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act, willful tax evasion is punishable by imprisonment up to three years or a fine up to three times the evaded tax; NTS also imposes a 20% fraudulent under-reporting surcharge and can suspend tax certificates and VAT e-invoice issuance.

Director personal liability: Korean courts may pierce the corporate veil when the Chusik Hoesa is used as a facade; the representative director becomes jointly and severally liable for unpaid CIT, personal assets are subject to seizure, travel bans are imposed through the National Tax Collection Act, and directors who signed false returns face criminal imprisonment up to three years, or five years in aggravated cases.
Withholding Tax Non-Compliance and Unlawful Profit Remittance

Statutory sanction: The withholding agent is personally liable for the unwithheld tax, plus a withholding non-reporting surcharge of up to 20% and daily late-payment interest at 0.022% per day; deliberate non-withholding also constitutes a criminal tax collection offense.

Director personal liability: Directors and the compliance officer are personally and jointly liable for unremitted withholding tax, deemed dividend taxes, and penalties; NTS can blacklist directors, order travel restrictions, seize personal and corporate accounts, and refer intentional false treaty claims for criminal prosecution with imprisonment up to three years.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation and Shell Substance

Statutory sanction: NTS re-determines taxable income on an arm's length basis under Article 4 of the Adjustment of International Taxes Act, disallows non-arm's length payments, imposes an under-reporting penalty of 10% (20% when concealed) with daily delinquency interest, and applies statutory TP documentation penalties for failure to attach the required files.

Director personal liability: Directors may be assessed as secondary taxpayers under the Basic National Tax Act if corporate assets are insufficient; intentional TP manipulation exposes directors to personal civil restitution, travel bans and blacklisting, and criminal imprisonment up to three years for submitting false evidence during an NTS transfer-pricing audit.
