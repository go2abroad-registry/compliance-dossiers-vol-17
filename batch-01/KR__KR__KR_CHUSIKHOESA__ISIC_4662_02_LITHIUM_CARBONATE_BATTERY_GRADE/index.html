Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital is imposed by the Commercial Act for the formation of a Chusik Hoesa. The full subscription price of the issued shares must, however, be paid into a domestic bank account before incorporation registration, and the bank must issue a certificate of share payment. There is no registry-managed escrow account, but subscription monies are held in a dedicated incorporation deposit account and may not be withdrawn before the company is entered in the commercial register. For non-resident investors seeking benefits under the Foreign Investment Promotion Act, the foreign investment must be at least KRW 100,000,000, remitted from an overseas account, and reported through a domestic foreign exchange bank; the bank then issues the Foreign Investment Registration Certificate. Registration tax is 0.4 percent of declared paid-in capital plus an education surcharge, together with court document fees.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

National Tax Service (NTS) of the Republic of Korea; electronic portal: HomeTax (www.hometax.go.kr); customs-related export filings and export declarations are processed through Korea Customs Service (KCS).

E-government filing portal

For a Korea-resident Chusik Hoesa, the statutory tax identifier is obtained by (1) completing incorporation under the Korean Commercial Act at the local District Court registry; (2) filing a business registration application with the competent NTS district tax office through the HomeTax portal within 20 days from the commencement of business; (3) attaching the corporate registration certificate, articles of incorporation, business premise lease or ownership proof, and representative identity documents; and (4) receiving the 10-digit Business Registration Number, which functions simultaneously as the corporate TIN and the mandatory VAT registration ID. For a foreign non-resident corporation with no domestic place of business, a Korean tax representative must be appointed, and the representative must apply through the HomeTax foreign business operator registration channel before the foreign entity supplies Korean-source goods or services; the NTS then assigns a foreign-entity TIN/VAT ID. English-language documents are accepted with certified Korean translations; there is no statutory minimum capital requirement for the Chusik Hoesa, and non-resident ownership is permitted without an automatic tax registration waiver.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

10%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

No monetary registration threshold - every person or entity carrying on a trade or business in Korea must file a business registration with the NTS within 20 days of commencement regardless of turnover; annual taxable sales below KRW 80 million merely qualify the taxpayer for simplified small-business VAT status, which reduces reporting and payment burdens but does not eliminate the registration obligation.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating for goods requires a supply to a non-resident buyer, an accepted export declaration proving the goods have physically departed Korea, and repatriation of the export proceeds by the statutory deadline under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act. Qualifying offshore digital and cross-border services are zero-rated where the place of supply is outside Korea and the services are performed or effectively used outside Korea; in all cases, the exporter must retain complete transactional, customs, transport, and foreign-exchange documentation to substantiate the 0% rate.

  • Export Declaration and customs clearance certificate issued by Korea Customs Service
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill evidencing physical export from Korea
  • Commercial invoice and packing list
  • Export contract, purchase order, letter of credit, or equivalent trade documentation
  • Export zero-rated VAT tax invoice
  • Foreign Exchange Received Certificate issued by an authorized Korean foreign exchange bank, or inward-remittance documentation directly linked to the underlying export

Electronic tax invoices are mandatory for all entities whose prior-year taxable turnover exceeds KRW 100 million; e-invoices must be issued and transmitted to the NTS through the HomeTax eSero system or an NTS-certified electronic tax invoice service provider, with the invoice data received by the NTS at or immediately after issuance. The NTS performs automated cross-matching of buyer and seller e-invoice data, refund validation, and risk-based audit; anti-fraud controls rely on mandatory electronic transmission, digital signing, central storage, and cross-checking of purchase and sales ledgers rather than physical fiscal stamping.

If the NTS or Korea Customs Service determines that export zero-rating is unsupported, fraudulent, or not substantiated by the required customs and foreign-exchange documentation, the supply is reclassified as a domestic taxable supply; VAT at 10% is retroactively assessed with accumulated interest, and penalty taxes are added, generally 10% of the underdeclared tax for negligent underreporting and 20% for fraudulent or repeated evasion. False electronic tax invoices or false export declarations may also trigger criminal prosecution under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act and the Customs Act, and the statutory collection period for fraudulent evasion is extended to 10 years, creating a long retrospective clawback window.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

South Korea operates a liberalized current account convertibility regime for trade-related payments, while capital account transactions remain subject to targeted declaration, reporting, and authorization procedures. The Foreign Exchange Transactions Act classifies transactions into ordinary and capital categories; current account items such as trade settlement, freight, insurance, and commissions are generally freely convertible through authorized foreign exchange banks. Capital account operations, including inbound equity investment by non-residents, loans, and cross-border intercompany lending, must be filed through the Foreign Exchange Information System, with post-transaction reporting within prescribed deadlines. There is no blanket surrender of export proceeds to the central bank; exporters may either retain foreign currency in onshore foreign currency accounts or convert into Korean won, but the underlying export payment must be settled through an authorized foreign exchange bank under the original export contract. Profit remittance, including dividends, requires a certificate of foreign investment registration, audited financial statements, evidence of tax clearance, and a foreign exchange bank confirmation that the remittance is consistent with the earnings recorded in the foreign investment registration. In practice, Korean banks impose enhanced documentary checks for repeated large dividend outflows and require proof of compliance with transfer-pricing rules, intra-group service agreements, and withholding tax settlement. The overall assessment is that Korea is a partially open capital account jurisdiction with operational convertibility on trade flows but administrative friction on equity returns and financial intercompany flows.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Under the Enforcement Decree of the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act, export proceeds must be collected and settled through authorized foreign exchange banks, generally within 18 months from the date of shipment or the date recorded in the goods export declaration. Where collection is delayed, the exporter must apply for an extension to the head of Korea Customs Service with supporting shipment and payment evidence. There is no mandatory conversion ratio; foreign currency receipts may be fully retained in a Foreign Currency Account provided that the inflows trace to a declared export sale, and conversions to Korean won may be executed at the customer rate of the authorized bank. The obligation is reported through the Federation of Banks and the Foreign Exchange Information System, and the bank retains the export documents, foreign exchange purchase/sale certificates, and B/L for audit.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Korea (The Bank of Korea) acting jointly with the Ministry of Economy and Finance under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act and the Foreign Exchange Information System

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low. Digital fintech EMIs operating on a pure electronic money license under the Electronic Financial Transactions Act cannot offer full commercial treasury accounts, deposit-taking, trade finance, or import settlement services that match the documentary requirements of a Chusik Hoesa handling battery-grade lithium carbonate imports. Local open-banking and payment startups are restricted from providing foreign currency accounts, capital verification certificates, or SWIFT trade services. Offshore unlicensed EMIs have no legal standing to service a Korean domestic entity because receiving business transaction funds on behalf of a Korean corporation would create a licensing exposure under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act and the Electronic Financial Transactions Act. Accordingly, the only compliant treasury route is through a domestic authorized foreign exchange bank.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

The parent entity or non-resident shareholder must remit the investment amount through an authorized foreign exchange bank into an 'Investment Purpose Foreign Currency Account' or a temporary Capital Escrow Account under the beneficiary Chusik Hoesa's name. The remittance must reference the Foreign Exchange Information System declaration type 'Acquisition of Securities by Non-Resident', foreign exchange purpose code 900100 for equity participation or the appropriate inward direct investment code provided by the receiving bank. The bank must issue the certificate of foreign investment registration after settlement, and the capital contribution must be evidenced by a board resolution, share issuance documents, and a Bank of Korea ancestral report through the reporting system. Documentary Collections can be used for low-risk repeat relationships, with delivery against documents, but are less preferred for battery-grade critical materials. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Low. Digital fintech EMIs operating on a pure electronic money license under the Electronic Financial Transactions Act cannot offer full commercial treasury accounts, deposit-taking, trade finance, or import settlement services that match the documentary requirements of a Chusik Hoesa handling battery-grade lithium carbonate imports. Local open-banking and payment startups are restricted from providing foreign currency accounts, capital verification certificates, or SWIFT trade services. Offshore unlicensed EMIs have no legal standing to service a Korean domestic entity because receiving business transaction funds on behalf of a Korean corporation would create a licensing exposure under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act and the Electronic Financial Transactions Act. Accordingly, the only compliant treasury route is through a domestic authorized foreign exchange bank.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: KRW 10 billion per transaction category (sales/purchases) for Local File and Master File; Country-by-Country Report for MNE groups with annual consolidated revenue above KRW 1 trillion (approx. EUR 750 million).

Economic substance scope: Korea applies a substance-over-form / General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) to disregard artificial or sham entities; a shell company with no office, employees, or business functions cannot enjoy treaty benefits under the OECD principal purpose test and may have income reattributed to the Korean Chusik Hoesa.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Transfer Pricing Documentation Failure and Non-Arm's Length Pricing

Statutory sanction: Under Article 11 LFITA: 10% penalty on the value of undisclosed related-party transactions; income adjustment to arm's length plus up to 40% negligent/understatement penalty under Framework Act on National Taxes Article 47-3; additional tax assessment and collection enforcement.

Director personal liability: KR tax law allows piercing where gross negligence or intentional misconduct is found; civil liability of directors for the resulting corporate tax deficiency and penalties; travel ban (overseas departure restraint) for unpaid tax exceeding KRW 50 million; criminal prosecution under Punishment of Tax Evaders Act (imprisonment up to 5 years) for wilful intent.
Shell Company / Economic Substance and Sham Transaction

Statutory sanction: GaAR recharacterization and denial of tax benefits (Article 17 of Framework Act on National Taxes); 40% civil penalty for submitting fraudulent returns; claim for evasion penalties under Punishment of Tax Evaders Act Article 3.

Director personal liability: Directors become personally liable under Article 39 Basic National Tax Act for the corporation's unpaid TRP if they yielded an intent to evade corporate tax; asset freeze, travel ban; criminal conviction may carry prison term up to 5 years and criminal fines.
Unreported Outbound Dividend / Withholding Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Corporate Tax Act Article 98: the withholding agent (Chusik Hoesa) is liable for 22% uncollected tax plus 10% per month late remittance surcharge (Article 47(4) Framework Act on National Taxes); punitive penalties of 40% of the undisclosed amount in aggravated evasion cases.

Director personal liability: Chief executive and board of directors are jointly and severally liable for withheld tax remitted to the state (Basic National Tax Act Article 38); personal bank accounts may be garnished; travel ban is triggered; criminal prison term under Punishment of Tax Evaders Act for intentional failure to withhold/remit.
