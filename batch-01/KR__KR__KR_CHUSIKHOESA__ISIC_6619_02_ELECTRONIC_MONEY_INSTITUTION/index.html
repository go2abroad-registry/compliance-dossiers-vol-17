Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

No statutory minimum paid-up capital is required for the incorporation of a Chusik Hoesa under the Commercial Act itself. However, because the target business is an electronic money institution under ISIC 6619, the Electronic Financial Transactions Act and its Enforcement Decree require a paid-up capital of at least KRW 2,000,000,000 for the issuance and management of electronic money. Payment must be made in cash, in Korean won, into a dedicated blocked escrow subscription account opened at a domestically licensed bank in the name of the company, and the bank must issue an official deposit certificate. The escrowed funds may not be released or returned to promoters except as permitted by law and must remain in place until the Financial Services Commission completes the EMI registration or formally waives or scales that capital requirement for a particular operating approval.

Funding & unlock path: The company must obtain a Fire Safety Completion Certificate from the local fire station after inspection of fire detection, alarm, suppression, emergency lighting, exit signs, and evacuation routes. A civil defense evacuation plan and assembly-area approval must be confirmed by the municipal civil defense office. Electrical installations and emergency power systems require certification from the Korea Electrical Safety Corporation. The workplace must satisfy the Ministry of Employment and Labor under the Occupational Safety and Health Act, including submission of a workplace risk assessment, operation of first-aid equipment, and subscription to workplace accident compensation insurance. For any physical server room, mandatory controls include clean-agent gas suppression, interlocked ventilation, smoke detection, structural load approval, and a fire and business continuity drill program.

Available election forms

None. A Korean Chusik Hoesa is a resident corporation taxed at the entity level under the Corporate Tax Act; South Korea does not operate a check-the-box or entity classification election. Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating is available under Article 21 of the Value Added Tax Act of the Republic of Korea for actual exports of goods and for specified international services supplied to non-residents. Goods exports must be physically departed from Korea under customs export clearance and the export proceeds must be collected. Services, including digital or electronic money and wallet services, may be zero-rated only when supplied to a non-resident or foreign enterprise for use wholly outside Korea and supported by documentary proof of overseas consumption; otherwise such financial or intermediation services are VAT-exempt rather than zero-rated.

  • Customs Export Declaration generated through the Korea Customs Service UNI-PASS system, evidencing export clearance and exit
  • Bill of Lading, air waybill, or electronic equivalent evidencing physical export movement
  • Commercial invoice and packing list
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (FIRC) or foreign exchange bank purchase record confirming receipt of export proceeds in convertible foreign currency
  • Written international service contract, non-resident recipient status evidence, proof of overseas usage, and foreign exchange remittance confirmation or SWIFT credit advice for zero-rated services

Mandatory electronic tax invoices must be issued and transmitted through the National Tax Service HomeTax e-invoice system, either directly via NTS-specified APIs or through certified e-invoice service providers. Tax invoices must be delivered by the statutory VAT invoice dates, and the electronic transmission data must reconcile with the VAT returns filed in the HomeTax portal. This is not a real-time fiscal stamp or continuous clearance jurisdiction; control is achieved through periodic VAT filing, cross-matching of e-invoices, and cash receipt reporting for consumer transactions.

Where export zero-rating is challenged or disallowed, the National Tax Service will assess output VAT at the standard 10% on the supply, deny input VAT refunds, and impose penalty surcharges, statutory interest, and late-filing penalties under the Value Added Tax Act, Framework Act on National Taxes, and Customs Act. Fraudulent export declarations, forged FIRCs, or false non-resident service documentation carry criminal liability, an extended 10-year assessment period for fraudulent returns, retrospective VAT clawbacks, and potential disqualification of the taxpayer from future zero-rating claims.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Korean won (KRW) is fully convertible for current-account transactions under FETA's negative-list system, including trade, services, royalties, ordinary business expenses, dividend remittances, and profit repatriation, subject to filing a Foreign Exchange Transactions Form with a licensed foreign exchange bank. Capital-account transactions such as inbound direct investment, intercompany loans, portfolio equity, and M&A are subject to prior declaration, reporting, or approval under the Foreign Investment Promotion Act and FETA. Dividend and profit remittance hurdles are principally tax and documentation related: the Korean Chusik Hoesa must first satisfy corporate income tax obligations, apply the correct dividend withholding tax rate, obtain a board resolution declaring the dividend, prepare audited financial statements where statutorily required, and file the remittance request with the bank using the appropriate FEIS purpose code. Capital controls under emergency safeguard measures remain legally available but are not currently imposed. Non-resident KRW accounts are permitted only for limited purposes, and all FX transactions must be supported by accurate FEIS reporting to avoid administrative penalties.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Under FETA, an exporter is required to collect export proceeds in foreign currency and deposit them into a domestic foreign exchange bank in Korea within 18 months from the date of export declaration, unless the exporter obtains an extension or an offshore-receipt approval from the Ministry of Economy and Finance. There is no statutory mandatory conversion ratio requiring export receipts to be converted into KRW; an exporter may retain the foreign currency in a domestic foreign-currency account or convert it at the applicable market rate. Extended collection periods apply for projects, shipbuilding, and deferred-payment exports. Failure to repatriate export proceeds within the approved timeline is a violation of FETA and may trigger fines, tax reassessment, and criminal liability.

Central bank supervision: The Bank of Korea (BOK) as the central bank, together with the Ministry of Economy and Finance (MOEF) as the foreign exchange policy authority, administers the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act (FETA), the Foreign Exchange Transactions Regulations, and the Foreign Exchange Information System (FEIS). The Financial Services Commission (FSC) and the Korea Financial Intelligence Unit (KoFIU) supervise AML/KYC and electronic financial business compliance for the EMI sector.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility Rating: Low for direct offshore EMI licensing in South Korea; Medium for an offshore EMI acting exclusively as a technology, compliance-software, or program-manager partner to a licensed Korean bank. Legal constraints: an offshore digital EMI without a domestic electronic financial business license under the Electronic Financial Transactions Act and, where applicable, the Specialized Credit Financial Business Act may not issue KRW virtual IBANs, hold e-money reserves, operate a Korean payment system, or settle on BOK-Wire+ directly. A foreign EMI licence is not passportable into South Korea. The workable legal structure is a bank partnership in which the licensed Korean bank owns the settlement accounts and clears payments, while the offshore EMI supplies software and analytics. Full local licensing would require a physical Seoul office, local compliance personnel, minimum capital, and supervisory approval under the FSC and KoFIU.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

All inbound equity capital for the Chusik Hoesa must be declared under the Foreign Investment Promotion Act before the remittance. The foreign investor opens a foreign-investment escrow account at a licensed foreign exchange bank in Korea in the name of the entity being incorporated, then remits the funds via SWIFT MT103 from a bank account held in the legal name of the declared investor. The MT103 must include a Foreign Direct Investment purpose code and reference the Foreign Investment Declaration Number or KOTRA certificate in the remittance narrative. The bank verifies the remitter's legal name, the source-of-funds documentation, beneficiary details, and the absence of any third-party remittance arrangement. After receipt, the bank issues a Foreign Capital Deposit Certificate, and the company uses that certificate to complete share subscription and commercial registration. The final foreign investment report is filed with the FSC through Kokplex or the designated bank using FEIS. No disbursement of the subscribed capital is permitted until the foreign investment declaration and deposit verification are completed.Outbound trade disbursements are cleared through the domestic foreign exchange bank using SWIFT MT103 for direct supplier payments, or SWIFT MT700-series letters of credit where bank-arranged documentary credit is required. All payments must be supported by an underlying trade or service contract, a customs declaration number where applicable, and the relevant FEIS purpose code. For KRW domestic payouts from the virtual IBAN wallet, the bank uses BOK-Wire+ for high-value and real-time settlement and the Cheque and Clearing System for batch low-value transfers. For cross-border settlements in USD, EUR, or GBP, the bank routes via its correspondent network and applies OFAC, UN/EU, Korean FX, and KoFIU screening at both the initiating bank and intermediary bank stages. Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Progressive national corporate income tax: 9% up to KRW 200 million; 19% from KRW 200 million to KRW 20 billion; 21% above KRW 20 billion. Local income tax surcharge is 10% of national tax, so the aggregate effective marginal rate at the top band is 23.1%.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Non-resident dividends paid by a Korean Chusik Hoesa are subject to withholding at 20% under the Corporate Tax Act plus a 10% local income tax surcharge, resulting in an aggregate statutory rate of 22% on gross dividend payments.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: The Local File is required when the total amount of transactions with foreign related parties exceeds KRW 5 billion in the fiscal year; the Master File is required when annual revenue exceeds KRW 100 billion and the aggregate amount of foreign related-party transactions exceeds KRW 5 billion; a Country-by-Country report is required for a group whose consolidated revenue equals or exceeds KRW 1 trillion. Financial transactions with foreign related parties may require separate loan-level disclosure when principal or debt in kind exceeds KRW 10 billion.

Economic substance scope: South Korea does not maintain a Gulf-Cooperation-Council-style Economic Substance Regulation. Instead, substance is enforced through: (i) the general anti-avoidance rule in the Framework Act for National Taxes; (ii) Korean controlled foreign corporation rules, which currently impose immediate Korean taxation on the undistributed passive income of low-tax foreign subsidiaries without economic substance; (iii) thin-capitalization and excessive-management-fee disallowance rules; (iv) the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act requiring reporting of foreign direct investment; and (v) for licensed EMI institutions, the Specialized Credit Financial Business Act and Electronic Financial Transactions Act, which require a physical Korean place of business, executive directors, internal control governance, AML compliance officers, and audited financial statements. Failure to maintain real economic substance is a ground for license revocation and for denial of treaty residual withholding tax relief.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Corporate Income Tax Filing

Statutory sanction: Under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act, imprisonment up to 5 years for aggravated fraud and a fine up to 2 times the unpaid tax; additional 40% negligence penalty and 0.022% daily late-payment surcharge; mandatory correction order and interest on deferred tax.

Director personal liability: The court may pierce the corporate veil when the Chusik Hoesa is found to be an alter ego of the governing directors; directors become jointly and severally liable for tax restitution, may be placed on the immigration travel-ban list, their bank accounts and residences may be seized, and they face arrest and imprisonment in the same criminal proceeding.
Artificial Shell Company Arrangement and Licence Abuse

Statutory sanction: Under the Electronic Financial Transactions Act and Specialized Credit Financial Business Act, the Financial Services Commission may revoke the licence, order suspension, and impose administrative fines up to KRW 100 million or more; the corporation may be ordered to liquidate.

Director personal liability: Directors who ratified the sham structure are liable for damages to corporate creditors and users, may be prohibited from financial-company directorships for up to 10 years, banned from travel by the Financial Services Commission or the court, and exposed to criminal imprisonment for fraud under the Aggravated Punishment of Specific Economic Crimes Act.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance and TP Documentation Failure

Statutory sanction: The National Tax Service may adjust Korean taxable income to the arm's length result and assess the understated tax, an understatement penalty of up to 40% of the resulting tax, and a daily late-payment surcharge of 0.022%; failure to furnish documentation may also trigger a separate administrative fine under the Adjustment of International Taxes Act.

Director personal liability: Directors who instructed or participated in the transfer pricing manipulation can be jointly assessed in equity-collection proceedings; if the adjustment is supported by evidence of fraudulent concealment, directors face criminal tax-evasion prosecution, travel bans, and attachment of personal assets.
Anti-Money Laundering and Beneficial Ownership Violation

Statutory sanction: Under the Act on Reporting and Use of Specified Financial Transaction Information, imprisonment up to 5 years and a criminal fine up to KRW 50 million; the Financial Services Commission can suspend or revoke the EMI licence and order withdrawal of the harmful activity; administrative penalties may be cumulative.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable for civil restitution for money lost by users as a result of AML failures; on criminal conviction they may be sentenced to imprisonment, placed on travel-ban lists, and disqualified from serving as a director of any financial services company in Korea.
Failure to Withhold and Remit Non-Resident Dividend, Royalty, and Interest Tax

Statutory sanction: The withholding agent remains liable for the uncollected national tax plus a 10% local income tax surcharge, a 20% negligence penalty for non-withholding, and a daily late-payment surcharge of 0.022%; if the non-resident later claims the reduced treaty rate, the company must still prove beneficial ownership and proper filing.

Director personal liability: Because the withholding obligation is statutory and non-delegable, the managing director responsible for financial distribution or remittance can be personally assessed for the unpaid withholding tax under a joint liability order when the corporation has insufficient assets; serious breaches also carry criminal liability for embezzlement of tax withheld but not paid.
