Although the Commercial Act does not require ownership of premises, the National Tax Service requires that the registered place of business be a real location where corporate books, records, personnel and assets can be kept or controlled. A commercial mail drop or post-office box will not satisfy the place-of-business test for a company providing outbound market-entry consultancy and invoicing abroad. A virtual office may be acceptable only if the Korean provider supplies a physical suite, a dedicated private desk or office leased under a genuine contract, local management access, and permission to display the company signboard. In this industry, a coworking membership with a private lockable room and a physical resident manager is the minimum permissible arrangement; a mailbox-only address is likely to be rejected by the local tax office as well as by Korean banks during account opening.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

National Tax Service (NTS) of the Republic of Korea, electronically administered through the HomeTax online portal at hometax.go.kr; export and customs-side declarations are administered by Korea Customs Service (KCS) through UNI-PASS

E-government filing portal

For a foreign non-resident entity, first determine whether a Korean domestic place of business (DioP) exists under the Framework Act on National Taxes. If a DioP exists, the entity must file a Business Registration Certificate application with the district tax office having jurisdiction over the Korean office, within 20 calendar days from commencement of business in Korea. The filing must be accompanied by the foreign entity's certificate of incorporation, articles of association, apostilled corporate registry extracts or notarized translations, board resolution appointing the Korean place-of-business manager, power of attorney designating a Korean tax manager, proof of the Korean business address (lease or ownership), and a certificate of foreign tax residence. NTS then issues the 10-digit Business Registration Number (BRN), which simultaneously serves as the corporate TIN, VAT registration ID, and e-Tax Invoice reporting identifier. A foreign non-resident entity without a Korean DioP that derives only Korean-source withholding income must register through a Korean tax representative to obtain a withholding TIN; it is not permitted to issue Korean VAT invoices. Failure to register creates unregistered-business additional tax exposure and bars input VAT recovery.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation (Chusik Hoesa is a domestic stock corporation under the Corporate Tax Act with worldwide taxable income; no pass-through or transparent taxation; profits are subject to corporate income tax and dividends to non-residents are subject to withholding tax)

Available election forms

The Republic of Korea does not provide a U.S.-style check-the-box pass-through election. Statutory elections that do apply include the consolidated corporation tax regime for qualifying 100-percent-owned domestic parent-subsidiary groups, foreign tax credit versus deduction elections, and permissible accounting/tax credit elections under the Corporate Tax Act. Hybrid mismatch rules operate as mandatory anti-abuse adjustments rather than elective mechanisms.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

10%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

No monetary threshold for a corporation; a Chusik Hoesa is a mandatory VAT registrant from the first day of business. The simplified taxable person turnover bracket is available only to individual proprietors and cannot apply to a Chusik Hoesa.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under the Value-Added Tax Act, zero-rating applies to exported goods and to services supplied to a non-resident or foreign enterprise for use or consumption outside Korea. For the ISIC 7020 overseas market-entry consultancy service, zero-rating is available only if the customer is a non-resident/foreign enterprise without a Korean place of business, the service is genuinely performed for and delivered/consumed overseas, the contract and invoice are in foreign currency, and payment is received in foreign exchange from abroad. Qualifying offshore digital services may also be zero-rated if supplied to non-residents for offshore use; supplies of digital services to Korean-resident consumers remain subject to 10% VAT.

  • Korea Customs Service Export Declaration Acceptance Certificate (the Korean equivalent of an export Bill of Entry), issued through the UNI-PASS system
  • Bill of Lading, Air Waybill, or multimodal transport document showing the Korean exporter as shipper and the foreign buyer as consignee
  • Foreign exchange receipt confirmation issued by a Korean licensed foreign-exchange bank (the Korean FIRC-equivalent inward remittance certificate) evidencing receipt of export proceeds in foreign currency
  • Commercial invoice and packing list corresponding to the export declaration, including customs tariff codes, quantities, FOB/CIF values and payment terms
  • Executed cross-border service contract, overseas deliverable/report, foreign-currency invoice and bank remittance receipt for the ISIC 7020 consultancy service export; plus, if available, the foreign customer's non-resident status or foreign business registration certificate

All tax invoices from a VAT-registered business must be issued as electronic tax invoices (e-Tax Invoices) through NTS HomeTax or an NTS-certified EDI program and transmitted to the NTS by the end of the following business day. Korea does not operate a real-time line-item fiscal clearance or anti-fraud digital stamping regime; instead, the NTS uses centralized e-Tax Invoice matching, cross-referencing against customs export declarations, cash receipts, credit card merchant data and bank foreign-exchange receipts. Failure to issue or transmit e-Tax Invoices triggers additional tax under the Value-Added Tax Act.

NTS and Korea Customs Service jointly cross-validate export declarations, e-Tax Invoices, transport documents, ship/air departure records, and foreign-currency remittance inflows to the exporter's Korean bank account. If export proceeds are not repatriated and proved within the statutory period, or if the documentation bundle is incomplete, fabricated, or non-contemporaneous, NTS will reverse the 0% rate, assess 10% VAT on the supply value, and impose additional tax penalties under the Value-Added Tax Act for late or false e-Tax Invoices and unsubstantiated refund claims. Refunds previously paid are clawed back with statutory interest. Repeated or fraudulent export zero-rating claims may trigger criminal prosecution under the Act on the Punishment of Tax Evaders, including imprisonment and fines computed on the evaded tax, together with public disclosure.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Korean won operates under a liberalised but regulated foreign exchange regime. Onshore current account transactions such as service fees, dividends, royalties and payment for market-entry consulting services are generally freely convertible once submitted to an authorised foreign exchange bank under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act. Capital account transactions, including inbound equity investment and foreign loans, are subject to a reporting and declaration system rather than prior approval, with thresholds requiring filing with the Bank of Korea through the Korea Foreign Exchange Information Network (KFEN). Profit remittance is permissible after statutory tax clearance, audited financial statements, shareholder resolution and board approval; however, the bank may require additional evidence of a genuine underlying business purpose, proof of withholding tax payment, and absence of tax arrears. The Ministry of Economy and Finance retains emergency policy tools under Article 6 of the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act, allowing temporary conversion requirements or capital controls during severe external imbalances. Consequently, convertibility is commercially acceptable for a low-risk consultancy entity, but treasury operations must preserve a complete documentary trail to prove genuine trade and service transactions, avoid mixed-purpose remittances, and comply with each bank's internal KYC and anti-money laundering surveillance. Practical convertibility for a Chusik Hoesa is supported for operating expenses and cross-border consulting fees, provided all transactions are declared according to current account regulations.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Under the Revised Foreign Exchange Transaction Regulations, export proceeds and service revenue from overseas operations must normally be collected within 6 months from the date of export or the date the consulting service is rendered. Extensions are possible with authorised foreign exchange bank approval if justifiable trade or settlement delays exist. There is currently no statutory mandatory conversion ratio for ordinary export proceeds; exporters and service providers may retain foreign currency receipts in a designated Foreign Currency Account at a domestic commercial bank. Any conversion of held foreign currency into Korean won must be executed through an authorised foreign exchange bank, and if the conversion amount exceeds the prescribed report threshold, the transaction must be reported to the Bank of Korea through KFEN. Documentary evidence such as the original export declaration, service contract, commercial invoice, and beneficiary customer confirmation must be retained to prove the genuine trade or service nature of the proceeds.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Korea (BOK) and Ministry of Economy and Finance (MOEF) through the Foreign Exchange Transaction Regulations and Korea Foreign Exchange Information Network (KFEN)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Cross-border digital EMIs such as Wise B2B or similar neobank payment firms cannot establish themselves as the primary clearing bank for a Korean Chusik Hoesa. Under the Electronic Financial Transactions Act, any payment service provider must be licensed by the Financial Services Commission, and an offshore EMI without a Korean license is legally barred from maintaining settlement accounts or conducting foreign exchange brokering for Korean resident entities. Such platforms may only operate in partnership with a licensed Korean bank as a remittance interface, but they cannot replace the statutory corporate banking relationship, physical KYC, and foreign exchange declaration obligations. Practical viability is therefore Low for the main treasury rail; the platform may be used as an ancillary payment initiation channel if the receiving bank, beneficiary identification, and purpose codes are fully disclosed and compliant with BOK regulations.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

The non-resident shareholder executes a SWIFT MT103 transfer into the entity's capital escrow account at a tier-1 foreign exchange bank, using the Korea Foreign Exchange Information Network (KFEN) declaration code for direct equity investment. The receiving bank issues a Foreign Currency Receipt Confirmation and the corporate treasury must submit the foreign investment notification to KOTRA or the relevant Korean authority where the Foreign Investment Promotion Act threshold is applicable. Capital verification is completed by obtaining a bank deposit certificate, filing the paid-in capital with the Korean commercial court, issuing shares in accordance with the board resolution, and updating the shareholder registry. For current account revenue, the underlying consulting contracts and tax invoices must be matched to each inbound remittance. For profit repatriation, the firm must use the appropriate dividend remittance purpose code, provide withholding tax payment certificates, and maintain audited financial statements and shareholder meeting minutes.For overseas market-entry consultancy, outbound settlement is executed through SWIFT MT103 messages for service fees and professional charges, with full name-and-address fields for both remitter and beneficiary. If the scope incidentally involves physical goods, standard clearing instruments such as Commercial Letters of Credit and Documentary Collections can be used through the same tier-1 bank. The bank requires the underlying contract, commercial invoice, tax invoice, service completion certificate, and a foreign exchange declaration showing the proper purpose code. Payments exceeding regulatory thresholds are reported under KFEN, and the treasury must ensure that no outbound transaction is fragmented to avoid reporting duties. All outgoing payments should be pre-screened against sanctions and restricted-party lists before release.Onshore deliverable forward foreign exchange contracts (FX forwards) with an authorised Korean foreign exchange bank, strictly supported by an underlying current account or approved capital account exposure.Cross-currency swaps for genuine investment-linked cash flows where a verifiable commercial exposure to foreign currency debt or equity exists and prior bank compliance approval has been obtained.Non-deliverable forwards (NDFs) are not generally accessible to onshore Korean resident entities for speculative treasury purposes; access is limited to internationally active banks and specific approved hedge structures. The Chusik Hoesa should therefore rely on deliverable forward outrights and only consider NDFs after obtaining written regulatory confirmation of eligibility.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Cross-border digital EMIs such as Wise B2B or similar neobank payment firms cannot establish themselves as the primary clearing bank for a Korean Chusik Hoesa. Under the Electronic Financial Transactions Act, any payment service provider must be licensed by the Financial Services Commission, and an offshore EMI without a Korean license is legally barred from maintaining settlement accounts or conducting foreign exchange brokering for Korean resident entities. Such platforms may only operate in partnership with a licensed Korean bank as a remittance interface, but they cannot replace the statutory corporate banking relationship, physical KYC, and foreign exchange declaration obligations. Practical viability is therefore Low for the main treasury rail; the platform may be used as an ancillary payment initiation channel if the receiving bank, beneficiary identification, and purpose codes are fully disclosed and compliant with BOK regulations.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

Progressive base corporate income tax rates under the Corporate Income Tax Act (CITA): 10% on taxable income up to KRW 200 million; 20% on taxable income between KRW 200 million and KRW 20 billion; 22% on taxable income between KRW 20 billion and KRW 300 billion; 25% on taxable income exceeding KRW 300 billion. A local income tax surcharge equal to 10% of the assessed corporate income tax is imposed, producing a maximum combined headline effective rate of 27.5%.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

22% on the gross amount of dividends distributed to non-resident shareholders, composed of 20% national corporate income tax withholding plus 2% local income tax withholding; DTA can reduce the rate upon proper claim.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Local File is mandatory when the aggregate value of any category of foreign related-party transactions, such as services, tangible goods, intangibles, or loans, is KRW 3 billion or more during the fiscal year. Master File is mandatory when annual revenue is KRW 100 billion or more and total foreign related-party transactions are KRW 5 billion or more. Failure to prepare and submit these documents when required deprives the taxpayer of the presumption of good faith and increases penalty exposure.

Economic substance scope: Korea does not maintain a generic Economic Substance Regulations regime analogous to certain offshore jurisdictions, but CITA Article 14, Korean Tax Court precedent, and the general anti-abuse doctrine require genuine economic substance. For an ISIC 7020 overseas market entry consultancy, the core income-generating activities (CIGA) must be performed in Korea: client solicitation, regulatory analysis, market feasibility, contracting, risk-taking, and executive decision-making. A foreign subsidiary or foreign branch that merely receives invoices without employees, office premises, or actual functional performance will be disregarded or recharacterized, and CFC rules will currently tax undistributed profits earned in a low-tax foreign entity.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Underreporting

Statutory sanction: CITA Article 60 and Article 76 apply additional taxes of 20% for non-filing, 10% for negligent underreporting, and 40% for fraudulent underreporting; daily late-payment interest compounds; Punishment of Tax Evaders Act Article 3 authorizes imprisonment up to three years and fines up to three times the evaded tax, with aggravated penalties for large-scale evasion.

Director personal liability: Directors and actual controlling persons are personally liable under National Tax Collection Act Article 39 as secondary taxpayers if the corporation dissipates assets to avoid collection; personal bank accounts, homes, and overseas assets can be seized; the National Tax Service may impose a travel ban and list the director and corporation as delinquent taxpayers with public blacklisting; the corporate veil may be pierced entirely where the Chusik Hoesa is found to be a sham or merely an instrumentality for the director's personal profit.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: International Tax Coordination Act Articles 5 and 8 permit the National Tax Service to re-determine the arm's length price and increase taxable income; the resulting tax deficiency attracts the underreporting additional tax of 10% to 40% and daily late-payment interest; where transfer pricing documentation is absent or false, the penalty margin is magnified and the burden of proof shifts adversely to the taxpayer.

Director personal liability: The representative director and actual management can be held jointly liable if the transfer pricing deficiency is coupled with fraudulent accounting, destruction of evidence, or deliberate concealment; directors who approve non-arm's-length foreign payments may face criminal liability under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act for aiding tax evasion; personal asset attachment, travel bans, and blacklisting apply when the corporation becomes deliberately insolvent to avoid payment.
Offshore Shell Arrangement and CFC Avoidance

Statutory sanction: Korean CFC rules under CITA Article 55 and related International Tax Coordination Act provisions require current inclusion of the low-tax foreign entity's retained income in the taxable income of the Korean shareholder; the National Tax Service disallows treaty reduction and can recharacterize the foreign entity as a sham; administrative penalties include up to 40% additional tax for fraudulent failure to report CFC income, with daily late-payment interest and additional penalties for failure to report foreign financial accounts.

Director personal liability: Directors who own or control the foreign shell can be personally assessed for the unpaid tax under asset-restraint and de facto manager liability rules; Korean tax authorities may pierce the corporate veil by treating the foreign entity as the taxpayer's alter ego; civil liability extends to recovery of diverted profits, director compensation, and transferred assets; criminal prosecution can lead to imprisonment, disgorgement, and international travel restriction while under investigation.
