Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

None as to a minimum statutory paid-up capital for a Chusik Hoesa under the Korean Commercial Act. However, the full subscription price for the issued shares must be paid in before the incorporation registration application is submitted, and the payment must be made through a designated Korean bank and evidenced by a bank-issued capital payment deposit certificate. Funds are effectively unavailable for withdrawal to the corporate treasury until the Commercial Registry registration is completed; there is no separate post-registration escrow lockbox requirement for a general service corporation, but banks may apply enhanced due diligence and restricted disbursement procedures for a maritime towage and salvage entity classified as Medium-Risk Enhanced.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

National Tax Service (NTS), Republic of Korea, 90 Gajeong-ro, Yuseong-gu, Daejeon; online portal HomeTax (https://www.hometax.go.kr); export and customs declarations are processed with Korea Customs Service (KCS) via UNI-PASS (https://unipass.customs.go.kr).

E-government filing portal

A foreign non-resident entity must first determine whether it has a domestic place of business in Korea under the Value-Added Tax Act. If it maintains a branch, fixed office, vessel operating base, or other permanent establishment with management authority in Korea, it must file a business registration application with the district NTS tax office having jurisdiction over that domestic place of business. The application is made in person or through a tax representative and requires: (i) foreign certificate of incorporation and articles of association authenticated or apostilled; (ii) evidence of the domestic place of business (lease agreement, land registry abstract, or office ownership certificate); (iii) appointment of a domestic chief officer or tax representative with a domestic address and a power of attorney if the foreign corporation has no Korean-resident officer; (iv) passport/ID copies of the authorised representative; and (v) any relevant foreign business/tax registration certificate. If the entity has no Korean domestic place of business but is nonetheless liable for Korean VAT on supplies to Korea, NTS provides a HomeTax foreign-business registration path in which the foreign taxpayer supplies its home-country tax identification number, foreign address, and representative details and receives a Korean VAT identification number. Once registered, NTS issues a 10-digit business registration number, which functions as the Korean TIN and VAT ID, and the taxpayer must activate electronic tax-invoice issuance, e-filing credentials, and foreign currency receipt reporting through HomeTax.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

10%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

No de minimis threshold in KRW: VAT registration is mandatory from the commencement of business for every person engaged in a supply of goods or services, including foreign non-resident entities with a domestic place of business and foreign businesses supplying qualifying electronic or digital services to Korean customers. Simplified VAT-filing mechanics are available for small businesses with prior-year supplies below KRW 80 million, but the registration threshold remains zero.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating under Korean VAT law is available for exported goods and for services supplied to, used in, or consumed in a foreign country, including international maritime towage and salvage engagement by a Korean marine operator. The service must be rendered pursuant to a contract with a foreign counterparty, the place of performance must be outside Korea or in international waters, and the consideration must be invoiced to and settled from the foreign party in foreign currency. For deep-sea towage and salvage, a normal commercial contract such as Lloyds Standard Form of Salvage Agreement or an international towage contract supports zero-rating only if complete documentary proof of export performance and foreign exchange earning is maintained. Qualifying offshore digital services supplied to non-resident customers are also zero-rated when effectively used or consumed outside Korea.

  • Customs Export Declaration (export clearance report accepted through Korea Customs Service UNI-PASS electronic system, including the declaration number and export approval date)
  • Marine Bill of Lading, sea waybill, or equivalent ocean-transport document for towed equipment, vessels, and salvage service performance
  • Salvage or towage contract (Lloyds Standard Form of Salvage Agreement, SCOPIC evidence, or international towage hire agreement) identifying the foreign principal and currency of remuneration
  • Commercial invoice and itemised salvage/towage remuneration statement (statement of account and final disbursements)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (Foreign Exchange Receipt/Settlement Certificate issued by a Korean Foreign Exchange Bank) evidencing actual receipt of the salvage or towage consideration in foreign currency
  • Master's service report or salvage service performance report (narrative, location of salvage, casualty log, towage route, and time of service)
  • Certificate of vessel registration or classification and international voyage evidence for the tug or salvage vessel

Korea operates a mandatory electronic tax-invoice (e-tax invoice) system through the HomeTax portal. For every VAT-taxable supply, the supplier must issue a tax invoice and transmit the electronic tax invoice to NTS by the end of the day following invoice issuance, using HomeTax online filing, EDI, or an approved e-invoice service-provider API. There is no real-time fiscal pre-clearance or government digital stamp requirement; fraud prevention is achieved by automated cross-matching of issued/received e-invoices with VAT returns, customs export declarations, and foreign-exchange settlement data, plus system-generated unique invoice sequences and mandatory taxpayer authentication.

Export zero-rating is available only where the goods or services are physically exported or rendered abroad and the foreign exchange is properly repatriated within the statutory export settlement period. If the prescribed evidence is missing, the service is diverted to domestic use, or the foreign-exchange proceeds are not received or repatriated within the deadline, the tax authorities recharacterise the supply as a taxable domestic service and assess VAT at 10%, with statutory additional tax and overdue interest. False invoices, fictitious export declarations, or fraudulent use of export certificates are treated as tax evasion: the VAT credit or refund is clawed back retrospectively, the taxpayer is liable for the underpaid VAT plus surcharges and daily overdue penalties, and the prosecutor may impose criminal penalties (fines and imprisonment) under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act. The ordinary five-year VAT reassessment period applies to honest-error cases; in cases involving false documentation or fraudulent foreign-exchange schemes, Korean authorities may pursue investigation without the benefit of a shortened limitation period.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

South Korea operates a liberal but notification-heavy foreign exchange regime. Current-account transactions, including invoicing for offshore towage and salvage services, are generally free and convertible, subject to submission of supporting contract evidence to a designated foreign exchange bank. Capital-account transactions, including inbound foreign direct investment into a Chusik Hoesa, are permitted but require pre-registration under the Foreign Investment Promotion Act via the ROK government Invest KOREA portal, followed by issuance of a Foreign Investment Registration Certificate (for large or review-worthy amounts) or filing confirmation with the chosen foreign exchange bank. KRW is convertible to foreign currency for bona fide trade and service payments, but banks must verify underlying documentation and customer purpose codes. The key practical hurdles are documentation-heavy remittance procedures, anti-money laundering checks on the beneficial owner, strict beneficial ownership disclosure to banks under the Real Name Financial Transaction Act, and the need to demonstrate that the salvage or towage contract is economically real and not a disguised capital movement. Profit remittance is permitted after tax clearance and after presenting annual audited financial statements or a certified public accountant confirmation, but the remittance is treated as a dividend subject to Corporate Tax Act withholding in the absence of a tax treaty benefit. Foreign currency deposits may be held in domestic foreign currency accounts without mandatory conversion to KRW, but any conversion must be priced at market and reported through the bank. Overall, current-account convertibility is high, while capital-account flows require structured documentation, bank approvals, and post-transaction reporting to the Bank of Korea through the bank's foreign exchange information system.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Under the current Foreign Exchange Transaction Framework, South Korea has no mandatory percentage surrender requirement for export service proceeds; a domestic entity may retain up to 100% of foreign currency receipts in a foreign exchange deposit account held with a licensed foreign exchange bank. The statutory discipline is procedural rather than conversion-based: proceeds from cross-border maritime services must be collected through an officially designated foreign exchange bank, must be reconciled against the underlying towage or salvage contract and invoice, and the bank must record the receipt in the Foreign Exchange Information System within the reporting cycle prescribed by MOEF regulations. In practice, foreign currency receipts from service exports must enter the Korean banking system; they cannot settle through unlicensed offshore digital payment intermediaries. There is no fixed statutory export surrender timeline for service income under the current law, but the underlying salvage or towage contract should state a commercial payment due date, and the bank will require the entity to submit evidence of collection status for annual foreign exchange audits. If the proceeds are received from a jurisdiction with restricted settlement, the bank may require a dual-use letter of credit or an independent payment undertaking from a correspondent bank. The mandatory conversion ratio is zero; however, any conversion to KRW, or any use of those foreign currency funds for payments to third parties, will be treated as a foreign exchange transaction requiring appropriate purpose codes and source documentation.

Central bank supervision: The Bank of Korea (BOK), operating in conjunction with the Ministry of Economy and Finance (MOEF) as the primary foreign exchange policy authority under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act and the Financial Services Commission (FSC) as supervisory coordinator; day-to-day foreign exchange business is executed exclusively through licensed foreign exchange banks in South Korea.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Offshore fintech electronic money institutions are not viable as a primary banking rail for a resident Korean Chusik Hoesa. Under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act, only foreign exchange banks licensed by the MOEF and authorized by the Bank of Korea may conduct foreign exchange business with Korean residents. Unlicensed offshore EMIs, including digital wallet providers and non-bank payment institutions, are legally barred from opening KRW-denominated accounts, collecting service export proceeds on behalf of a Korean resident entity, or executing outward remittances in KRW. An offshore EMI may be used only for non-KRW, third-country receivables in jurisdictions where the Korean entity maintains a separate operational presence, but this is ancillary and cannot replace a domestic corporate account. Any attempt to route salvage or towage payments through an unlicensed EMI will be rejected by Korean banks and may trigger adverse foreign exchange violation findings, monetary penalties, and a freeze on existing banking relationships. The compliance stance is that the Korean entity must onboard with at least one Tier-1 domestic commercial bank, with SWIFT connectivity, and any overseas settlement must be cleared through licensed correspondent banking channels.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

10%–25% progressive national corporate income tax: 10% on tax base up to KRW 200 million, 20% on KRW 200 million to KRW 20 billion, 22% on KRW 20 billion to KRW 300 billion, and 25% above KRW 300 billion; plus a 10% local income tax surtax producing an aggregate headline range of 11%–27.5%.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

22% aggregate on outbound dividends (20% national dividend withholding plus 2% local income tax surtax), unless reduced by a bilateral double tax treaty. Treaty rates often range from 5% to 15% depending on shareholding thresholds.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: KRW 5,000,000,000 in aggregate foreign-related-party transactions for the taxable year triggers the contemporaneous Statement of Transactions with Foreign Related Parties and the Local File; Master File is required when consolidated revenue exceeds KRW 100 billion and foreign-related-party transactions exceed KRW 10 billion, and Country-by-Country reporting applies to groups with annual consolidated revenue of at least KRW 700 billion.

Economic substance scope: Korea does not apply the offshore ESR regime, but economic substance is enforced through substance-over-form, the anti-conduit provisions of the Korea-U.S. and similar DTAs, and the specific foreign corporation deemed-income rules of the Law for Coordination of International Tax Affairs. For an offshore towage and salvage Chusik Hoesa, Core Income-Generating Activities must include tugboat chartering negotiation, salvage command, crew supervision, safety and emergency-response coordination, invoicing and bank account control in Korea; if those functions are performed from a head office in another jurisdiction, profits must be allocated accordingly and transfer-pricing returns must reflect the true value-creation locations.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Bank Protocol Fraud

Statutory sanction: Corporate Tax Act Articles 66 and 76, and Tax Evasion Punishment Act Article 3: additional tax equal to 40% of omitted tax plus daily late-payment interest, criminal fine up to five times the evaded tax, and imprisonment up to 7 years; the tax authority can blacklist the corporation and representative for three years and revoke the maritime towage/salvage licence.

Director personal liability: The representative director who signs false returns is personally secondarily liable under National Tax Collection Act Article 10 where corporate assets are insufficient; the director's personal bank accounts and vessels can be seized, the director is placed on the National Tax Service travel-ban list, and criminal conviction for evasion exceeding KRW 300 million carries a mandatory minimum imprisonment term; civil restitution claims by minority shareholders and creditors can pierce the corporate veil on alter-ego grounds.
Intercompany Transfer Pricing and Profit Shifting

Statutory sanction: Law for Coordination of International Tax Affairs Articles 4 and 6: the tax authority re-computes taxable income under the arm's-length principle and imposes a 20% unfaithful-return penalty and tax interest; failure to produce contemporaneous documentation can expose a KRW 20 million to KRW 150 million administrative fine, denial of the refund statute protection, and criminal punishment where false invoices are used to disguise the transfer.

Director personal liability: Directors are joint tortfeasors with the company where they sign false transfer-pricing documentation or order the destruction of vessel charter records; Korean courts will pierce the corporate veil where the director commingles personal escrow accounts, uses the offshore affiliate as a conduit, or abandons corporate formalities, thereby imposing personal liability for the reassessed tax, interest, penalties, and litigation costs; the director may also be personally investigated for fraud and prohibited from leaving Korea.
