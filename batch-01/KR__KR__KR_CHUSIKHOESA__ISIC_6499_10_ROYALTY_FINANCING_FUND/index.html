No statutory minimum paid-up capital exists for an ordinary Chusik Hoesa, but each promoter must subscribe at least one ordinary share. Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

National Tax Service (NTS) of the Republic of Korea; official electronic portal HomeTax (hometax.go.kr), supplemented by Korea Customs Service UNI-PASS (unipass.customs.go.kr) for customs export clearance records

E-government filing portal

A foreign non-resident entity must first determine whether it maintains a domestic place of business in Korea. If establishing a Korean branch or a fixed domestic place of business, register the foreign company's domestic business establishment with the relevant Korean court registry under the Commercial Act, then file the business registration application through the NTS HomeTax portal or at the competent district tax office. Upon approval, NTS issues the 10-digit Business Registration Number (BRN), which serves as the unified Tax Identification Number (TIN) for corporate income tax, VAT, and withholding tax. For a foreign company with no Korean physical presence that supplies taxable goods or services, including digital services and cross-border financing/royalty services, to Korean customers, the company must complete a direct VAT registration through HomeTax as a foreign VAT-registered operator, appoint a domestic tax representative unless exempted, and obtain a BRN; a simple overseas vendor identifier is not sufficient. The application package must include certified incorporation documents, memorandum and articles of association, foreign business registration certificate (with apostille or consular legalization where applicable), board resolution appointing the local tax representative, proof of domestic place of business or Korean digital supply activity, and financial information required to determine taxable scope. The NTS assigns the BRN after verifying the submitted documents; failure to register before commencing taxable supplies may trigger registration penalties and late-filing additional tax.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation; the Chusik Hoesa (stock corporation) is a domestic corporation subject to Korean corporate income tax on its worldwide income at entity level, with no pass-through eligibility to shareholders; non-resident ownership is permitted, and a Korean branch of a foreign company is subject to entity-level tax on Korean-source income and branch profit tax on after-tax branch earnings attributable to the foreign head office.

Available election forms

Korean tax law does not provide a US-style check-the-box election, LLC pass-through election, or hybrid mismatch preferential classification. A domestic corporation may elect consolidated corporate income tax filing only for wholly-owned domestic subsidiaries under the Corporate Tax Act, but this is an income-tax consolidation election and is not an entity classification or VAT election. For VAT purposes, the simplified taxpayer status available to individual sole proprietors is not available to a stock corporation. Therefore, for this entity, the statutory election mechanism may be stated as: None other than ordinary corporate income tax elections available under the Corporate Tax Act.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

10%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

No general VAT registration turnover threshold applies to corporations; any corporation making taxable supplies of goods or services in Korea is required to be VAT-registered from the date it commences business. For individual sole proprietors, the simplified VAT regime applies only where annual taxable turnover is below KRW 80,000,000, but a Chusik Hoesa is never eligible for simplified taxpayer status.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A 0% VAT export rate applies to supplies of goods actually exported from Korea to a foreign buyer, provided the goods leave Korea under a valid customs export declaration and the exporter retains the full statutory export evidence package. Qualifying services, including financing-related royalties and offshore digital services, are zero-rated when the recipient is a non-resident, the service is performed for use outside Korea, the consideration is duly earned in foreign currency, and the supply qualifies as an export of services under the Korean VAT Act and Foreign Exchange Transactions Act; the zero-rating claim must be supported by commercial contracts, foreign-currency inward remittance, and, for services embedded in cross-border trade, the underlying customs or service delivery documentation.

  • Export Declaration (Export Clearance Certificate) generated through Korea Customs Service UNI-PASS
  • Bill of Lading, Air Waybill, or multimodal transport document evidencing physical export from Korea
  • Zero-rated statutory tax invoice (e-tax invoice) issued and transmitted through HomeTax
  • Commercial invoice and packing list matching the export declaration particulars
  • Bank inward foreign-exchange remittance certificate or Foreign Exchange Receipt Certificate issued by a Korean authorized foreign-exchange bank
  • Underlying cross-border contract, purchase order, service agreement, or royalty financing agreement evidencing foreign counterparty and commercial purpose

VAT-registered businesses must issue a valid Korean tax invoice for taxable supplies. The mandatory electronic tax invoice (e-tax invoice) system requires qualifying registered businesses to issue and transmit e-tax invoice details to the NTS through HomeTax or through an approved e-invoice service provider, using API integration or the HomeTax B2B interface, with transmission generally required by the day following issuance. There is no pre-issuance fiscal stamp or central real-time approval of invoices; anti-fraud control is achieved through NTS cross-matching of issued versus received e-invoices, automatic reconciliation against customs export declarations, mandatory invoice serial numbers, cash receipt issuance for final consumers in designated industries, and strict refusal of input VAT recovery where e-invoice filing is omitted or mismatched. Foreign non-resident digital suppliers are required to maintain simplified VAT registration and issue digital receipts for Korean B2C supplies, with periodic returns and payment through HomeTax.

Export zero-rating is accepted only where the export declaration, foreign buyer or recipient, and foreign-currency repatriation form one consistent audit trail. During NTS or Korea Customs Service review, if the goods were not actually exported, the export declaration is fictitious or inflated, the foreign-currency proceeds are not repatriated by the statutory deadline, or the underlying service/royalty is found to be consumed in Korea, the NTS will recharacterize the supply as domestic taxable, assess VAT at 10% on the gross consideration, disallow the input VAT refund or offset, impose statutory additional taxes for understatement and dishonest tax evasion under the Framework Act on National Taxes and the Value-Added Tax Act, charge late-payment interest for the period from the original return due date, and make criminal referral under the Punishment of Tax Offenses Act for fraudulent export refund schemes. The clawback is retrospective and is not limited to the VAT year of export; the general assessment period is five years, extended to ten years in cases of tax fraud, and repeated violations can result in enhanced penalties and suspension of the entity's ability to claim zero-rated treatment.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

South Korea maintains a liberalized current account for genuine trade and service settlements, and the Korean won is freely convertible for bona fide commercial transactions through authorized foreign exchange banks. However, revenue-based financing and royalty financing transactions with a hybrid debt and equity nature are closely reviewed under capital account rules. Capital inflows and outflows are not banned, but they trigger structured reporting, registration, and purpose code obligations under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act and the Foreign Exchange Transactions Regulations. This assessment confirms that ordinary operating receipts and profit remittances are convertible, yet the first practical hurdle for a non-resident investor or fund is demonstrating the true beneficial ownership of the Chusik Hoesa and ensuring the financing instrument is not classified as a lending business requiring a regulatory license. The second hurdle is the strict documentary requirement for any capital contribution that is paid in KRW after conversion: the remitting bank, the receiving Korean tier-one bank, the corporate resolution, the source of wealth and the underlying royalty financing agreements must all be consistent and traceable. The third hurdle is the profit remittance process, which requires corporate tax clearance, the filing of relevant foreign exchange reports, and the presentation of a foreign exchange certificate issued by the bank. A 0% mandatory surrender rate applies to export proceeds; foreign currency receipts may be retained wholly in a Foreign Currency Deposit Account or converted into KRW when needed. The real constraints are therefore not convertibility limits but the evidential, tax withholding, and purpose coding discipline carried out under supervision of the Bank of Korea and the MOEF.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Under Foreign Exchange Transactions Regulations, export proceeds must be collected through an authorized foreign exchange bank within 18 months from the date of export declaration for goods, or by the contractual collection date for services and royalties. Mandatory conversion is 0%: 100% of export foreign currency may be retained in a Foreign Currency Deposit Account; conversion into KRW is optional and driven by liquidity needs. The receipt itself must be reported to the Foreign Exchange Information System and reconciled with customs declarations where applicable.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Korea (BOK); Foreign Exchange Administration Department of the Ministry of Economy and Finance (MOEF)

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Foreign digital EMIs are not legally permitted to service a Korean resident corporation as a primary transactional bank. Under the Korean Banking Act, only licensed banks may introduce deposits, operate won-denominated accounts, and execute won-for-foreign-currency conversions. A non-bank EMI, even if authorized in an EU or APAC jurisdiction, cannot clear KRW, cannot issue payment guarantees, and cannot provide a compliant domestic cash management rail. Its viability for a Korean treasury operation is therefore low: an EMI may only act as a supplemental foreign-currency collection conduit when paired with a tier-one Korean bank for final KRW conversion and settlement, and all receivables must still pass through the Korean bank's Foreign Exchange Information System reporting.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital injections must be routed via SWIFT MT103 to a dedicated Capital Escrow Account held with a licensed Korean tier-one bank. The remitter must include an appropriate capital transaction purpose code in the payment message, and the receiving bank will submit the transaction to the Bank of Korea Foreign Exchange Information System upon receipt. For paid-in equity capital, the investor must execute a share subscription, deposit payment into the escrow account, and obtain a foreign exchange registration certificate through the bank before issuance. An independent certified public accountant then performs capital verification against the foreign exchange purchase memorandum, the bank ledger, and the corporate resolutions, producing a capital contribution certificate that is filed with the Korean registration court. Any conversion of the foreign currency into KRW must be executed at a licensed bank and recorded with the exact purpose code for capital contribution.Corporation-to-corporation trade settlements, royalty remittances, and operating disbursements should execute on SWIFT MT103 for open-account transfers; SWIFT MT700 or MT760 for commercial letters of credit and standby letters of credit; and documentary collections through DP/DA procedures for supplier financing where performance security is not required. Domestic KRW payments should use the Korea Financial Telecommunications and Clearings Institute (KFTC) automated transfer rails through the tier-one bank's electronic banking product. All outbound foreign currency payments will carry the relevant Foreign Exchange Transactions Act purpose code, and payments exceeding the bank's threshold will be reported automatically to the Bank of Korea. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Foreign digital EMIs are not legally permitted to service a Korean resident corporation as a primary transactional bank. Under the Korean Banking Act, only licensed banks may introduce deposits, operate won-denominated accounts, and execute won-for-foreign-currency conversions. A non-bank EMI, even if authorized in an EU or APAC jurisdiction, cannot clear KRW, cannot issue payment guarantees, and cannot provide a compliant domestic cash management rail. Its viability for a Korean treasury operation is therefore low: an EMI may only act as a supplemental foreign-currency collection conduit when paired with a tier-one Korean bank for final KRW conversion and settlement, and all receivables must still pass through the Korean bank's Foreign Exchange Information System reporting.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

22% national corporate income tax on taxable income above KRW 20 billion, plus the 10% local income tax surcharge, producing a combined headline effective rate of 24.2%; progressive rates of 10% and 20% apply to lower brackets.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

22% (20% national withholding tax plus 2% local income tax surcharge) on dividends paid by a Korean Chusik Hoesa to a non-resident shareholder. The rate may be reduced under an applicable bilateral tax treaty (commonly 10%-15% for non-portfolio dividends), but full 22% must be withheld unless treaty relief is approved/claimed before payment.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: KRW 10,000,000,000 (KRW 10 billion) of aggregate international related-party transactions in the preceding fiscal year triggers mandatory preparation and submission of a Korean Local File/Master File-type transfer pricing report. Lower thresholds may apply outbound payments to tax-havens or certain financial arrangements.

Economic substance scope: South Korea does not apply Caribbean-style standalone Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) to a Chusik Hoesa; however, Korean tax law requires the place of effective management, central administration, and operations to be in Korea. The Corporate Tax Act and General Anti-Avoidance Rule under the International Tax Adjustment Act permit the NTS to disregard or recharacterize a shell company lacking real substance. For a cross-border royalty/financing fund, Core Income-Generating Activities must include strategic investment decisions, credit-risk management, funding contracts, and treasury function actually performed in Korea; otherwise the entity may be denied resident status, DTA benefits, or deductions for intercompany royalties/interest.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion by omission/underreporting of cross-border income

Statutory sanction: The Tax Evasion Punishment Act (Article 3(1)) imposes imprisonment for up to three years and/or a fine of up to five times the evaded tax (aggravated fraud thresholds can raise the maximum term). The NTS also levies a 40% false-return penalty and daily late-payment interest on the underpaid corporate income tax.

Director personal liability: A director or actual controller who ordered or participated in the evasion may be jointly and severally liable for company tax debts under Commercial Act Article 401 (liability to third parties) and may have personal bank accounts/assets attached. The director may be placed on the NTS overseas-travel restriction blacklist while tax arrears are outstanding and, upon criminal conviction, serves the imprisonment term personally; corporate veil piercing is applied through separate juristic personality exceptions where the company is used as a sham/agent for fraud.
Transfer pricing manipulation and missing documentation

Statutory sanction: The International Tax Adjustment Act Article 5 empowers the NTS to re-determine the arm's length price and re-calculate taxable income; Article 12-related sanctions impose a KRW 10 million unfilled-report penalty and up to 30% understatement penalty when TP documents are absent. If the falsification is willful, the Framework Act's 40% misconduct penalty applies on the shortfall.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign or approve knowingly false transfer pricing disclosures incur personal civil liability for the tax shortfall and interest under the Korean Commercial Act, and can be indicted for facilitating tax evasion. The NTS may also demand security, issue a departure ban, and use civil restitution to freeze personal assets where the corporate entity is a mere alter ego of the non-resident fund.
Shell company and AML/beneficial ownership abuse

Statutory sanction: Under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act and Specified Financial Transaction Information Act, non-disclosure of beneficial owners or conduct of finance-business-like activity without AML program registration subjects the entity and directors to fines and penal sanctions (commonly imprisonment up to five years and/or fines up to KRW 100 million). The tax authority additionally applies the Corporate Tax Act's anti-abuse/substance-over-form rule and denies the DTA reduction, exacting 22% WHT plus 40% fraud penalty and interest.

Director personal liability: Nominee directors cannot hide behind corporate limited liability when the company is a facade: Commercial Act Article 401 creates direct joint liability for damages caused by their willful or negligent unlawful conduct. Directors may be blacklisted and blocked from overseas travel pending tax or criminal resolution, and on conviction can be sentenced to imprisonment; personal bank accounts and real property are reachable for restitution of tax unpaid by the shell.
