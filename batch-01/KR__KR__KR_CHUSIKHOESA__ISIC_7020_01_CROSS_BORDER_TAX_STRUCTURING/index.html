A registered office in Korea must be a real physical location capable of receiving official mail and being inspected. Pure virtual offices and commercial mailboxes are generally rejected by the National Tax Service (NTS) for business registration and by regulated banks for corporate account opening in the tax advisory and cross-border consultancy sector. A serviced office with a dedicated private desk, physical signage, mail logs and staff presence may be accepted, but the NTS and KoFIU require full access to books, records and personnel. For ISIC 7020 tax structuring consultancy, a virtual office is high-risk and likely to be refused for value-added tax and anti-money laundering purposes.

Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

No statutory minimum paid-in capital applies to a Chusik Hoesa under the Korean Commercial Act. The articles of incorporation may fix any positive capital amount, and the entire subscription price must be paid in or deposited before the incorporation meeting is held. No blocked escrow account is required by law, but the subscriber or promoter must place the subscription money in a Korean bank or securities company account in the name of the company or the promotor, and a bank certificate of deposit must be produced to the registration court. For non-resident shareholders, the foreign capital contribution must additionally comply with the reporting regime under the Foreign Investment Promotion Act through a designated foreign exchange bank or KOTRA.

The company must, however, comply with the Occupational Safety and Health Act risk assessment duties, register as an employer with the Korea Workers' Compensation and Welfare Service, and ensure the leased office building has a valid fire safety inspection certificate from the local fire authority.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

National Tax Service (NTS) of the Republic of Korea; online electronic portal: HomeTax (hometax.go.kr); customs clearance authority for exports: Korea Customs Service (customs.go.kr)

E-government filing portal

Step 1: Complete incorporation of the Chusik Hoesa by registering with the competent Korean district court registry office (Commercial Registration) and obtaining the corporate registration certificate. Step 2: Within 20 days of business commencement, apply for Business Registration with the competent NTS district tax office through HomeTax or in person, using the Business Registration Application under Article 8 of the Korean Value-Added Tax Act. Required documents for a foreign-owned corporation include the corporate registration certificate, articles of incorporation, proof of the registered place of business (lease agreement or ownership document), identification and power-of-attorney documents for the responsible representative, and, where applicable, Alien Registration Card or foreign director identification. Step 3: NTS will issue a unified 10-digit Business Registration Number (BRN, also known as Saupja Deungnok Beonho) which functions simultaneously as the corporate income tax TIN and the VAT registration ID; no separate VAT ID is required. Step 4: If the foreign non-resident entity itself maintains a domestic place of business or branch in Korea, the foreign corporation must separately register that domestic place of business under the Corporate Tax Act with the same competent tax office; a mere non-resident shareholder of the Chusik Hoesa has no direct NTS registration obligation unless it creates a Korean permanent establishment or taxable Korean-source activity.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

None. The Republic of Korea does not operate a U.S.-style check-the-box election, and a Chusik Hoesa is an entity-level taxpayer that cannot elect pass-through or fiscally transparent treatment. Qualifying parent-subsidiary groups may elect consolidated corporate tax filing for wholly owned domestic subsidiaries under the Corporate Tax Act, but this is a group filing election, not an entity classification election. Hybrid mismatch outcomes are resolved by mandatory statutory rules under Korean tax law and are not elective.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

10%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

Not applicable to a Chusik Hoesa; all corporate business operators must VAT-register from business commencement regardless of turnover. The simplified taxable person regime, with an annual revenue threshold of KRW 80 million, is available only to eligible individual small businesses and cannot be used by a Chusik Hoesa.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under Article 21(1)(1) of the Value-Added Tax Act, 0% VAT applies to exported goods where the goods are sold to a foreign buyer and physically leave the Republic of Korea. Under Article 21(1)(2) of the Value-Added Tax Act and Enforcement Decree Article 24, 0% VAT applies to services supplied to a non-resident or foreign enterprise where the service recipient is outside Korea, the service is intended for use outside Korea, and the service is not supplied in connection with a Korean permanent establishment or Korean-domestic use. The Chusik Hoesa cross-border tax structuring and transfer pricing advisory scope is eligible for 0% VAT when the recipient is a non-resident enterprise, the advisory deliverables are consumed outside Korea (or only used to manage non-Korean risks and transactions), and the service is not effectively connected with a Korean permanent establishment. The zero-rating must be substantiated by contemporaneous contractual, performance, and foreign-currency settlement evidence.

  • Customs Export Declaration and Export Declaration Receipt (Export Bill of Entry) issued by Korea Customs Service evidencing physical departure of goods from Korea
  • Commercial invoice and packing list
  • Bill of Lading for sea freight or Air Waybill for air freight evidencing international transport
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (FIRC) or Foreign Exchange Receipt Confirmation issued by a Korean Foreign Exchange Bank evidencing receipt of the export proceeds in foreign currency
  • For zero-rated advisory services: signed cross-border advisory contract, detailed scope and deliverable report, statement or certificate from the non-resident recipient confirming place of use outside Korea, zero-rated electronic tax invoice, and FIRC or comparable foreign-exchange receipt evidence; customs declarations are not applicable for services

All VAT-registered enterprises, including a Chusik Hoesa, are required to issue electronic tax invoices (e-tax invoices) for taxable supplies. E-tax invoices must be transmitted to NTS through HomeTax, the Electronic Business Platform, or NTS-certified ERP API and machine-to-machine interfaces, no later than the day after issuance. The Korean system is central invoice reporting and validation rather than real-time fiscal clearance; NTS validates the supplier and recipient Business Registration Numbers and uses a central e-invoice repository with risk-scoring analytics. There is no anti-fraud digital stamp, but NTS authentication uses HomeTax credentials and public-key digital certificates for secure issuance and transmission.

NTS and Korea Customs Service conduct post-export audits, cross-border information exchange, and documentation verification for zero-rated supplies. If the required export declaration, Bill of Lading, FIRC, foreign-currency receipt evidence, or service-use documentation is missing, inconsistent, or non-compliant with the 180-day foreign exchange settlement deadline, NTS may recharacterize the zero-rated supply as a taxable domestic supply and assess unpaid 10% VAT, additional tax liabilities generally ranging from 10% to 40% of the underreported or disallowed VAT depending on negligence or intentionality, monthly statutory interest, and penalties. Materially false tax invoices, fraudulent export zero-rating claims, or deliberate VAT evasion additionally expose responsible officers to criminal sanctions under the Punishment of Tax Offenses Act, including imprisonment up to three years and fines up to three times the evaded tax amount.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

South Korea operates an advanced current-account convertibility system under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act. Ordinary operational inflows and outflows for consultancy services, royalties, dividends, salaries and trade settlements are free-standing, meaning a Korean Chusik Hoesa may remit or receive funds promptly through any licensed domestic foreign-exchange bank using the required FEIS purpose code and supporting documentation. Capital-account items such as equity injection, cross-border standby loans, securities purchases, and merger-related payments are not fully convertible without filing or licence; the regulator primarily relies on a negative-list model under which prohibited or restricted transactions require prior MOEF/BOK approval. Profit remittance from the Korean entity is considered current-account once the underlying earnings are realised and taxes are withheld, but foreign investors must also maintain their Foreign Investment Notice registration if they rely on FIPA rights. In practice, KRW is deliverable onshore, and non-resident KRW accounts are restricted to trade and employment purposes. The outstanding failure risk is not convertibility itself but documentary classification; a tax-structuring consultancy must pre-approve purpose codes and retain contracts, invoices, and board minutes to avoid FEIS rejection.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act and BOK guidelines, exporters must cause all foreign currency export proceeds to be received no later than six months from the shipment date, although an extension to twelve months may be requested for sound transactional reasons. Once received, the proceeds must be channelled into a domestic foreign-currency account at a delegated foreign-exchange bank; there is no mandatory percentage surrender requirement, so the exporter may retain 100% of receipts in that account. If conversion to KRW is required, the bank will execute at market rate. The underlying proof set includes the customs export declaration, commercial invoice, transport document and a BOK FEIS settlement report submitted by the bank.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Korea (BOK) as the monetary and foreign-exchange authority under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act, with foreign-exchange administration exercised by the Ministry of Economy and Finance (MOEF) through delegated foreign-exchange banks and Korean Customs Service reporting.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low viability as a primary banking rail. Offshore digital EMIs without a Korean banking licence cannot open KRW IBAN-equivalent current accounts or serve as settlement banks for a resident Chusik Hoesa. Legal barriers include the Electronic Financial Transactions Act and Financial Supervisory Service licensing. Such EMIs may only act as a payment interface supported by a licensed domestic partner bank, not as a corporate treasury account or capital escrow provider. Any usage is limited to small vendor payments and must funnel into an onshore bank account to remain compliant.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Non-resident shareholders must first open a designated foreign-currency corporate account at the selected Korean bank, submit a Foreign Investment Declaration to either KOTRA or the delegated bank under the Foreign Investment Promotion Act, and then remit equity funds via SWIFT MT103 or MT202 using the correct FEIS purpose code, such as 110100 classification for equity contributions, with a detailed payment reason identifying the capital injection rather than a generic current transfer. The bank performs capital verifification and issues a Foreign Investment Notification; the external auditors use the bank certificate to verify subscribed share capital and register the inflow with FEIS in order to support future dividend and exit remittances.Corporate treasury should route outbound fee and trade settlements using SWIFT MT103 for direct payments to non-resident beneficiaries, complemented by SWIFT MT202 for large interbank cover payments. For goods movement, use Commercial Letters of Credit issued by Korean banks through correspondent networks, while Documentary Collections serve for shipments where the counterparty accepts document-as-acceptance basis. Every outbound transfer requires an underlying contract or invoice, a specific FEIS purpose code for consultancy or trade, and proof of withholding tax if the beneficiary is rendering taxable services in Korea. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

25% (progressive: 10% up to KRW 200M; 20% above KRW 200M to KRW 20B; 22% above KRW 20B to KRW 300B; 25% above KRW 300B) plus 10% local income tax surtax, resulting in a top aggregate effective rate of 27.5%.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

20% (15% corporate income tax plus 2% local income tax on the dividend income for non-resident shareholders; for a non-resident entity, a 20% final withholding tax applies unless a treaty reduces it). Additional 10% of the dividend amount may be withheld as surtax if the dividend is an income-sourced in Korea.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

For the fiscal year ending December 31, the corporate income tax return must be filed and paid within 3 months from the fiscal year-end (i.e., by March 31 of the following year). For non-calendar year-end entities, within 3 months after each fiscal year-end. If the deadline falls on a non-business day, it is extended to the next business day. A written extension of up to 1 month may be granted upon application, provided the NTS approves (limited to certain conditions, e.g., overseas business).

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Mandatory Transfer Pricing (TP) documentation thresholds per the Law for Coordination of International Tax Affairs (LCITA): Local File (per-entity) must be prepared when intercompany transactions with foreign related parties exceed KRW 10 billion for services/goods/year (or KRW 10 billion for all intercompany transaction categories combined). Master File required when the consolidated group revenue exceeds KRW 100 billion and intercompany foreign transactions exceed KRW 10 billion. Simplified documentation thresholds may apply for transactions under KRW 5 billion per counterparty, but a written rationale is still recommended. Thresholds are per each cross-border related-party transaction category (sales, purchases, loans, intangibles, services).

Economic substance scope: Korea does not have a formal 'Economic Substance Regulation' (ESR) as in offshore jurisdictions, but the doctrine is embedded in the corporate tax general anti-abuse rules (Article 52 of the Corporate Tax Act) and the CFC rules (Article 63 of LCITA). For a Korean Chusik Hoesa, the 'substance over form' test requires that the company maintains real business premises, employees, contract sign-off authority, bank accounts, and management functions in Korea. For a holding or advisory entity, the Core Income-Generating Activities (CIGA) must be performed in Korea – not merely receiving invoices and issuing receipts. The NTS applies the 'economic ownership' principle to deny treaty benefits or tax deductions to a Korean entity that is a shell or lacks the capacity to perform the consulting services. The OECD's BEPS Action 6 and Korea's treaty abuse limitation on benefits (LOB) provisions demand that the entity demonstrates active business and real value creation. If substance is insufficient, the NTS may deem the Korean entity to be a 'paper company,' recharacterize income, and levy a 40% penalty plus liability upto the personal assets of the directors.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion through Artificial Shell Structuring (Piercing Corporate Veil under Corporate Tax Act Art. 52 and Criminal Act Art. 347)

Statutory sanction: The NTS recharacterizes the transactions, disallows deductions, imposes 40% penalties on the underreported income, and charges up to KRW 200M in additional taxes. Criminal referral for intentional fraudulent tax evasion under Article 128 of the Corporate Tax Act and Article 347 of the Criminal Act (Fraud).

Director personal liability: The National Tax Collection Act (Article 28) allows the NTS to pierce the corporate veil and impose secondary tax liability on a director who was grossly negligent or abusively used the corporation as a conduit for tax evasion. The director's bank accounts may be frozen, and a travel ban ('blacklisting') is issued through the NTS and Immigration Service to prevent flight. Criminal conviction for tax evasion under the Punishment of Tax Evasion Act (Article 9) carries imprisonment up to 5 years and fines up to 3 times the tax evaded. The director's personal assets, including the residence, are subject to civil restitution orders to recover the unpaid tax.
Transfer Pricing Fraud and Concealment of Intercompany Transactions

Statutory sanction: The NTS adjusts the transfer price to arm's-length, imposes an income adjustment, and adds a 40% negligence penalty on the underreported amount (under Article 3 of the Law for the Coordination of International Tax Affairs). The CPA who signed the false documentation may be referred to the regulatory authorities, and the firm's license suspended. If the adjustment exceeds KRW 50 billion, the case is automatically referred to the Public Prosecutors' Office.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly signed the false TP documentation are personally liable for the unpaid corporate tax under the doctrine of 'piercing the corporate veil' in the National Tax Collection Act and the Corporate Tax Act. Criminal indictment under Article 347 of the Criminal Act can lead to imprisonment up to 10 years if the fraud amount is extremely high. A restraining order is placed on their assets, and a travel ban is issued preventing them from leaving Korea. Courts have awarded restitution to the NTS for the entire tax, penalties, and interest out of the director's personal property.
