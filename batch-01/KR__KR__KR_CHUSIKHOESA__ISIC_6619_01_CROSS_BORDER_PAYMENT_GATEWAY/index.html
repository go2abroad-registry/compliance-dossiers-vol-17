Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

National Tax Service (NTS) of the Republic of Korea; electronic portal: HomeTax (www.hometax.go.kr).

E-government filing portal

For a domestic Chusik Hoesa, business registration must be filed with the NTS within 20 days from business commencement. For a foreign non-resident entity establishing Korean VAT or corporate tax obligations, the foreign company or its appointed tax representative files a Business Registration Application through HomeTax or at the district tax office having jurisdiction over the domestic place of business. Supporting documents include the company's certificate of incorporation, articles of association or bylaws, board resolution appointing the Korean or tax representative, lease or ownership evidence for the fixed domestic business premises, and an apostilled or consular-legalized home-country registration certificate plus the responsible officer's passport. Upon validation, NTS issues the Business Registration Certificate containing a 10-digit Business Registration Number that simultaneously operates as the corporate TIN and VAT registration number. A foreign non-resident supplier of electronic or digital services without a domestic fixed place of business must register under the VAT Act's simplified non-resident registration procedure and must appoint a tax representative; NTS then issues a non-resident VAT registration number. Separately, for export and import physical goods movements, the entity must register with the Korea Customs Service to obtain a customs importer/exporter registration identifier.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

None. South Korea does not provide a check-the-box pass-through election or a general hybrid classification election; a Chusik Hoesa is per se subject to entity-level Korean corporate income tax. The Korean Corporate Tax Act's consolidated taxation for eligible domestic groups is a separate elective filing regime, not an entity classification election.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

10%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

There is no general monetary turnover threshold for VAT registration; every person making taxable supplies in Korea must register from the first taxable transaction. Domestic small suppliers whose annual taxable turnover is not more than KRW 80,000,000 may use the simplified VAT taxpayer regime, which affects return filing and calculation but does not defer or remove the VAT registration obligation. Foreign non-resident suppliers of digital and electronic services to Korean consumers are required to register under the non-resident electronic service supply rules when Korean B2C supplies can be reasonably expected.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rated exports are available under the Korean VAT Act for goods physically exported from Korea and for qualifying services supplied to non-resident recipients and used outside Korea. For goods, title and risk must pass to a foreign buyer, the goods must be cleared through Korea Customs Service as exports, and the outward movement must be supported by shipping documents. For the cross-border payment gateway service, zero-rating is available if the recipient is a non-resident merchant, acquirer, or payment institution, the service is performed in Korea but is not consumed or utilized in Korea, the recipient has no domestic place of business, and the consideration is settled in foreign currency through Korean foreign exchange reporting channels. The service is taxable at 10% where it is effectively supplied for Korean domestic merchants or Korean cardholder settlement activities that lack a genuine overseas supply character.

  • Export Declaration Certificate (export clearance confirmation issued by the Korea Customs Service)
  • Commercial invoice and packing list
  • Bill of Lading or sea/air waybill evidencing physical export movement
  • Cross-border payment gateway service contract or merchant agreement showing the non-resident recipient and offshore usage of the service
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or foreign currency receipt confirmation issued by a Korean foreign exchange bank evidencing repatriation of export proceeds

The NTS operates the mandatory HomeTax electronic tax invoice (e-Tax Invoice) system. Every VAT-registered business must issue tax invoices electronically through HomeTax, either via the HomeTax web portal or via the NTS-certified API channel. E-Tax Invoice data is transmitted to NTS at the time of issuance, operating as real-time fiscal reporting and generating the NTS-controlled invoice number and digital integrity markers. There is no independent private fiscal stamping vendor; the HomeTax e-Tax Invoice number and validation chain constitute the anti-fraud control. For consumer B2C transactions, suppliers must also issue cash receipts through the HomeTax cash-receipt system where a consumer requests one or where the transaction is mandatory under the cash-receipt rules. Late issuance or failure to transmit e-Tax Invoices may trigger VAT penalty surcharges under the VAT Act.

The NTS will retrospectively reverse zero-rating and claw back the VAT benefit if the export declaration, bill of lading, service contract, or foreign-exchange receipt documentation is missing, false, late, or internally inconsistent. The company would then be assessed standard VAT at 10% on the full supply value plus statutory penalty surcharges and interest. Penalty surcharges apply under the VAT Act for failure to register, failure to issue an e-Tax Invoice, issuance of false tax invoices, and failure to report the taxable export; aggregate penalty surcharges can reach up to 30%-40% of the tax involved in severe cases. Fraudulent zero-rating or refund claims are additionally prosecuted under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act, carrying criminal fines and imprisonment, and the statutory retroactive assessment period for fraud is ten years.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Korean won is fully convertible for current-account transactions, while capital-account transactions are governed by a declaration and monitoring regime under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act. Merchant acquiring, cross-border digital content settlement, and online payment gateway service fees are current-account services when supported by genuine commercial contracts, invoices, and SRV-related documentation; these flows may be freely converted into KRW at market rates through a licensed domestic foreign exchange bank. Inbound equity and loan capital into a Chusik Hoesa requires a formal foreign investment report and registration procedure before the funds can be recognised as verified paid-in capital; the remittance must pass through a Korean foreign exchange bank with the correct inward remittance purpose code. Dividend remittances to non-resident shareholders are permitted after tax withholding, using the applicable treaty rate, but the bank will require evidence of audited retained earnings, the foreign investment registration certificate, and tax clearance. There is no general mandatory surrender of export proceeds to KRW; export receipts may be retained in foreign currency accounts for the statutory settlement window. Non-resident fintech companies and unlicensed offshore EMIs cannot access the Korean won clearing system, and all KRW settlement must occur through domestic banks interconnected with the Korea Financial Telecommunications and Clearings Institute (KFTC).

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no mandatory conversion ratio for export proceeds under current Korean foreign exchange rules; exporters may lawfully hold receivables in foreign currency accounts. However, the statutory settlement requirement dictates that export goods or digital export service earnings must be collected and settled through a licensed domestic foreign exchange bank within 12 months from the date of export or service performance, unless an extension is approved by the relevant bank. The exporter must file transaction reports and retain the underlying commercial invoices, waybills, contracts, and payment receipts for BOK or MOEF examination. For PSP cross-border collections, each receipt should be reconcilable to an underlying merchant transaction record, with the foreign exchange bank issuing a purchase/sale confirmation certificate for each conversion.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Korea (BOK), acting with the Ministry of Economy and Finance (MOEF) as the foreign exchange administrator under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act, and with the Financial Services Commission (FSC) as the licensing authority for electronic payment service providers.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Offshore digital EMIs such as global payment platforms are not viable as the primary banking layer for a Korean-domiciled Chusik Hoesa. An offshore EMI is not licensed by the Financial Services Commission under the Electronic Financial Transactions Act or the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act, and it cannot directly access KRW clearing, issue foreign exchange certifications, or perform the statutory reporting functions of a Korean foreign exchange bank. The Korean payment gateway must maintain its operating, escrow, and settlement accounts at a domestic Tier-1 commercial bank connected to KFTC, with cross-border flows settled through SWIFT/CHIPS/Fedwire. An EMI may only be used as a supplementary receive-only wallet for selected overseas merchant collections, subject to the ultimate clearance of funds through a licensed domestic bank; it cannot act as the compliance anchor for Korean foreign exchange regulation.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound equity and shareholder loan funding must be routed by SWIFT MT103 to a foreign currency bank account in the name of the Chusik Hoesa or the foreign investor at a licensed Korean foreign exchange bank. The remitter must include the Korean entity corporate registration number and the approved foreign exchange purpose code for inward direct investment or equity contribution. The receiving bank will verify the remittance, issue a foreign exchange purchase or capital deposit certificate, and require the prescribed foreign investment report to be filed with MOEF through KOTRA or the delegated foreign exchange bank. Once the bank confirms the capital deposit and the Commercial Court issues the final corporate register amendment, the escrowed capital can be converted into KRW and moved to the operational account. Capital verification in Korea requires the deposit of cash, the issuing bank's confirmation, and a statutory auditor review where required; the foreign investor must retain the foreign investment registration certificate for all future dividend remittances and capital return purposes.For physical goods-related payments, the company may use SWIFT MT103 for simple wire settlements, commercial letters of credit for supplier credit, and documentary collections where the merchant requires transport-document-linked payment. For digital content licensing, software subscription, and cross-border payment gateway service fees, the dominant compliant clearing rail is SWIFT MT103 through the domestic foreign exchange bank, supported by service agreements, invoices, and tax withholding certificates where applicable. USD clearing in the United States will be completed via Fedwire or CHIPS through a correspondent account of the Korean bank, while Visa and Mastercard settlement flows come into the acquiring account through the card network and are then transferred to the operating account after merchant fees and FX conversion. 22% (20% national dividend withholding tax under Article 98 of the Corporate Tax Act plus 2% local income tax surtax under the Local Tax Act), subject to reduction under an applicable tax treaty.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: KRW 10,000,000,000 (KRW 10 billion) in cross-border transactions with foreign related parties in a single business year triggers mandatory preparation of simultaneous transfer pricing documentation, including the Local File and the integrated Report on International Transactions; the Master File is required when the group meets the relevant consolidated revenue threshold under Article 11 of the Law for Coordination of International Tax Affairs, and Country-by-Country Reporting is required for multinational groups with consolidated revenue of at least KRW 1,000,000,000,000 (KRW 1 trillion).

Economic substance scope: Korea does not enforce an annual Economic Substance Regulation filing like certain zero-tax jurisdictions, but the National Tax Service and Korean courts apply a substance-over-form doctrine, the general anti-abuse rule under Article 17 of the Framework Act on National Taxes, and the OECD MLI standards; a Chusik Hoesa operating a cross-border payment gateway must demonstrate Core Income-Generating Activities in Korea, including bank account management, merchant on-boarding, transaction monitoring, risk control, board meetings and operational employees; a shell entity with no Korean decision-making or operational substance will be denied treaty benefits and may be directly recharacterized as an agent or conduit of its foreign parent.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Failure to Withhold Cross-Border Payments

Statutory sanction: Punishment of Tax Evaders Act Article 3(1) imposes imprisonment of up to 3 years or a fine of up to 30 times the evaded tax; the Framework Act on National Taxes adds a 40% fraudulent under-reporting penalty plus a 0.03% daily late-payment surcharge on unpaid tax; the National Tax Service may additionally impose penalties for failure to file withholding schedules.

Director personal liability: The corporate veil may be pierced when a director uses the Chusik Hoesa as a facade for fraud; under Article 39 of the National Tax Collection Act a director may become a secondary taxpayer and be personally liable for unpaid corporate tax and withholding tax if corporate assets are insufficient; directors may face criminal imprisonment for intentional false filing, financial restitution to the tax authority, and a travel ban or departure restriction imposed by the National Tax Service.
Unlicensed Payment Gateway Operation and AML Violations

Statutory sanction: Article 49 of the Electronic Financial Transactions Act penalizes unregistered electronic financial business with imprisonment of up to 5 years or a fine of up to KRW 50,000,000; the Financial Information Act imposes imprisonment of up to 3 years or a fine of up to KRW 30,000,000 for willful AML failures; the Financial Intelligence Unit may impose administrative fines, revoke authorizations, and refer the case to prosecutors.

Director personal liability: Directors who direct or knowingly permit unlicensed PSP operations are not protected by limited liability; they are jointly and severally liable for restitution of merchant and consumer funds, can be blacklisted by the FIU and Financial Supervisory Service, may face travel bans, and can be convicted of criminal AML offenses with actual imprisonment.
