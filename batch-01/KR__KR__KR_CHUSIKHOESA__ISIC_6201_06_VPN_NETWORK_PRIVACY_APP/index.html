Statutory Formation & Board Resolutions

Competent registry, governing statute, capital lockup rules, and first board resolution template.

Competent corporate registry

Supreme Court Registry Office of the Republic of Korea (대법원 등기국) through the local Korean Commercial Registry Court (상업등기소) having territorial jurisdiction over the registered office; administrative online filings are processed via the Korean Supreme Court 'I-RO' (Internet Registry Office) system. For foreign investment-related notifications and tax incentives, the competent authority is the Korea Trade-Investment Promotion Agency (KOTRA) Invest Korea and the Ministry of Trade, Industry and Energy (MOTIE).

Governing statute

Commercial Act of South Korea (상법), particularly Articles 288 through 328 (incorporation, articles of incorporation, capital, shares, and promoters), Article 289 (articles of incorporation execution), Article 292 (registration of incorporation), Articles 312-316 (subscription and payment of shares), Article 317 (registration of incorporation), and Article 363 (first general meeting provisions). Furthermore, the Foreign Investment Promotion Act (FIPA), Articles 2, 4, 5, and 6, governs foreign-invested stock corporations and the required Foreign Investment Report.

Formation / constitutional filings

Certificate of Incorporation Registration (주식회사 설립등기신청서) together with the Articles of Incorporation (정관), and, for foreign investors, the Foreign Investment Report Certificate (외국인투자신고확인증) filed under the Foreign Investment Promotion Act.

Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

The Commercial Act of South Korea imposes no statutory minimum paid-up capital for a Chusik Hoesa; however, the Act requires that the total issue price of subscribed shares be paid in full no later than six months after the closing date of subscription, and prohibits payment by set-off in kind unless approved by the court-appointed inspector where required. For a foreign-invested company, the Foreign Investment Promotion Act requires a Foreign Investment Report to be filed before the share subscription; a remittance of the consideration must be made through a foreign exchange bank, and a deposit or escrow receipt issued by the bank must be attached to the registration application. The paid-up capital may be set at KRW 100 million (approximately USD 75,000) as a practical bank account and licensing threshold, but no blocked escrow account is legally mandated by the Commercial Act.

Funding & unlock path: Not Applicable at site level: because the company does not operate a physical production facility, no Civil Defense fire safety certificate for industrial premises, structural load inspection, or workplace safety installation approval is required for manufacturing. However, the registered office must be a legally occupied business premises that satisfies ordinary building code fire safety requirements and maintains basic workplace safety compliance under the Occupational Safety and Health Act for any non-industrial office staff; no separate industrial fire permit is required.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

National Tax Service of the Republic of Korea (NTS); online portal HomeTax (https://www.hometax.go.kr), with Korea Customs Service under the Ministry of Economy and Finance operating the UNI-PASS export declaration system.

E-government filing portal

A wholly domestic Chusik Hoesa must apply for a business registration number with the NTS district tax office having jurisdiction over its Korean place of business within 20 days from commencement of business. The application is made on the NTS business registration form, together with the articles of incorporation, corporate certificate of registration, three-year business plan or lease contract of the Korean registered office, and identification of the representative; NTS issues the 11-digit business registration number, which is simultaneously the corporate tax TIN and the VAT registration number. For a foreign non-resident entity without a Korean place of business that supplies VPN/network privacy app electronic services to Korean consumers, the foreign entity must appoint a Korean tax representative (Korean certified tax accountant or attorney) and file a simplified VAT registration application with the NTS if its Korean electronic-service turnover is expected to exceed the statutory threshold of KRW 30,000,000 per annual taxable period. The application must attach the foreign certificate of incorporation, board resolution, certificate of representative, power of attorney to the local tax representative, and the contract or platform evidence showing Korean-sourced electronic supplies; NTS then issues the same 11-digit business registration number for VAT filing and zero-rating compliance. Foreign shareholders of the Chusik Hoesa are not separately required to obtain Korean TINs unless they are engaged in their own Korean taxable business or have Korean-source taxable activities.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

None. The Republic of Korea does not provide check-the-box elections or hybrid-mismatch transparency elections for Korean Chusik Hoesa corporations. A Chusik Hoesa is mandatorily taxed as a resident entity under the Corporate Tax Act and is not eligible for pass-through classification.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

10%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

For a domestic Chusik Hoesa there is no monetary turnover threshold: VAT registration is mandatory upon commencing any taxable business in Korea. For a non-resident foreign supplier of electronic services to Korean consumers, mandatory VAT registration is triggered when annual Korean electronic-service supplies exceed the statutory threshold under the Value-Added Tax Act and Presidential Decree, currently KRW 30,000,000; below that threshold registration is voluntary rather than mandatory.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A Korean VAT-registered business may export goods with a 0% VAT rate where the goods are shipped from Korea under a customs export declaration and consideration is earned in foreign currency from the foreign buyer. VPN/network privacy software-as-a-service and electronic digital content may be zero-rated as an overseas supply when the customer is a foreign non-resident recipient, the service is delivered and consumed outside Korea, and the subscription fee is received from abroad in foreign currency. The exporter must maintain evidence of conclusion of contract, service delivery, and actual inward foreign exchange remittance; only these verified supplies may be recorded as zero-rated rather than standard-rated supplies in the quarterly VAT return. Excess input VAT connected with zero-rated exports is refundable after NTS documentary verification.

  • Customs Export Declaration issued by Korea Customs Service through UNI-PASS (the Korean equivalent of the Bill of Entry/export declaration)
  • Ocean Bill of Lading or Air Waybill in the name of the foreign buyer; for digital/SaaS/VPN app supplies, an electronic delivery and usage record evidencing foreign subscriber consumption
  • Commercial invoice and signed contract / purchase order
  • Inward Remittance Certificate / Foreign Exchange Certificate issued by a Korean licensed foreign exchange bank evidencing the foreign currency collection
  • Packing list, export insurance or other trade documents necessary to prove the physical export
  • NTS zero-rated export supporting schedule / export performance confirmation when requested during audit

Korea operates a mandatory electronic tax invoice system under which every VAT-registered business must issue an e-tax invoice through the NTS HomeTax e-Tax invoice system or an NTS-approved service provider for taxable business-to-business supplies. The invoice data is submitted in near-real time via XML, API, or EDI interfaces and is centrally matched by the NTS against VAT returns, corporate tax returns, cash receipt and credit card sales data, and customs export declarations. There is no physical fiscal stamp or pre-approval clearance; the fiscal clearance and anti-fraud control is achieved through mandatory e-tax invoice transmission, sequential invoice validation, and administrative penalties for non-issuance or false e-tax invoice issuance.

The NTS will compare zero-rated return claims with UNI-PASS customs declarations, e-tax invoice transmissions, and foreign exchange bank reports. If the required customs, delivery, contract, or foreign-currency remittance documentation is missing or inconsistent, or if export consideration is not repatriated within 180 days, the zero rating is denied with retrospective effect, the supply is reclassified as a standard-rated Korean supply, and 10% VAT is assessed, together with penalty taxes under the Framework Act on National Taxes and the Value-Added Tax Act. The exposure includes non-payment/under-reporting penalties of up to 40% of the unpaid VAT, false e-tax invoice penalties, and criminal prosecution under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act for fraudulent export zero-rating claims, plus statutory interest on all clawed-back amounts.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

South Korea maintains a largely liberalized current-account regime. Payments for software subscriptions, license royalties, VPN access fees, and data-processing services are generally treated as invisible current transactions and may be remitted freely through licensed foreign exchange banks, provided the underlying purpose is truthfully disclosed and the remittance is reported where applicable. Capital-account transactions, by contrast, remain subject to a rule-based declaratory system under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act: equity contributions, offshore loans, deposits, and guarantees require prior declaration, though not necessarily administrative approval, and any unauthorized cross-border capital flow may be investigated by MOEF and BOK. For a foreign-owned Korean Chusik Hoesa, inbound capital is additionally governed by the Foreign Investment Promotion Act (FIPA), which requires a foreign-investment notification and a foreign-investment registration certificate before the remittance is booked as share capital. Dividends, branch remittances, and liquidation proceeds are convertible only after corporate tax and withholding tax clearance; a bank will require a formal dividend resolution, audited financial statements, tax payment certificates, and evidence that the company is not delinquent on Korean statutory contributions. The Korean won is not an internationally delivered currency in the general sense; onshore settlement occurs in KRW, and export proceeds in foreign currency may be retained in designated foreign-exchange accounts or converted at market rates without a fixed mandatory surrender ratio. However, every transaction above the statutory reporting threshold must be transmitted to FEIS by the handling bank. Overall, convertibility is high for bona fide operating cash flows, but it is a documentary discipline rather than an unfettered freedom, and compliance failures can result in administrative penalties, retroactive tax reassessment, and deferred remittance.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act and its Enforcement Decree, export proceeds—including software licensing and VPN subscription revenue invoiced to non-residents—must be collected within six months from the shipment or service-provision date through a licensed foreign exchange bank. The invoiced proceeds must be credited to the exporter's foreign-currency account or sold to the bank; there is no fixed statutory conversion percentage imposed on the date of collection. Instead, the key legal requirement is timely collection, accurate reporting, and retention in a permitted account. Any conversion of retained foreign currency into KRW must be executed at prevailing market rates with a licensed bank, and the bank must file the transaction to FEIS. If proceeds are kept beyond the statutory collection period or remain unusually unused, the entity must submit an extension or explanation to the bank and, if required, to MOEF/BOK. For VPN services settled through Stripe or Paymentwall, the gateway settlement in USD or EUR is treated as service-export revenue and should be reconciled against the underlying merchant agreement, invoicing module, and subscriber data so that the bank can confirm the flow is trade-related rather than an unlicensed money transmission activity.

Central bank supervision: The Bank of Korea (BOK), acting as the central bank and foreign-exchange market supervisor, together with the Ministry of Economy and Finance (MOEF) under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act; day-to-day transaction reporting is collected through the Foreign Exchange Information System (FEIS) operating via licensed foreign exchange banks.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Direct offshore EMI viability is very low. An offshore digital EMI is not licensed in Korea under the Electronic Financial Transactions Act or the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act and therefore cannot open resident KRW settlement accounts, cannot connect to the BOK interbank payment rails, and cannot act as a licensed cross-border FX intermediary for the Chusik Hoesa. Such entities may be legally barred from providing deposit-taking or payment-account services in Korea, and Korean commercial banks will generally reject an onboarding package that relies on an unlicensed EMI for inbound share capital, subscription revenue settlement, or dividend repatriation. Stripe and Paymentwall can be used only as merchant acquirers and collect-on-behalf-of platforms; the final clearing and FX conversion must land in a domestic Tier-1 bank account in the Korean entity's name. Attempts to route operational subscriptions through an unlicensed international wallet or EMI structure will trigger AML suspicion, foreign-exchange offense risk, and possible violation of Korea's real-name financial transaction framework. The treasury architecture should therefore treat Stripe and Paymentwall as front-end payment gateways only and anchor all primary liquidity in a licensed Korean bank.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Mandatory Local File and Master File are triggered when annual foreign related-party transactions equal or exceed KRW 5 billion; consolidated multinational group revenue of KRW 1 trillion or more triggers a Country-by-Country Report; separate reporting and lower compliance thresholds apply to transactions with comprehensive tax-haven jurisdictions.

Economic substance scope: Korea does not operate a UAE-style Economic Substance Regulations filing regime, but the National Tax Service applies a statutory substance-over-form doctrine and the arm's-length principle under the Adjustment of International Taxes Act. For a VPN/privacy-app Korean operating entity, core income-generating activities include server and network administration, encryption software development, user contracting, access provisioning, payment and AML compliance, and local decision-making. These Korean functions, assets, and risks must support the reported income. Paper-only boards, foreign-resident decision-makers, and uncontrolled foreign servers weaken the Korean CIGA-equivalent substance, create PE exposure for foreign counterparties, and trigger anti-abuse denial of DTA benefits or controlled foreign corporation reattribution for Korean shareholders.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Corporate Income Tax Return

Statutory sanction: Punishment of Tax Evaders Act Article 3: imprisonment up to 3 years or a fine up to twice the evaded national tax; where evaded tax exceeds KRW 500 million, aggravated imprisonment up to 10 years and a fine up to three times the tax evaded. The Basic National Tax Act imposes an additional tax of 20%-40% of the under-reported or unpaid tax and daily late-payment surcharges.

Director personal liability: The representative director is personally secondarily liable under Basic National Tax Act Article 39 when corporate assets are insufficient and the director willfully failed to pay tax; personal bank accounts and assets may be attached. The NTS may impose departure restriction and blacklisting orders under the National Tax Collection Act, and a criminal conviction results in imprisonment, fines, and prohibition from future corporate service.
Non-Resident Withholding Tax Evasion and Improper DTA Claim

Statutory sanction: Corporate Tax Law Articles 5, 73 and 76 impose additional withholding taxes of 10% for non-withholding, 20% for under-declaration, and up to 30% for failure to remit withheld amounts, plus daily late-payment surcharge; a retrospective audit can deny treaty reductions and claw back foreign-investment incentives.

Director personal liability: Directors and withholding officers are jointly and severally liable for unremitted withholding tax and surcharges; personal assets can be seized without a separate veil-piercing determination where funds were diverted. Intentional non-withholding constitutes criminal tax fraud punishable by imprisonment and fines, and responsible non-resident directors are subject to travel bans and NTS blacklisting.
Sham Shell Company, Transfer Pricing Mispricing and Economic-Substance Abuse

Statutory sanction: The Adjustment of International Taxes Act Articles 4-6 and 12 authorize the NTS to re-determine income at arm's length, impute profit to the Korean entity, levy a 10% surcharge on the adjusted amount, and impose interest; anti-abuse rules under the Principal Purpose Test or Limitation-on-Benefits cancel treaty relief; CFC rules tax foreign undistributed profits in the hands of Korean shareholders; foreign-investment incentives may be revoked.

Director personal liability: The corporate veil will be pierced where the company is a facade and assets are stripped; directors face secondary tax liability under Basic National Tax Act Article 39, civil damages under Commercial Act Article 401, and criminal prosecution. If the artificial structure is used to launder funds through alternative payment rails or to operate unlawful VPN or data-privacy services, the director may be sentenced under the Act on Reporting and Use of Specific Financial Information to up to 5 years' imprisonment, criminal fines, forfeiture of digital and fiat assets, and travel ban/blacklisting.
