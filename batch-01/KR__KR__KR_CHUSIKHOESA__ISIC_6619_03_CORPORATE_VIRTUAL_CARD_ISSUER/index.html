Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

      
Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

    

    

      
Tax ID Application Infrastructure

      

        
Competent tax authority
National Tax Service (NTS) of the Republic of Korea; electronic portal: HomeTax System (www.hometax.go.kr) for tax registration, e-invoicing and VAT filing; Korea Customs Service UNI-PASS (www.unipass.customs.go.kr) for export declarations.

        
E-government filing portal
A foreign non-resident company operating in Korea must complete HomeTax business registration. Step 1: Confirm existence of a domestic place of business or appoint a Korean tax representative if no fixed place of business exists. Step 2: Prepare a business registration package comprising (i) proof of foreign statutory existence and registration certificate of the foreign company, (ii) notarized and apostilled or consular-legalized articles of incorporation and board resolution approving the Korean establishment or representative, (iii) power of attorney to the Korean tax representative, (iv) lease agreement or other documentary evidence of the Korean place of business, (v) identification documents of the responsible officer/director, and (vi) opening financial statement of the Korean operation. Step 3: Submit the application via HomeTax or in person to the district tax office with jurisdiction over the Korean place of business; a foreign company without a fixed place of business registers with the Seoul District Tax Office. Step 4: The NTS verifies the filing and issues a 10-digit Business Registration Number, which serves as the VAT ID, corporate TIN, and employer withholding registration number. Step 5: For a newly incorporated Chusik Hoesa, complete Commercial Court incorporation, obtain a corporate seal registration, and file the NTS tax registration within one month of business commencement; foreign ownership is permitted and does not alter the registration procedure.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

      

        
Default tax classification
Entity_Level_Taxation

        
Available election forms
None. Korea does not operate a check-the-box pass-through election or a domestic hybrid mismatch election for a Chusik Hoesa; the entity is mandatorily taxed at entity level under the Corporate Tax Act, with foreign branches or CFCs subject to separate Korean rules.

      

    

    

      
VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

      

        
Standard VAT / GST rate
10%

        
Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)
No revenue-based registration threshold: all persons carrying on business in Korea, including foreign non-resident suppliers and Chusik Hoesa corporations, must register for VAT from commencement of taxable supplies. A simplified VAT regime for individual entrepreneurs below KRW 80 million taxable turnover does not apply to corporations or foreign companies.

      

      
      

        Export 0% zero-rating conditions
        
A 0% VAT rate applies to goods exported from Korea upon export declaration acceptance, to supplies related to international maritime or aviation services, and to services performed for or used outside Korea where the recipient is a foreign non-resident. For a virtual card issuer, cross-border card issuing, transaction processing, interchange and settlement fees will qualify as zero-rated exported services only where the substantive service is performed outside Korea for a non-resident customer; the company must prove the overseas use or delivery, the identity and location of the customer, and the actual receipt of currency from a foreign source. Domestic B2B or consumer card transactions in Korea remain subject to the 10% VAT rate.

Mandatory electronic tax invoice (e-tax invoice) issuance through the NTS HomeTax electronic invoice system or NTS-certified EDI/API channel. E-invoicing is compulsory for all corporate VAT registrants and for individual entrepreneurs whose prior-year taxable supplies exceed KRW 100 million. The invoice data is transmitted to NTS in near real time and matched against VAT returns; no separate fiscal clearance stamp is used, but anti-fraud validation is performed by the NTS digital invoice database, and failure to transmit can carry additional tax penalties.

      
Export zero-rating is subject to strict NTS audit review. If documentation does not substantiate the export or the foreign use of the service, the NTS will assess VAT at 10% on the full supply value, impose additional taxes for under-declared VAT and unfaithful tax invoices (commonly 10% to 40% of the underpaid amount depending on negligence, intent or evasion), levy statutory late-payment interest, and may prosecutionally pursue fraudulent export invoice cases. The statutory assessment window is extended to 10 years where the zero-rating claim is supported by false documents; previously claimed input VAT refunds are recoverable with interest and penalties.

Stage 04
Banking & Treasury Architecture

      
Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

    

    

      
Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

      

        
Current-account convertibility
South Korea maintains a liberalized but fully regulated convertibility framework. Current-account payments for trade, services, and profit remittance are generally unrestricted and may be processed through domestic banks; however, every external payment requires a valid foreign exchange purpose classification and, where transactional thresholds are exceeded, prior or post reporting to the Bank of Korea through the reporting bank or Korea Customs Service. Capital-account operations are subject to the Foreign Investment Promotion Act notification, FDI registration, and supporting evidence requirements. The Korean won is not freely accessible offshore in a fully unrestricted manner because the KRW is not deliverable outside Korea in significant volume; cross-border KRW settlement is generally restricted to legitimate trade and investment flows. For a corporate virtual card issuer, routine settlement with card network processors is permissible, but proceeds in foreign currency must pass through a domestic foreign-currency account, and profit remittances require a clear sponsor/license and tax clearance document. The automated teller machine style conversion is not available for high-risk fintech treasury operations; every treasury transaction must be tested against the underlying commercial identity, card network settlement schedules, and ISIC/ELF compliance declaration.

        
Export proceeds surrender rules
Exporters must collect export proceeds within six months of the export declaration/customs clearance date; the period may be extended by the head of Korea Customs Service or the Minister of Economy and Finance for justified causes. There is no compulsory foreign-exchange surrender ratio for ordinary export receipts under the current free-floating regime, so the mandatory conversion ratio is 0%; exporters may credit the proceeds to a licensed domestic foreign-currency account and convert at commercial rates as needed.

      

      
Central bank supervision: Bank of Korea (BOK) under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act, with policy and supervisory coordination by the Ministry of Economy and Finance (MOEF) and the Financial Supervisory Service (FSS)

    

    

      
Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

      

        
        

          

            Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails
            Fit: High
          

          
Rating: Low. Offshore digital-only EMI providers are not recognized as authorized banks for the purpose of holding a resident Korean corporation's treasury balances. South Korea requires foreign-exchange business and payment services to be conducted through a locally licensed institution under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act and the Electronic Financial Transactions Act. A Korean Chusik Hoesa must open its primary operating and settlement accounts at a domestic commercial bank or registered brokerage/securities firm; an offshore EMI may at most provide a technical card management or program management interface if a domestically licensed credit-card issuing company underwrites the card scheme. It cannot legally service the entity's corporate deposits or provide cross-border clearing rails independently.

Core KYC Rejection Triggers & Defense Dossier

      
      

        Unclear beneficial ownership or deviation between statutory officers on the corporate registry and actual controlling persons, potentially masked by nominee directors or multi-layer overseas shareholding.
        
Root cause: Unclear beneficial ownership or deviation between statutory officers on the corporate registry and actual controlling persons, potentially masked by nominee directors or multi-layer overseas shareholding.

        
Defense evidence: Submit the complete corporate registry extract (Gibu), a certified share register, a legal entity structure chart, passport copies and ID documents for every individual owning or controlling in aggregate 25 percent or more of shares or voting rights, signed director appointments, and a notarized statement of no nominee arrangement; also provide a six-month capitalization and shareholder funding audit trail.

      

      
      

        Regulatory classification mismatch: the virtual expense card issuing business may look like unregulated card arbitrage rather than a licensed financial service, exposing the bank to money service business liability.
        
Root cause: Regulatory classification mismatch: the virtual expense card issuing business may look like unregulated card arbitrage rather than a licensed financial service, exposing the bank to money service business liability.

        
Defense evidence: Provide the FSS license or registration certificate issued under the Credit Specialized Financial Business Act and/or Electronic Financial Transactions Act, the program manager agreement with the issuing bank or Mastercard/Visa BIN sponsor, the ISIC 6619 classification rationale, and evidence that the entity has obtained all Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) certifications and anti-money laundering program manuals and independent AML audit reports.

      

      
      

        Cross-border wire profile inconsistent with declared fintech operation: high frequency of mixed-purpose transfers, round-dollar treasury movements, or rapid pass-through of funds to third countries.
        
Root cause: Cross-border wire profile inconsistent with declared fintech operation: high frequency of mixed-purpose transfers, round-dollar treasury movements, or rapid pass-through of funds to third countries.

        
Defense evidence: Deliver the underlying customer agreements, merchant settlement schedules, sample expense card transaction log, signed invoices or purchase orders for treasury disbursements, projected cash-flow model, and the official FX transaction declaration forms previously filed at the bank; a certification that all SWIFT wires bear correct intended FX purpose codes and that no transaction breaches the 10,000 USD reporting threshold is also required.

      

      
      

        Sanctions and virtual asset exposure links through corporate card loads or settlement corridors in jurisdictions with AML/CFT deficiencies.
        
Root cause: Sanctions and virtual asset exposure links through corporate card loads or settlement corridors in jurisdictions with AML/CFT deficiencies.

        
Defense evidence: Provide an OFAC/EU/UN sanctions screening report covering all cardholders and corporate clients, a banned-jurisdiction list embedded in the card programme, an annual independent sanctions compliance audit, and evidence that virtual asset deposits or crypto-funded loads are contractually prohibited and operationally blocked by the card issuer platform.

      

      
    

    

      
Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

      

        In-bound foreign equity contributions must be lodged as Foreign Direct Investment under the Foreign Investment Promotion Act before the remittance. The investor submits a declaration to a domestic bank or KOTRA, obtains an FDI registration certificate, and then sends the funds via SWIFT MT103 in the investor's name using the FX purpose code corresponding to inward direct investment. The receiving bank credits the corporate foreign-currency escrow/deposit account and later converts to KRW with the precise FDI declaration. Capital verification is performed by the bank against the FDI registration certificate, the MT103 SWIFT message, the investor's capital account record, and the paid-in capital ledger of the Chusik Hoesa. Dividends or return-of-capital outbound remittances must be supported by audited financial statements, corporate tax clearance, and an FX remittance application showing the earnings were legally generated.Outbound treasury payments use SWIFT MT103 for direct cross-border purchase, commercial letters of credit issued under UCP 600 for documentary trade settlements, and documentary collections where the card-funded supplier requires shipping-document confirmation. Virtual card transactions flow independently through Mastercard/Visa network settlement to merchant acquirer banks; the licensed card issuer must maintain a settlement bank account in Korea and settle net card balances in KRW to the merchant acquirer through the network, with foreign-currency exposure offset by domestic bank FX contracts. All outbound wires must include the correct Foreign Exchange Transactions Act purpose code and maintain source-of-funds documentation for every beneficiary.Onshore Korean won foreign exchange forward contracts with a licensed domestic bank supported by a genuine underlying trade or card settlement exposure (real demand principle under Foreign Exchange Transactions Act)Deliverable cross-currency swaps with a licensed domestic bank for hedging contracted foreign-currency cash flows, provided the swap is booked under the entity's treasury policy and reported to the Bank of Korea where requiredOffshore non-deliverable forwards (NDFs) are not permissible as a primary onshore hedge; NDFs may be considered only outside the regulated Korean banking system to hedge third-party investor exposure and must never interfere with domestic bank settlement

Stage 06
Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

      
Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

    

    

      
Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)
24% national headline CIT, levied on progressive brackets of 9% for taxable income up to KRW 200 million, 19% for KRW 200 million to KRW 20 billion, 21% for KRW 20 billion to KRW 300 billion, and 24% above KRW 300 billion; an additional 10% local income tax surcharge yields an aggregate top rate of 26.4%

      
Dividend withholding tax (WHT)
22% aggregate, comprising 20% national corporate withholding tax plus 2% local income tax surcharge, unless reduced or exempted by an applicable double tax treaty; for example, under the Korea-US tax treaty a 10% dividend rate may apply to a corporate beneficial owner holding at least 10% of the voting stock, bringing the aggregate local-incidence treatment to 11% (10% national plus 1% local surtax)

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

      
Master / Local File threshold: KRW 10 billion per business year for mandatory Local File preparation and KRW 1 trillion group consolidated revenue for the Master File, while all foreign related-party transactions exceeding KRW 100 million must be reported in the annual International Transactions Report attached to the corporate tax return

      
Economic substance scope: Korea does not maintain a Kuwaiti or UAE-style Economic Substance Regulation as a separate statute; instead, the Framework Act on National Taxes Article 14 imposes a substance-over-form principle, and the International Tax Coordination Act incorporates OECD transfer pricing substance and beneficial ownership requirements; for treaty relief, the Korean tax authority applies the beneficial ownership test and the OECD Core Income-Generating Activities analysis to determine whether the foreign recipient actually exercises decision-making functions, assumes risks, and performs substantive business activities through employees and premises; a Korean Chusik Hoesa that issues virtual cards through a foreign shell with no local management, no risk distribution, and no operating substance will be denied treaty benefits, and the CFC rules in the International Tax Coordination Act may attribute undistributed foreign income of such a shell back to Korean shareholders

      

    

    

      
Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

      
      

        Tax Evasion and Unfounded Expense Claiming
        
Statutory sanction: The Punishment of Tax Evaders Act Article 9 punishes corporate tax evasion with imprisonment not exceeding 2 years, a fine not exceeding twice the evaded tax, or both; in addition, the Framework Act on National Taxes imposes a 40% fraudulent underreporting penalty, plus daily late-payment surcharges of 0.022% per day on the unpaid amount, and the National Tax Service will re-assess the Chusik Hoesa for the full deficiency

        
Director personal liability: Under Commercial Act Article 401, directors are jointly and severally liable to the company for damages caused by willful misconduct or gross negligence; for tax evasion, the director can be named individually in a criminal complaint, subjected to a departure restriction order, have personal bank accounts and assets attached, and be sentenced to imprisonment up to 2 years; if the company is dissolved without paying corporate tax, the court may pierce the corporate veil under the Court precedents on corporate personality abuse and hold the director personally liable for the unpaid corporate tax and penalties

      

      
      

        Transfer Pricing Documentation and Shell Structure Abuse
        
Statutory sanction: The International Tax Coordination Act empowers the National Tax Service to disregard the intercompany arrangement, recalculate income at the arm's length price, impose additional tax on the resulting understatement, and refuse treaty relief for failure to prove beneficial ownership; under-reporting due to intentional avoidance attracts a 40% penalty and a possible criminal fine of twice the evaded tax; the law also imposes administrative fines for failure to produce mandated transfer pricing documentation

        
Director personal liability: The director who certified the tax return may be held personally liable under Commercial Act Article 401 for the company's extra tax if the abuse arose from the director's intentional act; a travel ban may be issued for the director while a tax collection investigation is open; if the shell arrangement is found to be a tax evasion device, the director may be referred to the prosecution for the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act and the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act, with imprisonment up to 2 years and personal restitution of the evaded tax and penalties

      

      
      

        Unlicensed Fintech Card Issuing, AML and Beneficial Ownership Violation
        
Statutory sanction: Unlicensed credit card issuing is administratively prohibited and may be punished under the Credit Finance Business Act with imprisonment up to 7 years or a fine of up to KRW 100 million; failure to register as an electronic financial business under the Electronic Financial Transactions Act can result in a fine and cancellation of the payment network partnership; the Financial Information Act violations carry fines, and the Financial Services Commission can block the entity from the payment system; the National Tax Service will simultaneously treat the unreported fee income as hidden earnings and assess corporate tax with a 40% fraud penalty

        
Director personal liability: License violations are treated as personal criminal acts of the representative director if the director directly organized the unlicensed issuing; aside from a prison sentence, the director is jointly and severally liable to pay unpaid corporate tax, penalties, and regulatory restitution; the director is subject to an exit ban under tax collection law and can be named in sanctions enforcement lists; a court may pierce the corporate veil if the entity is used as a mere instrument for illegal card issuance and money laundering, and the director's personal assets may be attached for civil damages suffered by merchants and cardholders
