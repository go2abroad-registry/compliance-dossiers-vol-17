A registered office in South Korea must be a fixed, identifiable address in the Articles of Association and must be accepted by the District Court Registry as the company's head office. A virtual office or commercial mailbox is technically permissible for court registration if supplied by a licensed corporate-services provider with a dedicated office unit, signboard, mail-handling protocol, and visitor reception, but the National Tax Service and Korean banks for an ISIC 7310 overseas PR communications firm will likely reject a pure virtual mailbox without a physical lease. In practice, for retainers paid by ACH and wire, banks require a real floor desk, utility bill, and on-site representative before opening corporate accounts. Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

National Tax Service (NTS) of the Republic of Korea, with mandatory electronic filing through the HomeTax portal (www.hometax.go.kr). The Korean tax administration is centralized and unitary; the competent district tax office is determined by the corporation's registered head office address. Customs matters are administered by the Korea Customs Service (UNI-PASS) where goods are exported.

E-government filing portal

A foreign-owned Chusik Hoesa incorporated under the Korean Commercial Act is a domestic corporation and must register with the NTS by filing a business registration application (corporation type) with the district tax office within 20 days from commencement of business and in any event no later than the corporate tax registration deadline of 3 months from incorporation. The issued 10-digit Business Registration Number serves simultaneously as the corporate TIN, VAT registration number, and withholding agent account number. The registration may be completed online via HomeTax using the legal representative's authentication certificate; if the foreign legal representative has no resident registration number, a joint security certificate is required through the relevant tax office, and a domestic tax representative must be appointed for service of process and tax payment administration. The full procedure includes: (1) filing the articles of incorporation and certificate of incorporation; (2) submitting the lease contract and business-premises evidence; (3) obtaining the 10-digit Business Registration Number; (4) completing local tax registration with the competent local government for local income tax (residence tax) surcharge; and (5) opening a foreign currency account with a Korean foreign exchange bank and filing the related foreign exchange declaration for export receivables.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation / Territorial_Scope: A Chusik Hoesa is the Korean statutory stock corporation and is by default an independent corporate taxpayer under the Corporate Income Tax Act. As a domestic corporation, it is taxed on worldwide income, while non-resident and foreign corporate shareholders are taxed only on Korean-source income. No pass-through or transparency treatment is available. Local income tax (residence tax) is imposed as a 10 percent surcharge on corporate income tax. Dividend distributions to non-resident shareholders are subject to Korean withholding tax at the statutory 22 percent aggregate rate (20 percent corporate plus 2 percent local), subject to treaty reduction. The entity is also liable as a withholding agent for payroll and cross-border payments.

Available election forms

Korean tax law does not contain a US-style check-the-box election. The available statutory elections relevant to this structure are: (1) consolidated corporate income tax return election for a wholly-owned domestic corporate group under Article 76-2 of the Corporate Income Tax Act, requiring application to the NTS and applying to all qualifying group members; (2) the VAT zero-rating and foreign-currency-settlement election under Article 21(1)(e) of the VAT Act for cross-border service supplies, exercised through issuance of zero-rated e-tax invoices in the prescribed form; (3) the foreign tax credit versus foreign tax deduction election under Article 57(2) of the Corporate Income Tax Act for overseas branch and foreign-source income; and (4) hybrid mismatch rules under OECD BEPS Action 2, which apply compulsorily and are not elective; Korea maintains no hybrid entity classification election for Chusik Hoesa.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

10%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

There is no de minimis VAT turnover threshold above which registration is required in Korea; registration is mandatory for any business operator, including a Chusik Hoesa, that makes taxable supplies. A simplified taxation regime for individuals applies below KRW 80,000,000 annual turnover, but juridical persons such as Chusik Hoesa are categorically excluded from simplified taxation and must always file standard quarterly VAT returns irrespective of turnover.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero rating at 0 percent VAT is available for exported goods under Article 21(1)(1) of the VAT Act and for services supplied to non-residents that are actually consumed or utilized outside Korea, in particular advertising, public relations, communication services, and other offshore business services qualifying under Article 21(1)(e) of the VAT Act and Article 26(1)(7) of its Enforcement Decree. For this ISIC 7310 global PR agency, the eligibility conditions are: the service is rendered exclusively to a customer without a domestic place of business in Korea; the service is for use in promoting the customer's business outside Korea; payment is denominated in foreign currency and collected from outside Korea; and the entity retains an executed offshore engagement contract plus an inbound foreign-exchange receipt certificate. Input VAT attributable to zero-rated supplies is fully creditable and refundable. Pure offshore PR retainers qualify, but domestic media buying and domestic campaign management do not; mixed contracts must be split into taxable domestic and zero-rated offshore portions with separate e-invoices.

  • Zero-rated electronic tax invoice transmitted via NTS HomeTax with the zero-rating code for the transaction
  • Executed international PR service agreement or media placement contract identifying the non-resident customer, offshore market, and service scope
  • Certificate of receipt of foreign exchange (inward remittance or foreign exchange purchase certificate from a Korean foreign exchange bank) evidencing settlement of the export consideration
  • Customs Export Declaration (UNI-PASS electronic declaration) for any physical or tangible export component, such as marketing media assets, branded merchandise, or printed collateral
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill for tangible goods movement accompanying the service export, if applicable

Korea mandates fully electronic tax invoices (e-Tax Invoices) for all VAT-registered corporations. Every taxable supply, including zero-rated exports, must be evidenced by an e-Tax Invoice transmitted to the NTS HomeTax e-invoicing system no later than the day following the supply date. The NTS performs real-time clearance by issuing a unique management (acceptance) number to each e-invoice; AP/ERP integration is required and supported by the NTS Open API for e-invoice and returns, which issues acknowledgment receipts. No paper invoice option exists for B2B and B2G supplies; no separate digital stamping tax applies, as the statutory electronic invoice is validated through the centralized NTS database. Failure to transmit e-invoices attracts an additional tax of 2 percent of the supply value, and delayed issuance attracts an additional tax of 1 percent.

The NTS treats false export zero-rating and artificial offshore billing as aggravated tax evasion. The consequences are: (1) retroactive reassessment clawing back the 10 percent VAT previously zero-rated, plus an additional tax of 40 percent of the underpaid amount for deceitful non-filing or under-reporting under Article 47-3 of the Framework Act on National Taxes; (2) denial of input VAT credits traced to fictitious export transactions; (3) penalty interest at the statutory overdue rate; (4) criminal prosecution under the Punishment of Tax Evasion Act with imprisonment of up to 2 years or a fine of up to 2 times the tax evaded; and (5) corporate blacklisting for intensive NTS audit selection, which may trigger an integrated customs and foreign-exchange probe with secondary penalties under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act for failure to collect export proceeds within 180 days unless a prescribed extension is approved.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Current-account transactions, including overseas PR retainer receipts, subcontract payments, media placement disbursements, and licensing fees, are fully liberalized and require no prior MOEF approval; they must be routed through an authorized foreign exchange bank with a Foreign Exchange Declaration form and underlying contract or invoice, and the resulting data is captured in FEIS. Capital-account transactions remain restricted or reporting-based: inward foreign direct investment (FDI) must be filed under the Foreign Investment Promotion Act (FIPA) with MOEF, KOTRA, or a delegated authorized bank; foreign borrowings above prescribed thresholds require MOEF/BOK reporting; and outward investment requires prior filing. The Korean won (KRW) is not an internationally convertible currency; non-residents may not freely hold onshore KRW accounts without a permitted purpose, and offshore KRW liquidity is confined to the licensed NDF market. Profit remittance to foreign shareholders is lawful but subject to sequential hurdles: (i) confirmation of distributable reserves via audited financial statements; (ii) a shareholder dividend resolution and board resolution; (iii) withholding of the 22% dividend tax (20% plus 10% local surtax) or a treaty-reduced rate evidenced by a tax clearance certificate or National Tax Service confirmation; (iv) proof of FIPA-registered shareholding matching the remittee; and (v) completion of the FX declaration and FEIS reporting by the remitting bank. The bank may also request a foreign-invested company registration certificate and an overseas remittance report before executing the wire.

Export proceeds surrender rules

The Republic of Korea abolished mandatory surrender of export proceeds in 1998. Proceeds earned from overseas PR services (invisible trade or current-account receipts) must be repatriated to an authorized foreign exchange bank within the permitted settlement term, generally one year from the invoice date or completion of service, with documented extension available for justified commercial delay. The Chusik Hoesa is not required to convert foreign currency into KRW; proceeds may be retained indefinitely in a Foreign Currency Deposit (FCD) account, converted at market rates, or used directly for permitted current-account payments. All receipts above the de minimis threshold (USD 5,000 or KRW equivalent) must be declared via the FX Declaration form, and the bank will record the receipt in FEIS. Failure to settle or repatriate within the statutory period without approval exposes the company to administrative fines under FETA.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Korea (BOK) jointly with the Ministry of Economy and Finance (MOEF) as co-administrators of the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act (FETA); operational enforcement and declaration processing are delegated to authorized foreign exchange banks, and transaction-level surveillance is centralized in the Foreign Exchange Information System (FEIS). The Financial Supervisory Service (FSS) and the Korea Financial Intelligence Unit (KFIU) supervise bank conduct and AML/CFT obligations.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

LOW feasibility and legally constrained. Foreign electronic money institutions licensed under UK FCA EMI, EU EMI, or US money transmitter regimes are not recognized as authorized foreign exchange banks under FETA and cannot hold onshore KRW transactional accounts, cannot execute statutory FX declarations, and cannot file FEIS reports for a Korean resident entity. The Chusik Hoesa must maintain its primary treasury, FX, and settlement accounts with a domestic authorized foreign exchange bank. An offshore EMI may serve only as an auxiliary collection or disbursement wallet for non-KRW payments (e.g., USD or EUR client retainers), provided balances are swept daily into the Korean FCD account; using an EMI as the principal clearing rail creates regulatory, tax reporting, and audit deficiencies and risks fines for non-declared foreign receipts.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

All inbound equity capital must arrive by SWIFT MT103 from the FIPA-registered foreign investor to the Chusik Hoesa account at an authorized foreign exchange bank. The remitter's legal name, account number, and jurisdiction must exactly match the investor recorded on the FIPA Foreign Investment Report; mismatches cause automatic compliance holds. Upon receipt, the bank credits the Foreign Currency Deposit or KRW capital account, issues a Foreign Currency Deposit Receipt and Purchase/Sales Record, and files the FEIS declaration. The FIPA report must be lodged with MOEF, KOTRA, or the delegated bank before or promptly after the capital contribution, and the company must complete the foreign-invested company registration to unlock full repatriation rights. The remittance purpose code must reflect equity participation (the MOEF capital-transaction classification for FDI rather than a current-account service code). The supporting verification dossier comprises: the FIPA report form and registration certificate, board resolution and shareholder resolution authorizing the share issuance, share subscription agreement, amended articles of incorporation reflecting the capital increase, certificate of corporate seal, and signatory identification. Where capital enters as a shareholder loan rather than equity, the borrowing must be reported under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Regulations and a separate loan agreement plus drawn-down schedule must be retained.Primary clearing rails are: (1) SWIFT MT103 for direct cross-border wires to overseas clients, media vendors, and subcontractors, with current-account service purpose codes and the underlying invoice or contract retained for FEIS validation; (2) SWIFT MT202 COV for cover payments where the beneficiary bank operates through a correspondent in a different currency zone; (3) commercial documentary letters of credit (sight and usance) for procurement of production services, media inventory, or event logistics where the beneficiary requires LC security; and (4) domestic disbursements in Korean won via the KFTC HOFINET wire network for ordinary vendor payments and BOK-WIRE+ RTGS for high-value same-day settlements. Every outbound wire requires a Foreign Exchange Declaration, supporting commercial documents, and tax verification where the payment constitutes a deductible expense; the bank maps each payment to an R-series service purpose code and screens the beneficiary against sanctions and watchlists. For recurring retainer-based outflows, the company should establish a standing settlement instruction file with the Relationship Manager to expedite dual-control release and reduce per-transaction friction. The treasury should maintain compensating won balances and a USD FCD buffer to avoid failed-settlement charges and to execute vendor payments without forced spot conversion.Onshore deliverable KRW/USD and KRW/EUR foreign exchange forward contracts on a real-demand basis, with tenors aligned to the underlying PR retainer receivables and subcontractor payables.FX swaps and rollover extensions transacted with the authorized foreign exchange bank to bridge mismatches between retainer receipt dates and disbursement dates.Non-deliverable forward (NDF) contracts for KRW executed through the onshore licensed bank or a global treasury desk, permitted strictly where the company can document real trade exposure and the contract is for hedging rather than speculation.Cross-currency swaps (CCS) hedging long-term foreign-currency liabilities, including shareholder loans or multi-year overseas media commitments.Plain-vanilla FX options and zero-cost collars (forward-plus-option structures) with real-demand documentation, subject to the bank's product eligibility review and the company's board-approved hedging policy.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: LOW feasibility and legally constrained. Foreign electronic money institutions licensed under UK FCA EMI, EU EMI, or US money transmitter regimes are not recognized as authorized foreign exchange banks under FETA and cannot hold onshore KRW transactional accounts, cannot execute statutory FX declarations, and cannot file FEIS reports for a Korean resident entity. The Chusik Hoesa must maintain its primary treasury, FX, and settlement accounts with a domestic authorized foreign exchange bank. An offshore EMI may serve only as an auxiliary collection or disbursement wallet for non-KRW payments (e.g., USD or EUR client retainers), provided balances are swept daily into the Korean FCD account; using an EMI as the principal clearing rail creates regulatory, tax reporting, and audit deficiencies and risks fines for non-declared foreign receipts.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

24% national corporate income tax on taxable income exceeding KRW 300 billion; lower brackets apply at 9% up to KRW 200 million, 19% above KRW 200 million, and 21% above KRW 20 billion. A 10% Local Income Tax surcharge on the national corporate income tax produces an aggregate top marginal rate of 26.4%.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Non-resident shareholders receiving dividends from a Korean Chusik Hoesa are subject to aggregate statutory withholding of 22%, comprising 20% national Corporate Income Tax withholding plus 2 percentage points representing the 10% Local Income Tax surcharge on the national withholding tax.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The final corporate income tax return must be filed within three months after the fiscal year-end; a calendar-year corporation files by March 31 of the following year. A foreign corporation with a Korean permanent establishment follows the same rule. A foreign corporation without a Korean permanent establishment is generally subject to source withholding and does not file a self-assessment return.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Mandatory Local File submission is triggered when annual cross-border transactions with foreign related parties exceed KRW 5 billion. Master File and Country-by-Country Report obligations trigger when the multinational group's consolidated revenue for the preceding fiscal year exceeds KRW 100 billion. Contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation should still be prepared for smaller cross-border related-party transactions involving an overseas PR agency.

Economic substance scope: Korea does not administer a standalone Economic Substance Regulations regime comparable to offshore financial centers. However, the Korean Framework Act on National Taxes contains a statutory substance-over-form principle, and the International Tax Adjustment Act includes general anti-abuse and anti-treaty-shopping rules. A Korean Chusik Hoesa or its overseas PR affiliate must demonstrate actual offices, personnel, management control, risk-taking, and operational activity; artificial arrangements designed to shift Korean-source retainer income to tax-favored jurisdictions will be recharacterized.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Return

Statutory sanction: Imprisonment for up to three years or a fine up to five times the evaded national tax under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act, plus payment of the evaded tax, late-payment interest, and negligence penalties; administrative exclusion from government procurement and public subsidies.

Director personal liability: A controlling director, actual business operator, or de facto manager who caused the false filing may be criminally prosecuted and personally subjected to fines or imprisonment; the NTS can pierce the corporate entity where it is a sham, and unpaid corporate taxes may be collected from the individual's personal assets; departure bans are possible for delinquent tax amounts above KRW 50 million.
Shell Company and Artificial Foreign Affiliate

Statutory sanction: Recharacterization under the substance-over-form principle; disallowance of the related-party deductions; additional corporate income tax and Local Income Tax surcharge at the recharacterized bracket; negligence surcharge up to 20% of the restored taxable income; potential criminal prosecution where intentional evasion is proven.

Director personal liability: Directors who engineered or approved the artificial structure may be held jointly liable for the corporation's unpaid taxes under the corporate-veil-piercing doctrine; senior management can face criminal penalties, asset seizure, and court-ordered restitution; overseas travel may be restricted during active tax criminal investigation.
Money Laundering and Unreported Cross-Border Wire Movement

Statutory sanction: Administrative fines and penalties under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act, including monetary penalties scaled to the unreported amount; criminal liability under the Financial Transactions Reports Act for failing to report suspicious transactions, punishable by imprisonment up to five years or a fine up to KRW 50 million; tax assessments on undisclosed foreign-source and repatriated income.

Director personal liability: Directors and compliance officers may be prosecuted for knowingly assisting in money-laundering transactions or permitting deficient AML controls; personal fines, forfeiture orders, imprisonment, and travel bans can result; the regulator may separately seek disqualification from holding directorship positions.
Transfer Pricing Documentation Failure

Statutory sanction: Negligence surcharge of 10% or 20% of the unreported international transaction amount; unilateral transfer pricing adjustment by the NTS; imposition of a 20% additional tax on understated taxable income plus 0.022% daily late-payment interest; intentional or reckless behavior may trigger criminal fines under tax penalty provisions.

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false transfer pricing documents or direct the suppression of related-party transaction records may face personal liability under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act; where the company is insolvent, the NTS may pursue a director as the actual taxpayer or secondary taxpayer; travel bans and criminal imprisonment are available for severe intentional noncompliance.
