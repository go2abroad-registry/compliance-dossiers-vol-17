Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

No statutory minimum paid-up capital and no blocked escrow account is required for a domestic Yuhan Hoesa. Every subscribed quota must be fully paid before the application for incorporation registration is filed; non-cash contributions must be appraised and fully performed on or before registration. The Articles of Incorporation may fix any total capital amount, and the capital account need not be frozen after issuance of the registration certificate. If the subscriber is a foreign investor seeking protection under the Foreign Investment Promotion Act, the prescribed qualifying minimum remittance is KRW 100,000,000 and the funds must be remitted through a licensed foreign exchange bank with complete source-of-funds documentation; otherwise no escrow or blocked account requirement applies.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

National Tax Service (NTS) of the Republic of Korea; online electronic portal: HomeTax at hometax.go.kr for VAT registration and electronic tax invoices; Korea Customs Service UNI-PASS at unipass.customs.go.kr for export declarations and export clearance certificate verification.

E-government filing portal

For Yuhan Hoesa (Korean limited liability company), the representative director files a Business Registration application (business start registration) with the NTS district tax office having jurisdiction over the registered head office within 20 days after commencement of business. Attachments include the certified copy of corporate registration, articles of incorporation, evidence of the physical place of business (lease or ownership document), identity document of the representative director, and, where applicable, appointment of an authorized tax representative. The NTS issues the Business Registration Certificate containing the 10-digit Business Registration Number. This number is the single statutory Tax Identification Number (TIN) used simultaneously for corporate income tax, VAT registration, and electronic tax invoice issuance. For a foreign non-resident entity with no domestic place of business but a Korean VAT filing obligation (for example, a foreign e-service supplier), the foreign entity must appoint a Korean tax representative and apply through HomeTax; the NTS then issues a foreign-operator Business Registration Number/VAT ID. Export sales made by the Korean Yuhan Hoesa are zero-rated after such VAT registration is active.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

10%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

No general turnover exemption threshold for a Korea-resident corporation; VAT registration is mandatory from the first taxable supply. The KRW 80 million simplified taxpayer threshold available under Korean VAT law applies only to certain individual entrepreneurs and is not available to Yuhan Hoesa.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Export zero-rating is available under Article 20 of the Korean Value Added Tax Act. Tangible goods such as custom mechanical keyboard caps qualify when the goods physically leave Korea under an accepted Korea Customs Service export declaration, title and risk pass to a foreign purchaser, and the consequent foreign currency export proceeds are received and repatriated to Korea within the statutory foreign exchange deadline. The exporter must be a VAT-registered business and must retain contemporaneous proof of export and settlement in the accounting records; the zero-rated export supply is then reported in the periodic VAT return through HomeTax and associated input VAT credits or refunds can be claimed.

  • Korea Customs Service Export Declaration Certificate (Export Clearance Certificate) issued under the Customs Act at the time of export clearance
  • Commercial Invoice and Packing List matching the export declaration details
  • Transport document: Bill of Lading for sea cargo or International Air Waybill for air cargo
  • Foreign exchange repatriation evidence: Foreign Exchange Transaction Confirmation or Inward Remittance Certificate (FIRC) issued by an authorized foreign exchange bank under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act, evidencing receipt of the export proceeds in foreign currency
  • Export-related electronic tax invoice records transmitted through HomeTax

All tax invoices, including zero-rated export tax invoices, must be issued as electronic tax invoices through the National Tax Service HomeTax e-invoice system. The electronic tax invoice must contain the purchaser's Business Registration Number (TIN), supply date, supply value, and VAT amount, and must be transmitted to HomeTax by the day after the statutory invoice issuance date. HomeTax assigns a unique NTS-approved electronic tax invoice authorization number for anti-fraud and duplicate-invoice prevention. NTS continuously cross-matches electronic tax invoice data with Korea Customs Service export declarations, foreign exchange receipt records, bank remittance data, and VAT return filings. There is no pre-clearance of each transaction before sale, but each issued e-tax invoice receives NTS biometric/digital-stamp-style validation through the certified electronic invoice number, and as of systemic enforcement, taxpayers must use certified e-invoicing methods or face VAT registration and invoicing penalties.

NTS and Korea Customs Service may jointly audit export zero-rating claims by matching the export declaration, e-tax invoice, bill of lading, and bank foreign exchange receipt records. If the zero-rating is disallowed because the goods were not actually exported, the documents are false, or the export proceeds were not repatriated within the required 180-day period, the NTS will assess the uncollected VAT at the standard 10% rate, add statutory penalty surcharges for understated VAT or false electronic tax invoices, impose overdue interest from the original filing due date, and recover previous VAT refunds on a retrospective basis. Fraudulent refund claims or invoices without real underlying exports may also be referred to criminal prosecution under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act, with penalties including imprisonment and fines, and the statutory limitation period for reopening the tax assessment is extended in cases of false or fraudulent declarations.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

South Korea operates a liberalised but supervised convertible-currency regime. Current-account receipts and payments are generally unrestricted for trade and ordinary business uses; Korea's OECD membership and comprehensive free trade agreements have eliminated most historical surrender requirements. Capital-account transactions, however, remain subject to a notification and declaration system under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act and the Foreign Exchange Transactions Regulations. Foreign direct investment into a Yuhan Hoesa can be settled in KRW or freely convertible foreign currencies after filing a foreign exchange notification through a domestic foreign exchange bank. The key restrictions concern mandatory reporting of large cross-border payments to the Bank of Korea's Foreign Exchange Information System within the prescribed deadline, offshore foreign-currency borrowing requiring a loan report when the borrower is not a licensed financial institution, and profit and dividend remittance, which requires evidence of a valid distribution resolution, payment of Korean tax on those distributable profits, and an underlying foreign-investment notification. The Korean won is convertible for current-account settlements, but speculative capital flows may be moderated through macro-prudential measures, and banks commonly require trade documents before converting large FX amounts into KRW. In practice, permanent profit repatriation hurdles are modest for a low-risk custom keyboard-caps business if all equity injections and shareholder loans are properly reported and annual corporate taxes are paid.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Export proceeds from sales of custom mechanical keyboard caps must be repatriated into a licensed Domestic Foreign Exchange Bank and reported under the Foreign Exchange Information System; the standard statutory collection window is 180 days from the date of export declaration, with a possible extension to one year approved by the bank. No mandatory conversion ratio is imposed in the current liberalised regime; proceeds may be retained in a Foreign Exchange Deposit Account or sold for KRW at the prevailing market rate. An Overseas Foreign Exchange Deposit Account is available only with prior bank authorisation for specific working-capital needs.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Korea (BOK) with policy and enforcement authority shared by the Ministry of Economy and Finance (MOEF) under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act of South Korea

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low viability as a core account substitute. Foreign fintech EMIs and digital payment institutions are not licensed in South Korea under the Electronic Financial Transactions Act and cannot hold KRW transactional deposits or provide domestic wire settlement for a Korean corporate entity. They may be used only as payment gateways for international card capture, but settlement of funds into a compliant Korean corporate treasury must occur through a licensed domestic bank. Routing core treasury flows through an offshore EMI would constitute a serious AML red flag and would violate the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Initial shareholder capital must be remitted via SWIFT MT103 from the overseas corporate bank account with the beneficiary clearly identified as the Korean Yuhan Hoesa at the licensed domestic bank. The remitter must state the Foreign Exchange Transaction purpose code for foreign direct equity investment and the company's Foreign Investment Notification reference number. Within the statutory declaration window, the domestic bank files the foreign direct investment acquisition notification with the Ministry of Economy and Finance through the Foreign Exchange Information System. The receiving bank then verifies the paid-in capital against the corporate registration record and issues a Capital Verification Certificate, after which the funds may be released into the operational operating account.Supplier payments for PBT resin, keycap moulds, dyes, packaging and logistics are settled through three compliant clearing rails: SWIFT MT103 telegraphic transfers for standard import invoices with a valid import declaration and FX processing purpose code; Commercial Letters of Credit issued via SWIFT MT700 for large master-order values above USD 100,000 to protect against supplier non-delivery; and Documentary Collections via MT400 for medium-value transactions with trusted repeat suppliers. Every outbound FX purchase must be supported by the underlying trade contract, commercial invoice, packing list, transport document and customs declaration, and must be reported in accordance with Foreign Exchange Transactions Act thresholds.KRW/USD deliverable forward contracts with a licensed domestic bank for a term matching the export cash-flow cycle, with notional amounts capped at 100% of verified export sales commitments.FX swaps with an authorised domestic bank for rolling working-capital conversion between the Foreign Exchange Deposit Account and the KRW operating account arising from known trade settlement schedules.Cross-currency interest-rate swaps allowed only where a long-term USD-denominated capital injection or machinery financing loan exists; non-deliverable forward (NDF) structures are not permissible for an onshore Korean entity as a standalone hedging layer because settlement is deliverable in KRW.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Low viability as a core account substitute. Foreign fintech EMIs and digital payment institutions are not licensed in South Korea under the Electronic Financial Transactions Act and cannot hold KRW transactional deposits or provide domestic wire settlement for a Korean corporate entity. They may be used only as payment gateways for international card capture, but settlement of funds into a compliant Korean corporate treasury must occur through a licensed domestic bank. Routing core treasury flows through an offshore EMI would constitute a serious AML red flag and would violate the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

National Corporate Income Tax rates are progressive: 9% on taxable income up to KRW 200 million; 19% on income from KRW 200 million to KRW 20 billion; 21% on income from KRW 20 billion to KRW 300 billion; and 24% on income above KRW 300 billion. A 10% local income tax surcharge on national corporate tax raises the aggregate headline top effective rate to 26.4%.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Dividends paid by a Korean Yuhan Hoesa to non-resident shareholders are subject to withholding tax of 20% national income tax plus 2% local income tax surcharge, giving an aggregate statutory withholding rate of 22%, subject to reduction or exemption under an applicable double tax agreement.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Mandatory Master File and Local File preparation in Korea is required when aggregate cross-border related-party transactions exceed KRW 10 billion (KRW 10,000,000,000); the separate annual international transaction report threshold is KRW 100 million, and taxpayers below the Master File threshold are still expected to retain contemporaneous transfer pricing documentation to eliminate or reduce penalties.

Economic substance scope: Korea does not operate a Gulf-style Economic Substance Regulations regime, but the National Tax Service applies the statutory substance-over-form doctrine, the general anti-abuse rule, and DTA beneficial ownership requirements. A Yuhan Hoesa used merely as a booking shell, without genuine board governance, employees, business premises, contracts, accounting records, or operational decision-making in Korea, may be disregarded for tax purposes; treaty benefits are denied, intercompany deductions are disallowed, and the profits are recharacterized as Korean-source income.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Unreported Digital Sales

Statutory sanction: 40% fraudulent understatement penalty on the underpaid corporate tax, daily late-payment interest, seizure of bank and payment processor records, and criminal prosecution under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act with imprisonment of up to 5 years and a fine of up to three times the evaded tax.

Director personal liability: Representative directors and controlling shareholders may be jointly and severally liable for unpaid national and local corporate taxes, penalty surcharges, and interest; the court may issue travel bans, provisional asset seizures, and imprisonment upon conviction.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation and Related-Party Mispricing

Statutory sanction: National Tax Service adjustment to the arm's-length consideration, additional tax of up to 40% of the underreported tax, daily delinquency interest, disallowance of royalty or service fee deductions, CFC attribution of deferred foreign income, and separate TP documentation penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors who approve or sign false transfer pricing documentation may be treated as willful tax evaders; they face joint liability for the assessed deficiency, exit bans while the case is pending, criminal referral for tax fraud, and personal liability for related penalties.
Shell Company and Economic Substance Failure

Statutory sanction: The National Tax Service disregards the Korean legal entity form, denies deductions for undocumented expenses, reattributes shifted profit to the Korean taxpayer, imposes the 40% unreasonable claim penalty, cancels treaty benefits, and may cause the company to be administratively struck off for failure of substance.

Director personal liability: Courts pierce the corporate veil where the entity is an alter ego, making directors jointly liable for corporate taxes and penalties; travel restrictions, seizure of foreign bank accounts, and criminal liability for forged board minutes or invoices may follow.
Profit Repatriation and Withholding Tax Violations

Statutory sanction: Reclassification of the amount as a taxable dividend, assessment of 22% withholding tax on the gross distribution, under-withholding penalties, daily late-payment interest, denial of treaty relief, and refusal of expense deductions for fictitious intercompany payments.

Director personal liability: The representative director acting as withholding agent is personally liable for unpaid non-resident withholding tax and penalties; willful failure may trigger criminal sanctions, passport cancellation, and collection of foreign assets through international exchange of information.
Anti-Money Laundering and Foreign Exchange Red Flags

Statutory sanction: Sanctions under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act and anti-money laundering laws include administrative fines of up to KRW 100 million, imprisonment of up to 5 years for unlicensed money transmission, forfeiture of laundered proceeds, and revocation of business payment-rail access.

Director personal liability: Officers who direct or benefit from structured transactions face personal fines, confiscation orders, travel bans, and criminal conviction leading to disqualification from serving as directors of any Korean company.
