Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

No statutory minimum paid-up capital exists for a Chusik Hoesa. The Commercial Act requires that every issued share be validly subscribed and fully paid for before the incorporation registration is filed; shares may have a par value of at least KRW 100. The subscription proceeds must be deposited into a blocked escrow-style stock subscription account at a Korean commercial bank and may be withdrawn only after the incorporation registration is completed. Non-resident ownership may be 100% without local residency, but the bank and the registry require KYC, source-of-funds evidence, and apostilled/notarized corporate documents. Korean court registration fees are not a flat USD amount: the minimum court schedule is KRW 37,500 plus a 20% education surcharge and an ad valorem component of 0.4% of stated capital, so the 0 value in the machine-readable fee field reflects the absence of a stated capital amount in the payload rather than an absence of any fee.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

National Tax Service (NTS) of the Republic of Korea, operating the HomeTax System for electronic tax administration (www.hometax.go.kr). Korea Customs Service (KCS) via UNI-PASS platform (unipass.customs.go.kr) is the competent authority for export declarations and customs validation.

E-government filing portal

Step-by-step SOP for a non-resident foreign entity to obtain a Business Registration Number (사업자등록번호) which serves as the unified TIN/VAT ID: (1) Determine whether a Korean place of business or permanent establishment exists, as under the Value-Added Tax Act any foreign business making taxable supplies in Korea must register before commencement. (2) Appoint a Korean tax representative (tax agent) unless a domestic branch with an authorized direct representative is established. (3) Assemble the required statutory documents: foreign certificate of registration or incorporation, articles of association, board resolution authorizing the Korean registration, proof of foreign tax residence, power of attorney of the tax representative, and a lease or other evidence of the Korean business address. (4) Submit the Business Registration Application (사업자등록신청서) electronically via HomeTax (using a locally accepted digital certificate) or in person at the competent district tax office; the application may be filed by the tax representative. (5) NTS validates the application and issues the Business Registration Certificate (사업자등록증), documenting a unique 10-digit Business Registration Number that is the taxpayer's TIN, VAT ID, and corporate identity. (6) If the foreign entity is not carrying on any domestic taxable business but only exports from Korea, it may still be required to register for VAT refund purposes or, alternatively, to use zero-rating documentation without further VAT registration; a customs-based unique exporter number may also be issued by KCS.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

10%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

KRW 0 (no minimum turnover threshold). Any business, domestic or foreign, engaged in taxable supplies in Korea must register for VAT on the commencement of activity; there is no small-supplier exemption, though the 'simplified taxpayer' status is available only to certain small individual businesses and not to corporations.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Under Article 21 of the Korean Value-Added Tax Act, supplies of goods physically exported from Korea are zero-rated. For online retail of automotive aftermarket parts, zero-rating applies when: (i) the sale contract is concluded with a customer outside Korea; (ii) the goods are directly shipped from Korea to a foreign destination; and (iii) an export customs declaration has been accepted by Korea Customs Service. Services (if any) are zero-rated only when performed entirely outside Korea or supplied to a non-resident customer in connection with exported goods. Where goods are fulfilled from an overseas warehouse without any export movement from Korea, the transaction is outside the scope of Korean VAT and cannot be zero-rated. The seller must substantiate the zero-rating by demonstrating actual physical export, invoicing, and receipt of proceeds in foreign currency.

  • Export Customs Declaration (Export Declaration Certificate) issued by Korea Customs Service (KCS) via UNI-PASS
  • Bill of Lading (B/L) or Airway Bill (AWB) evidencing outbound shipment from Korea
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (IRC) issued by a Korean foreign exchange bank, or a Foreign Exchange Purchase/Withdrawal Certificate confirming the receipt of export proceeds in convertible foreign currency
  • Commercial Invoice and Packing List
  • Electronic Tax Invoice (e-invoice) transmitted via HomeTax showing the zero-rated export supply

All business-to-business (B2B) VAT invoices must be issued as electronic tax invoices (전자세금계산서) through the National Tax Service HomeTax system. The invoice data is transmitted to the NTS in real time (or within one business day) using approved EDI, sFTP, or HomeTax Open API. Each e-invoice is verified by HomeTax and carries a unique digital signature and encrypted authentication stamp. Since 1 July 2021, e-invoicing is mandatory for all business taxpayers without exception. Late or non-transmission of e-invoices triggers administrative penalties, and the input VAT deduction may be denied if the e-invoice is not properly cleared.

The National Tax Service and Korea Customs Service jointly audit export zero-rating claims using risk-based selection. If the required documentation is missing, forged, or the foreign exchange proceeds are not repatriated within the statutory 180-day deadline, the NTS rescinds zero-rating and retroactively assesses VAT at the standard 10% rate plus interest, and imposes additional tax penalties: 20% of the underpaying VAT for incorrect or late reporting, and 40% for fraudulent non-payment. Separate penalties apply for failing to issue electronic tax invoices (up to 1% of supply value) and for missing export documents (2% of export value). In cases of fabricated exports or over-invoicing, criminal referral under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act may lead to imprisonment up to five years or a fine of up to KRW 100 million.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Korean won is generally convertible for current account transactions, including cross-border e-commerce trade payments, service fees, and operational expenses, subject to the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act and the reporting regime administered through designated foreign exchange banks. Capital account transactions operate under a negative-list system, requiring prior or simultaneous filing through the Foreign Exchange Information System (FEIS) for specified categories. Direct equity subscription in a Chusik Hoesa by a non-resident is permitted under the Foreign Direct Investment Promotion Act, with no statutory minimum capital and no prohibition on 100% foreign ownership. Profit remittances, dividends, and residual capital liquidation proceeds are freely transferable after tax clearance and after submission of the required foreign currency remittance report through a designated foreign exchange bank. Repatriation hurdles are predominantly documentary: the bank will require evidence of the original FDI or capital acquisition report, audited financial statements or tax certificates confirming distributable profits, and a valid withholding tax certificate. Surrender requirements do not apply generally to export proceeds; companies may maintain foreign currency accounts, convert at market rates, and use the funds for offshore settlement as long as each transaction is recorded. Overall, South Korea is not a stringent exchange-control jurisdiction for ordinary trade, but strict documentation, reporting thresholds, and licensed financial channels create operational compliance burdens.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Export proceeds on goods exported by the online auto-parts retailer must be collected through a designated foreign exchange bank within 6 months from the date of export clearance unless the bank or MOEF approves an extended payment term. The mandatory conversion ratio is 0%; no statutory percentage of export foreign currency receipts must be sold to the central bank. All export proceeds may be retained in a foreign currency account and converted to Korean won at the market rate. The export settlement receipt and any failure to collect within the statutory period must be reported or explained through the FEIS and the bank's electronic foreign exchange reporting system.

Central bank supervision: The Bank of Korea (BOK) is the central bank and monetary authority; foreign exchange policy is administered by the Ministry of Economy and Finance (MOEF), with banking and foreign exchange business supervision conducted by the Financial Services Commission (FSC) and the Financial Supervisory Service (FSS).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Low viability for domestic treasury accounts. Under Korean banking law, only institutions licensed by the Financial Services Commission can receive customer deposits and operate Korean won IBAN-equivalent clearing accounts. Foreign fintech EMIs such as Wise, Airwallex, Stripe Treasury, or PayPal treasury services are not licensed deposit-taking institutions in South Korea and are legally barred from providing primary local KRW settlement, escrow, or corporate clearing services to a domestic Chusik Hoesa. They may be used only as offshore FX collection and payout facilitators, while the KRW clearing leg must be executed through a licensed tier-1 Korean commercial bank. The entity should therefore build its treasury infrastructure on a domestic bank rather than a fintech EMI.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

A foreign investor subscribing to shares in the KR Chusik Hoesa should, at the appropriate threshold, either file a foreign direct investment report with MOEF through a designated foreign exchange bank or file a capital acquisition report under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act. The bank assigns an FEIS reference and provides the exact foreign exchange purpose code to be used on the SWIFT wire. The remitter must be the foreign shareholder in exactly the same name as the shareholder registry, and third-party remitters are not permitted. Funds can be sent in USD or KRW; if USD, the receiving bank will convert at the market rate and credit the capital escrow or operational account. After settlement, the bank issues a foreign exchange purchase/transfer certificate, the Chusik Hoesa completes the share issuance registration with the court registry if required, and the paid-in capital statement is used for capital verification. Any credit, loan, or quasi-capital injection must be separately documented and reported to the designated bank.For outbound settlement, the Korean bank will execute SWIFT MT103 wire transfers for small recurring supplier payments, using import declaration numbers and supplier invoices as underlying documents. For large or high-value auto-parts purchases, an irrevocable commercial letter of credit or documentary collection will be opened to provide payment security. Korean won disbursements to domestic suppliers clear through BOK-Wire+ and local account transfers; foreign currency payments to overseas suppliers are made from the entity's foreign currency settlement account or through a spot FX conversion. Each outward remittance must cite the correct trade purpose code and be supported by the purchase order, commercial invoice, packing list, customs declaration, and any technology or license agreement for branded tuning components. Stripe and PayPal are accepted only as customer card-acquiring rails, not as treasury clearing rails.Onshore Korean won/USD forward contracts with a designated foreign exchange bank, permitted only when the Chusik Hoesa can evidence a real commercial trade or settlement exposure.Non-deliverable forwards (NDFs) offshore, subject to Bank of Korea and FSC limitations; these are generally available only to non-residents or foreign branches and are not a default hedging tool for a domestic operating entity.Currency swaps with the licensed domestic bank for matching foreign currency working capital and Korean won funding, requiring underlying FDI or trade documentation.Plain-vanilla FX options such as put and call structures on customers or suppliers, limited to transactions with documented underlying trade flows and approved through the bank's derivative eligibility assessment.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Low viability for domestic treasury accounts. Under Korean banking law, only institutions licensed by the Financial Services Commission can receive customer deposits and operate Korean won IBAN-equivalent clearing accounts. Foreign fintech EMIs such as Wise, Airwallex, Stripe Treasury, or PayPal treasury services are not licensed deposit-taking institutions in South Korea and are legally barred from providing primary local KRW settlement, escrow, or corporate clearing services to a domestic Chusik Hoesa. They may be used only as offshore FX collection and payout facilitators, while the KRW clearing leg must be executed through a licensed tier-1 Korean commercial bank. The entity should therefore build its treasury infrastructure on a domestic bank rather than a fintech EMI.

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

24% national corporate income tax on taxable income exceeding KRW 30 billion; progressive schedule: 9% on income up to KRW 200 million, 19% on KRW 200 million to KRW 2 billion, 21% on KRW 2 billion to KRW 30 billion; plus 10% local corporate surtax on national tax (top combined effective rate 26.4%)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Non-resident corporate shareholders are subject to withholding tax of 20% on gross dividends plus a 2% local surtax, resulting in an aggregate statutory rate of 22%. Resident domestic corporate recipients are normally not subject to dividend WHT because of the dividend-received deduction.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: A Chusik Hoesa must submit a local file when annual cross-border related-party transactions equal or exceed KRW 10 billion. A master file is additionally required when such transactions equal or exceed KRW 100 billion. Below these thresholds, the taxpayer must still prepare a contemporaneous pricing file and submit the foreign related-party transaction statement when transactions exceed KRW 500 million.

Economic substance scope: Korea does not have a dedicated Economic Substance Regulation code. However, economic substance is required through multiple anti-avoidance doctrines: (i) beneficial ownership requirement for treaty relief; (ii) General Anti-Abuse Rule under Framework Act on National Taxes Article 14; (iii) OECD MLI Principal Purpose Test; and (iv) Controlled Foreign Corporation (CFC) rules for low-tax foreign subsidiaries with insufficient substance. A paper Chusik Hoesa with no office, employees, inventory, or active retail operations in Korea may be disregarded or treated as a conduit, with resultant penalties and deemed withholding.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Fraudulent Under-reporting

Statutory sanction: Re-assessment with 40% fraudulent understatement penalty, daily overdue interest, asset seizure, and criminal prosecution under the Punishment of Tax Evasion Act carrying imprisonment up to 5 years and fines up to twice the evaded tax.

Director personal liability: Representative directors and CFOs who direct or knowingly oversee tax evasion face personal joint-and-several liability for the unpaid tax, travel bans, attachment of personal bank accounts and property, and possible imprisonment. Corporate-veil piercing occurs when the entity is used as a mere sham conduit.
Shell Company / Absence of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: Korean tax authorities may disregard the shell structure under the General Anti-Abuse Rule, deny treaty withholding relief, re-attribute profit to the beneficial operator, and impose constructive dividend withholding at 22%; CFC legislation may tax retained offline profits with an additional 20% penalty.

Director personal liability: Directors approving the hollow structure are jointly liable for the resulting tax deficiency and penalties. In egregious cases, a travel ban is issued if the director is disposing of KRW 100 million or more in corporate funds to avoid collection, and the court may impose a sentence of imprisonment for deliberate abuse of the corporate form.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Transfer pricing adjustment adding net income, 20% administrative penalty on the under-reported tax from the adjustment, interest on underpayment, and application of the TP substance rule on dormant related-party receivables. Failure to prepare proper documentation can trigger a KRW 10 million fine.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly submit false documents or fail to order a proper TP study are exposed to personal joint liability for the tax deficiency and penalties; habitual violators may lose eligibility for future tax settlement plans and face designation as insolvent taxpayers with travel restrictions.
AML & Payment Rails Misuse

Statutory sanction: Korean Financial Intelligence Unit (KoFIU) can block settlements, impose administrative fines for money-laundering violations, and the tax authorities will impose 20-40% penalties for unreported revenue. Misdeclaration in cross-border customs documentation for imported parts carries additional customs fraud penalties under the Customs Act, up to fines equivalent to five times the evaded duty plus imprisonment.

Director personal liability: The director who personally controls the non-corporate payment rails is treated as the de facto taxpayer, leading to individual assessments; attachment of personal shareholdings, felony counts for concealment of offshore proceeds, and travel bans are typical enforcement actions. Continued non-compliance can result in extradition under bilateral tax information exchange treaties.
