Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

No US-style check-the-box election exists in Korea; a Chusik Hoesa is always classified as a domestic corporation under the Corporate Tax Act and is taxed at entity level. There is no hybrid mismatch classification election. A qualifying parent with wholly-owned Korean subsidiaries may separately apply for the consolidated group taxation system under the Corporate Tax Act before the start of the group tax year, but that is not a check-the-box mechanism and does not alter the default entity-level taxation of the Chusik Hoesa.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

10%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

No turnover threshold; the statutory registration duty arises on the first taxable supply, meaning the threshold is KRW 0. A Chusik Hoesa must obtain a business registration certificate within 20 days after commencing business; the individual simplified taxpayer threshold is irrelevant for a corporation.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

The zero rate of 0% VAT applies to goods physically exported from Korea after a Korean Customs Service export declaration is accepted and the goods depart Korea; to services supplied to a non-resident where the place of supply or performance is outside Korea and the consideration is received in foreign currency from outside Korea; and to prescribed other foreign-currency-earning transactions for non-residents. For a cross-border M&A advisory boutique, the service contract must specify a non-resident client, the substantive engagement must be performed or consumed overseas, and the fee must be wired into a licensed Korean foreign exchange bank account. If the foreign exchange is not collected within 180 days, the supply is recharacterized as a domestic taxable supply at the standard 10% VAT rate unless a statutory exception applies.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Electronic Export Declaration cleared by Korea Customs Service via UNI-PASS, evidencing actual export movement for goods)
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill showing the consignee, port of loading, and foreign destination
  • Commercial invoice and packing list issued for the exported goods or services
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (FIRC) or Korean foreign exchange bank receipt certificate proving the collection and settlement of overseas payment in foreign currency
  • For exported services and digital deliverables: signed engagement letter or M&A advisory agreement, delivery or acceptance report, client confirmation that services were used outside Korea, and the foreign exchange remittance receipt

Korea mandates electronic tax invoices for B2B supplies; e-invoices must be transmitted to the National Tax Service through the HomeTax Electronic Tax Invoice system no later than the following business day. Each e-invoice carries a unique authenticated serial number and electronic signature. B2C cash sales require an electronic cash receipt transmitted through an approved VAN or NTS interface at the point of sale, giving automated daily fiscal reporting. Certified accounting or ERP software must use the HomeTax EDI or API-compatible submission channel. Anti-fraud protection is based on mandatory serialization, system-time stamping in HomeTax, and cross-matching of e-invoices with customs declarations, bank foreign exchange receipts, and VAT returns; there is no separate physical tax stamp.

The National Tax Service compares zero-rated export invoices with UNI-PASS customs data and bank foreign exchange receipts; missing or inconsistent documentation triggers recharacterization of the supply as domestic, with VAT assessed at 10% from the due date plus daily late-payment interest. Additional tax under the Korean Value-Added Tax Act and National Tax Basic Act also applies: 20% of the supply value for issuing a false e-tax invoice, 40% of the underdeclared VAT for intentional fraud, and 10% for negligent underreporting or late filing. Where the 180-day foreign exchange repatriation deadline is missed without approval, the VAT benefit is clawed back. Serious cases are referred for prosecution under the Act on the Punishment of Tax Evaders, and the retroactive assessment period is extended to 10 years for fraudulent or omitted facts.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

South Korea operates a liberalized but diligently regulated foreign exchange regime. Current account transactions, including properly documented cross-border advisory fees, are generally convertible through licensed foreign exchange banks without prior approval, subject to transaction documentation and FEIS reporting. Capital account flows are more constrained: inbound equity subscriptions under the Foreign Investment Promotion Act require a prior foreign investment report, a temporary investment capital account, and issuance confirmation through a domestic foreign exchange bank; outbound investments, loans, and capital-nature payments above established thresholds require supporting contracts, FEIS declarations, and in some instances MOEF pre-notification. The Korean won is not freely convertible for speculative or round-tripping purposes; foreign exchange banks enforce economic-substance tests, FX position ceilings, and strict beneficiary checks. Profit remittance requires audited financial statements, tax clearance, proof of the source of funds, and a foreign exchange bank remittance receipt; applicable withholding taxes must be settled before dividends or success fees are released. There is no hard surrender ratio on export or service proceeds, but any conversion to KRW must be executed through a licensed foreign exchange bank and declared where required. The practical hurdle is documentation completeness rather than blanket legal restrictiveness, particularly when capital is sourced through layered overseas holding vehicles.

Export proceeds surrender rules

The statutory default collection deadline for trade export proceeds is six months from the export declaration date, while service and advisory fees must be collected by the contractual due date. There is no mandatory conversion ratio; 100 percent of export proceeds may be retained in a resident foreign currency account, and if converted, the settlement must be made through a licensed foreign exchange bank under FEIS declaration thresholds. Extensions require the handling foreign exchange bank's prior approval with BOK/MOEF notification.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Korea (BOK) as the central bank, with the Ministry of Economy and Finance (MOEF) as the lead foreign exchange policy authority under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act (FETA) and the Foreign Exchange Information System (FEIS); BOK supervises foreign exchange banks and settlement systems through licensed banks.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low. Offshore digital EMIs are not licensed foreign exchange banks under FETA and cannot provide KRW settlement, FEIS reporting, capital verification, or compliant official remittance rails for a Chusik Hoesa. Domestic internet-only banks such as Kakaobank and Toss Bank are legal banks but are optimized for retail and are not practical for cross-border M&A advisory treasury, foreign investment capital escrow, trade finance, or multi-currency clearing. The corporate banking mandate must be placed with Tier 1 commercial banks with a fully licensed foreign exchange function; fintech EMIs may serve only peripheral local payroll or operating-expense needs.

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

24% top statutory national corporate income tax rate under the progressive 9%, 19%, 21%, and 24% brackets of Article 55 of the Corporate Tax Act; plus 10% local income tax surtax on the national tax due, yielding a 26.4% aggregate top effective burden for non-resident shareholders and domestic entities

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

20% national dividend withholding tax plus 2% local income tax surtax, for a 22% aggregate withholding tax rate on outbound dividends paid by a Korean Chusik Hoesa to non-resident shareholders when no treaty relief applies

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Cross-border intercompany transactions between the Korean entity and foreign related parties aggregating KRW 10 billion or more in a business year require Local File preparation; a Master File is additional required where the Korean entity belongs to a multinational enterprise with group-wide consolidated revenue of KRW 1 trillion or more; the Korean entity must also file the statutory international transaction summary in the corporate return whenever the statutory reporting floor is exceeded.

Economic substance scope: Korea does not impose a UAE-style Economic Substance Regulations label, but the Core Income-Generating Activities doctrine is enforced through the Framework Act on National Taxes Article 17 general anti-abuse rule, the International Tax Coordination Law Articles 6 and 61, OECD MLI principles, and Supreme Court substance-over-form precedent; for this M&A advisory boutique, the CIGA are deal sourcing, financial modeling, negotiation, due diligence, execution, and post-closing advisory; those activities must be performed through real Korean employees, premises, active management, contracts, and bookkeeping in Korea, and any booking of revenue or success fees to a Cayman, Singapore, Hong Kong, or Delaware shell with no actual staff or strategic control will be disregarded and the income re-attributed to Korea.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Under-Filing of Corporate Income Tax

Statutory sanction: Fraudulent omission attracts an additional tax of 40% of underpaid tax plus statutory daily interest; under the Punishment of Tax Evasion Act, the responsible person is subject to imprisonment of up to 5 years or a fine of up to KRW 100 million; the tax authority may also revoke the company's statutory M&A advisory license and disqualify it from participating in regulated cross-border deal structures.

Director personal liability: The director's personal assets may be collected under National Tax Collection Act Article 39 secondary liability for unpaid corporate taxes, and the director is jointly liable for restitution to the company and third parties under Commercial Act Article 401; the director may be placed on the National Tax Service delinquent-payer blacklist, subjected to a departure ban, and held personally liable for civil restitution of the diverted success-fee amount in addition to criminal imprisonment.
Withholding Tax Fraud on Outbound Dividends, Interest, and Royalties

Statutory sanction: The company remains liable for the full 20% national withholding tax plus 2% local surtax; additional withholding penalties of 10% or 20% apply for negligent versus fraudulent failure, together with daily interest; the payment is recharacterized as a dividend and no interest deduction or offset is available.

Director personal liability: The representative director is personally liable for remitting withheld taxes under National Tax Collection Act Article 41, with personal bank accounts and assets subject to seizure; the director is blacklisted, can be prevented from leaving Korea, and may face criminal punishment where the non-remittance is fraudulent or grossly negligent.
Transfer Pricing Mispricing and Shell Arrangement Without Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: The tax authority recomputes taxable income using an arm's-length method such as TNMM or Profit Split under Article 5 of the Law for the Coordination of International Tax Affairs; the adjustment adds 10% penalty on the understated tax, daily interest, and a separate transfer-pricing documentation penalty; the general anti-abuse rule disregards the Delaware shell and attributes the entire success fee to the Korean entity.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly executed the bogus management-rebate arrangement are personally liable for the assessed tax under National Tax Collection Act Article 39, and can be sued for breach of fiduciary duty under Commercial Act Article 401; personal assets funded from the shifted fees must be disgorged; the directors face criminal imprisonment and travel blacklisting where the shell arrangement is proven to be fraudulent, and they cannot invoke the corporate veil to protect personal estates.
