Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

No statutory minimum paid-up capital exists for a Chusik Hoesa under Korean law; however, all subscribed shares must be fully paid before registration of incorporation. The full subscription price must be deposited in a bank or postal savings account opened for the corporation under Article 290 of the Korean Commercial Act, held as a blocked escrow until after the court completes the incorporation registration, and evidenced by a certificate of subscription-money deposit issued by the bank. Non-cash contributions require valuation by a court-appointed appraiser unless a statutory exemption applies.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

National Tax Service (NTS) of the Republic of Korea; online statutory portal: HomeTax (www.hometax.go.kr); export declarations are processed through the UNI-PASS system of Korea Customs Service.

E-government filing portal

A Korean Chusik Hoesa is a domestic legal entity; it obtains a single 10-digit Business Registration Number (TIN/VAT ID) from the competent district tax office of the National Tax Service upon incorporation. For a foreign non-resident entity that is establishing or investing in a Korean corporation, the procedure is: (1) file the foreign investment report under the Foreign Investment Promotion Act with the Korea Trade-Investment Promotion Agency (KOTRA), a foreign exchange bank, or the National Tax Service as applicable; (2) appoint a tax representative or certified tax accountant with Korean residency; (3) submit a business registration application through HomeTax or in person at the district tax office, attaching the articles of incorporation, certificate of corporate registration, evidence of the domestic place of business, lease or ownership documents, and a power of attorney; (4) for a foreign non-resident company with a Korean permanent establishment that is not separately incorporated, complete business registration under Article 8 of the Value Added Tax Act and Article 109 of the Corporate Tax Act, using the Korean place of business as the registration nexus; (5) for a non-resident supplier of electronic services with no Korean establishment, complete the simplified special registration under the Value Added Tax Act through HomeTax after designating a tax representative in Korea; (6) after NTS verification, receive the Business Registration Certificate and the 10-digit Business Registration Number, which serves as the unified identifier for corporate income tax, VAT, withholding, and tax invoice issuance.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation; a Korean Chusik Hoesa is a separate juridical person taxed on its worldwide income under the Corporate Tax Act, with no pass-through treatment and no check-the-box classification.

Available election forms

None for U.S.-style check-the-box entity classification; Korea imposes mandatory entity-level taxation. The principal statutory election available to a Korean parent corporation is the consolidated corporate income tax return regime under the Corporate Tax Act, which may be elected by a parent that directly or indirectly owns 100% of one or more Korean domestic subsidiaries. Hybrid mismatch elections are not recognized; the International Tax Adjustment Act applies automatic anti-hybrid adjustments.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

10%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

Korean domestic corporations and permanent establishments are required to register for VAT from the commencement of any taxable supply in Korea, with no general KRW turnover threshold. For foreign non-resident suppliers of electronically supplied services to Korean individual end users without a Korean establishment, simplified VAT registration becomes mandatory when annual taxable supplies exceed KRW 300,000,000; below that threshold, those suppliers are not required to register.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero rating of 10% VAT applies to exported goods and qualifying internationally rendered services under the Value Added Tax Act. For granular elemental sulfur sold for export, zero rating requires that the goods are physically exported from the Republic of Korea and that an export declaration is accepted by Korea Customs Service. Direct dry bulk marine transport of sulfur ore qualifies as zero-rated international marine transport of goods where the voyage is between a Korean port and a foreign port or between foreign ports. Export brokerage, freight forwarding, and other services supplied to non-residents in connection with international cargo movements may also qualify as zero-rated foreign-exchange-earning services, provided the consideration is received in foreign currency or verified as foreign-exchange earnings. Qualifying offshore digital services supplied to non-resident persons and paid in foreign currency may also be zero-rated under the same internationally rendered services category.

  • Customs Export Declaration accepted through the UNI-PASS system of Korea Customs Service
  • Original Bill of Lading or sea waybill evidencing carriage of granular elemental sulfur to the foreign buyer
  • Commercial invoice and packing list issued by the Korean exporter or Chusik Hoesa as shipper
  • Sales contract or purchase order evidencing the underlying international sale of mineral ore
  • Cargo manifest, mate's receipt, or terminal weight certificate issued by the port authority for dry bulk cargo
  • Inward remittance certificate or bank foreign-exchange receipt confirmation issued by a Korean foreign exchange bank evidencing actual collection of export proceeds

All registered businesses must issue an electronic tax invoice through the NTS HomeTax system within the day following the date of supply; paper tax invoices are generally unavailable to corporate VAT-registered suppliers. Electronic tax invoice data is transmitted to the National Tax Service in near real time, and HomeTax pre-fills VAT returns and reconciles invoice and cash receipt data. Korea does not operate a per-invoice digital stamping system or a separate third-party fiscal clearance API; the HomeTax platform functions as the central fiscal clearance and matching system. Export declarations are transmitted through the UNI-PASS customs platform and linked to zero-rating claims on VAT returns.

If zero-rating is claimed without valid export documentation, or if export proceeds are not collected within the statutory one-year foreign-exchange collection period, the National Tax Service will reclassify the supply as domestic taxable, assess 10% VAT on the full supply value, and impose under-reporting and fraud additional taxes under the Framework Act on National Taxes. Fabricated export declarations, fraudulent bills of lading, or false bank receipts trigger criminal liability under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act, including imprisonment and fines up to multiple times the suppressed tax, and any VAT refund already paid will be retroactively clawed back with statutory interest and additional charges. Korea Customs Service may also verify export declarations through post-clearance audits and deny invalid zero-rating claims.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

South Korea maintains a predominantly liberalised foreign exchange system under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act. Current account transactions are freely convertible, enabling trade settlement for sulfur imports and exports through licensed foreign exchange banks without prior central bank approval. Capital account transactions, however, operate under a notification and registration regime rather than blanket liberalisation. Foreign equity injection into a Chusik Hoesa requires Foreign Investment Notification under the Foreign Investment Promotion Act, followed by remittance reporting via a designated bank. Export proceeds are subject to a statutory collection requirement requiring receipt within six months from export declaration, but there is no automatic surrender ratio; exporters may hold earned foreign currency in domestic foreign currency accounts or convert it to KRW. Profit remittance to foreign shareholders is permitted after audited retained earnings are verified and applicable withholding taxes are paid, but the remittance must be reported through a foreign exchange bank with supporting board resolution and tax clearance. In practice, banks conduct underlying transaction scrutiny to satisfy the real demand principle, and speculative or mischaracterised capital flows are rejected. This creates a low but not absent convertibility friction for trade-heavy treasury operations.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act, exporters must collect foreign currency proceeds within six months from the date of export clearance, with extension possible by application to the Ministry of Economy and Finance through the foreign exchange bank. The exporter must either deposit the foreign currency in a domestic foreign currency account or sell it to a foreign exchange bank. There is no fixed mandatory conversion percentage; however, all receipts must be reported through the Korea Customs Service and the foreign exchange bank. Failure to meet the six-month collection timeline without approval exposes the company to administrative penalties and potential restrictions on future trade settlement.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Korea (The Central Bank of the Republic of Korea), operating in coordination with the Ministry of Economy and Finance and the Korea Customs Service under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Onshore internet-only banks such as KakaoBank and Toss Bank are authorised as banks in South Korea, but their corporate foreign-currency treasury services for non-resident-controlled trading entities remain commercially limited. Offshore digital EMIs and non-bank payment service providers lack a Korean banking licence and cannot independently hold operational KRW or foreign currency accounts or clear SWIFT trade settlements for a Korean entity. The Foreign Exchange Transactions Act requires foreign exchange business to be registered with the Bank of Korea, and a Korean corporate treasury account must be maintained at a licensed domestic financial institution. Therefore, cross-border fintech EMIs have low viability and may only serve as ancillary payment-voucher tools, with a domestic partner bank acting as the clearing custodian.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound capital for equity injection and shareholder loans must be remitted via SWIFT MT103 to a licensed foreign exchange bank in South Korea, with the beneficiary account clearly designated as a capital escrow or foreign currency account. The remitter should use the foreign investment or direct investment payment purpose code required by the Bank of Korea; Korean banks will require the Foreign Investment Notification acceptance confirmation through the foreign exchange bank's information system. For capital verification, the bank must issue a certificate of receipt or foreign currency deposit after funds are credited, and the company must file the inward remittance report with the Bank of Korea through the bank. The funds must not be commingled with trade settlement proceeds, and source-of-wealth evidence from the remitting entity is required before funds are released for operational cash management.For payments to overseas sulfur suppliers or trade creditors, the company will use SWIFT MT103 for direct telegraphic transfers where no letter of credit is in place, attaching the relevant Korean Customs Service import/export declaration number. For credit risk mitigation, Korean commercial banks will issue and pay commercial letters of credit, either sight or usance, conforming to UCP 600. The full documentary set includes commercial invoice, packing list, original bill of lading, certificate of origin, independent weight survey certificate, sulfur quality certificate, insurance certificate, and customs permits. Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Mandatory Local File and Schedule for International Related-Party Transactions are triggered when annual cross-border related-party transactions exceed KRW 50 billion; Master File and Country-by-Country Reporting are additionally required for groups exceeding KRW 100 billion and KRW 125 billion consolidated revenue thresholds respectively; the NTS may still require contemporaneous documentation below these thresholds where transaction value or risk warrants review.

Economic substance scope: The Korean National Tax Service applies economic-substance doctrine through National Tax Basic Act Article 14 and the International Tax Adjustment Act. A Chusik Hoesa trading sulfur mineral ore must conduct Core Income-Generating Activities in Korea: negotiation, vessel charter decision-making, invoicing, price risk assumption, credit risk control, and contract administration. If the entity only holds a bank account and receives a margin while all decisions are made offshore, the NTS will disregard the Korean PE or deny treaty benefits and re-attribute income to the actual decision-maker.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Corporate Income Tax Evasion and offshore invoice manipulation

Statutory sanction: Article 3 of the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act imposes imprisonment of up to seven years and a fine of up to three times the evaded tax; the Basic Act for National Taxes Article 47-3 adds a negligence penalty of 20% for omission and 10-40% for understatement; late payment interest of 0.022% per day is also charged.

Director personal liability: The corporate veil is pierced under Korean Commercial Act Articles 389(3) and 399: directors who intentionally or negligently approved the fraudulent filing are jointly and severally liable to the State and third parties for evaded tax, penalties, and interest. The NTS may issue a departure ban and request criminal indictment; the director may be criminally fined, imprisoned for up to seven years, and ordered to forfeit the diverted assets.
Transfer Pricing documentation failure and artificial margin allocation

Statutory sanction: The International Tax Adjustment Act permits the NTS to make an upward adjustment to the Korean tax base and impose an additional penalty of 30% of the related understated tax for non-substantiation (Article 11); statutory interest of 0.022% per day accrues. Intentional failure may be prosecuted as aggravated tax evasion.

Director personal liability: Directors and tax officers who knowingly sign false transfer pricing documentation are personally liable for damages under Commercial Act Article 401; active involvement in cross-border margin shifting can trigger criminal charges for obstructing tax collection; a large unpaid assessment results in public blacklisting and travel restrictions and may disqualify the director from new credit facilities.
Treaty abuse, shell company formation, and insufficient economic substance

Statutory sanction: The National Tax Basic Act Article 14 substance-over-form rule and the International Tax Adjustment Act Article 17 general anti-abuse rule authorize the NTS to disregard the shell arrangement, recharacterize the income as dividends or Korean-source service income, apply the statutory 22% aggregate withholding, and impose a 40% negligence penalty or deny treaty relief under the Principal Purpose Test and Limitation on Benefits clauses.

Director personal liability: The director of the shell Chusik Hoesa may be held personally and severally liable for the unpaid corporate tax and penalties under Korean Commercial Act Articles 399 and 401. Repeated treaty abuse with fraudulent substantiation can lead to criminal tax fraud charges, imprisonment up to seven years, departure bans, asset attachment, and prohibition from serving as a director for a period that the court, applying the Commercial Act, considers necessary to protect creditors.
