Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of goods from Korea to a foreign country are zero-rated under the Value-Added Tax Act where the supplier retains proof of export declaration and transportation. For B2B MRO hardware shipped from Korea, the 0% rate applies if title and risk pass to the foreign buyer, goods leave the Korean customs territory, and the exporter possesses an accepted UNI-PASS export declaration, commercial invoice, and contract. Qualifying offshore digital services and services used wholly outside Korea may likewise be zero-rated. The Korean exporter must also comply with foreign-exchange settlement requirements to evidence receipt of export proceeds, and any blocked input VAT attributable to the zero-rated export is claimable as a refund through periodic VAT returns.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Bill of Entry) - Korea Customs Service UNI-PASS export declaration
  • Bill of Lading (or air waybill for air cargo) issued in the name of the Korean exporter
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (FIRC) or Korean foreign exchange bank statement evidencing collection of export proceeds

Mandatory e-Tax Invoice issuance for all business-to-business VAT supplies. The supplier must transmit the e-Tax Invoice to NTS HomeTax by the day after the supply; NTS operates real-time validation, cross-referencing purchase and sale ledgers. Large suppliers can integrate via NTS Open API or a licensed Value-Added Network (VAN) provider. No physical fiscal stamping is applied; anti-fraud control is performed through unique e-Tax Invoice numbers and automated mismatch detection.

Export zero-rating is verified ex post. Any deviation between UNI-PASS declarations, Bill of Lading data, and bank remittance records will cause NTS to disallow the 0% rate and assess the 10% VAT, plus additional tax under the Framework Act on National Taxes and, for willful evasion, criminal penalties under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act. Retroactive clawback of previously credited input VAT refunds and foreign-exchange penalties under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act may apply for failure to repatriate proceeds within the statutory period.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Republic of Korea operates a highly liberalised but administratively supervised convertible currency regime. Current account transactions such as trade in goods, service fees, royalties, and ordinary operating remittances are fully convertible without quantitative restrictions; however, each transaction must be reported and processed through a locally registered financial institution under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act. Capital account transactions, including foreign direct investment (FDI) equity injections, loans, and portfolio investments, are broadly permitted for a corporation but require mandatory initial and post-investment reporting through the FEIS, an investment registration certificate for the foreign investor, and a valid taxpayer identity certificate. There is no statutory surrender or forced conversion of export proceeds, but exporters must repatriate foreign currency receivables within two years from the export declaration date unless an extension is granted by the MOEF. Profit remittances (dividends, branch earnings, or liquidation proceeds) are freely convertible after corporate income tax, with the foreign investor required to submit a withholding tax certificate, audited financial statements, and evidence of the foreign-investment registration; failure to present these documents results in administrative blocking by the domestic bank. The practical hurdles are less about currency convertibility and more about documentary precision, registration timing, and the necessity to route every cross-border transfer through a Korean Foreign Exchange Bank. Overall, the KRW is fully convertible for both current and capital purposes of a foreign-owned Chusik Hoesa, subject to a robust filing architecture rather than direct exchange controls.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No mandatory conversion ratio applies to export proceeds; the statutory requirement is repatriation, not surrender. Exporters must collect and repatriate foreign exchange earned from exports within two years from the date of the export declaration (extendable to five years for vessel or equipment exports with MOEF approval). The exporter may retain 100% of the foreign currency in a domestic foreign-currency deposit account or convert it to KRW at the market rate through an authorised Foreign Exchange Bank, but every receipt must be reported to the FEIS within 30 days of receipt.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Low feasibility for pure offshore digital EMI platforms (e.g., Wise, Revolut, or unlicensed global payment wallets). Under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act, only licensed foreign exchange business institutions (Korean banks with global business licences or Money Exchange Business registrees) can operate an FEIS-reportable account for a Korean resident entity. Offshore EMIs cannot provide KRW IBAN/account numbers, cannot issue statutory Korean bank statements for customs or capital verification, and cannot serve as an account designated for the foreign-investment registration process. Such EMIs may act only as a supplementary payment execution node for outbound GBP/USD payments if the primary settlement account remains at a domestic Tier-1 bank. For a Chusik Hoesa requiring orderly FDI registration and capital verification, all inward capital injections must clear through a registered Korean foreign-exchange bank; an offshore EMI would trigger reporting failure and potential penalty if used for capital subscription.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inward FDI capital must be remitted via SWIFT MT103 from the foreign shareholder's bank to the Korean escrow bank designated in the Foreign Investment Registration Report. The remittance must carry a clear beneficiary name matching the Korean entity, a purpose of payment code under the Bank of Korea classification (usually 'FDIX' or '133010' for equity investment), and must be accompanied by a copy of the Foreign Investment Registration Certificate (or report number) sent via SWIFT field 70/72. The receiving Korean domestic bank will provisionally credit the capital escrow account, verify the foreign exchange declaration in the FEIS, and then issue a Certificate of Capital Introduction ('Foreign Capital Import') which is essential for annual customs verification and tax clearance. The company must then have the bank complete a 'Settlement of Foreign Exchange' report within 30 days and transfer the funds into an operational KRW account after exercising the currency conversion. Funds must not be fragmented across multiple accounts; a single designated escrow account should be used to maintain a clean audit trail.For payment of industrial MRO inventory to international suppliers, the preferred clearing rail is an SWIFT MT103 documentary cheque for amounts under USD 200,000, with a commercial invoice and purchase order attached to the bank remitting. For higher-value or structured shipments, a Uniform Rules for Demand Guarantee (URDG) backed Commercial Letter of Credit (MT700 issue / MT754 advice) is advisable, with a beneficiary bank at the supplier's side receiving a presentation of transport documents, packing lists, and insurance certificates. Documentary Collections (URC 522, DP/DA) remain permissible but require a formal collection instruction to the supplier's bank; these carry higher counterparty risk and are used only for trust-tested suppliers. All outbound payments must be submitted with a 'Foreign Exchange Business Reporting' form, quoting the corresponding purpose code linked to the trade (e.g., 'Measurement & Control devices under ISIC 4791 MRO'), and the bank will file the FEIS transaction report. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Low feasibility for pure offshore digital EMI platforms (e.g., Wise, Revolut, or unlicensed global payment wallets). Under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act, only licensed foreign exchange business institutions (Korean banks with global business licences or Money Exchange Business registrees) can operate an FEIS-reportable account for a Korean resident entity. Offshore EMIs cannot provide KRW IBAN/account numbers, cannot issue statutory Korean bank statements for customs or capital verification, and cannot serve as an account designated for the foreign-investment registration process. Such EMIs may act only as a supplementary payment execution node for outbound GBP/USD payments if the primary settlement account remains at a domestic Tier-1 bank. For a Chusik Hoesa requiring orderly FDI registration and capital verification, all inward capital injections must clear through a registered Korean foreign-exchange bank; an offshore EMI would trigger reporting failure and potential penalty if used for capital subscription.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

24% top marginal rate; progressive brackets apply: 9% on taxable income up to KRW 200,000,000; 19% on KRW 200,000,001 to KRW 20,000,000,000; 21% on KRW 20,000,000,001 to KRW 300,000,000,000; 24% above KRW 300,000,000,000.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

20% national withholding tax plus a 10% local income tax surcharge on the national tax (2% effective), resulting in a total statutory dividend withholding rate of 22% on gross distributions to non-resident corporate shareholders.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: KRW 10 billion aggregate foreign related-party transactions triggers mandatory preparation of a Local File and Master File. A transfer pricing disclosure summary must accompany the corporate tax return when cross-border related-party transactions reach KRW 5 billion. A Country-by-Country Report is due for multinational groups with consolidated group revenue exceeding KRW 1 trillion.

Economic substance scope: South Korea does not maintain a standalone Economic Substance Regulations regime, but economic substance is effectively enforced through the Corporate Tax Act General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR), the International Tax Coordination Act CFC rules, and treaty anti-abuse doctrines including the Principal Purpose Test and beneficial ownership requirements. A Chusik Hoesa lacking local employees, offices, board activity, and substantive operational control cannot rely on paper presence; transactions may be recharacterized, treaty benefits denied, and deductions disallowed.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Fraudulent Return

Statutory sanction: Under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act, imprisonment of up to 5 years or a fine of up to KRW 50 million is imposed for fraudulent evasion; in aggravated or repeated cases the penalty increases to up to 10 years' imprisonment and a fine not exceeding KRW 100 million, in addition to full tax assessment, surcharges, and 40% unjust-evasion penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors who participate in, direct, or knowingly benefit from tax evasion are jointly and severally liable for the evaded tax and penalties. Courts may pierce the corporate veil, order civil restitution, impose immediate departure bans, freeze personal bank accounts, and imprison responsible directors upon conviction.
Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: The National Tax Service may invoke the GAAR to recharacterize the transactions, deny expense deductions, impose a 40% penalty on unjust underpricing, deny foreign tax credits, and revoke treaty shelter benefits. Corporate registration itself may be administratively closed for chronically failing to satisfy the substantial business purpose test.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally assessed for the resulting unpaid corporate tax and withholding obligations as management officers responsible for compliance. Asset preservation orders, travel restrictions, and criminal liability for aiding profit-shifting are applied where the shell arrangement is used for intentional tax evasion.
Transfer Pricing and BEPS Violation

Statutory sanction: The tax authority may make a transfer pricing adjustment recalculating Korean-source taxable income, add unpaid corporate income tax plus daily late-payment interest of 0.022%, impose an understatement penalty of up to 30% or 40% for unjust deficiency, and levy an administrative fine of up to KRW 30 million for missing or late TP documentation.

Director personal liability: Directors who certified inaccurate TP records or who knowingly approved profit-stripping transactions are individually liable for the resultant tax deficit to the extent of their fault, may be subject to civil restitution claims by creditors or minority shareholders, and may face travel bans while under investigation, plus imprisonment in fraud-aggravated cases.
Anti-Money Laundering / Abusive Payment Rail Use

Statutory sanction: The Financial Transactions Reports Act imposes administrative fines up to KRW 50 million for AML compliance failures and KRW 100 million for missing suspicious transaction reports. Unlicensed foreign exchange business under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act carries imprisonment of up to 3 years or a fine of up to KRW 30 million, plus possible confiscation of transaction proceeds.

Director personal liability: Directors who allow the corporate treasury to be used for third-party money transmission can be held criminally liable for aiding unlicensed remittance and money laundering; they face personal disgorgement, civil liability to defrauded counterparties, travel bans, and imprisonment in aggravated money laundering conspiracies.
Withholding and Repatriation Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: The National Tax Service assesses the entire unreported withholding tax on the gross remittance, adds a 20% non-withholding penalty or a 10% underreporting penalty, and charges daily late-payment interest. Criminal fines apply for intentional failure to withhold where the amount exceeds the criminal threshold.

Director personal liability: Directors are personally liable for the payment of withheld taxes that were lawfully collected but not remitted to the Treasury. Repeated violations can trigger piercing of the corporate veil, civil restitution orders, travel bans, and imprisonment of the responsible officers under the Joint Penal Provisions of the Framework Act on National Taxes.
