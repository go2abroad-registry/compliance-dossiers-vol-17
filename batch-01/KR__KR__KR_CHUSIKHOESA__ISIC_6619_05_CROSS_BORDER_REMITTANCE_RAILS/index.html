Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation - The Korean Chusik Hoesa is a separate juridical person and is taxed at entity level under the Corporate Tax Act on its worldwide income; no pass-through treatment is available.

Available election forms

None. Korea does not administer a check-the-box classification regime or a hybrid mismatch election. A domestic corporate group may elect the consolidated corporate tax return regime under the Corporate Tax Act for wholly-owned Korean resident subsidiaries, but this does not reclassify the Chusik Hoesa's separate entity status and does not modify its default entity-level tax treatment.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

10%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

No monetary threshold for a corporation: every taxable business operator, including a Chusik Hoesa, must register with the National Tax Service from the commencement of taxable business activities. The KRW 80,000,000 annual revenue simplified-taxpayer threshold applies only to individual proprietors, not to stock corporations.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating under the zero-rate provisions of the Value-Added Tax Act is available for (i) goods exported from Korea and (ii) services supplied for use outside Korea to a non-resident, including qualifying exported digital and data-processing services, provided the supplier maintains documentation proving the foreign recipient and overseas place of use. The Chusik Hoesa's core cross-border remittance and money-transfer fee is an exempt financial service rather than a zero-rated service; therefore no input VAT recovery is available on that exempt revenue. Separately invoiced exported software-as-a-service, data-processing, or consulting services may qualify for zero-rating if the foreign-consumption and foreign-currency payment criteria are met.

  • Korea Customs Service export declaration / export clearance record generated through UNI-PASS (export report for goods).
  • Commercial invoice and export contract or purchase order identifying the foreign buyer and the goods/services supplied.
  • Ocean Bill of Lading, Air Waybill, or multimodal transport document (or electronic equivalent) evidencing physical export movement of goods.
  • Foreign exchange bank foreign-currency receipt certificate or trade settlement confirmation issued by a Korean Foreign Exchange Bank evidencing repatriation of export proceeds in foreign currency.
  • For exported services: written service contract, service performance report, foreign-currency invoice, and records demonstrating that the service was consumed outside Korea by the non-resident recipient.

Korea imposes a mandatory electronic tax invoice (e-invoice) transmission obligation on VAT taxpayers required to issue tax invoices. E-invoices must be transmitted to the National Tax Service through the HomeTax e-invoice system within the statutory time limits set by the Value-Added Tax Act and its Enforcement Decree; the NTS provides OpenAPI and bulk XML interfaces for such transmission. There is no real-time pre-clearance or anti-fraud digital stamping requirement of the type used in some other jurisdictions; the NTS instead uses ex-post e-invoice matching, cash receipt issuance requirements for consumer transactions, and tax invoice penalty rules as the anti-fraud control framework.

If the National Tax Service disallows a zero-rating claim due to missing or false export declarations, transport documents, foreign-currency remittance evidence, or overseas-consumption proof, it will reassess the headline VAT at 10% on the underlying supply. Additional tax under Korean VAT law is applied at 10% of the underpaid tax for negligent understatement and up to 40% for intentional understatement; overdue interest runs under the Framework Act on National Taxes. The statutory limitation period is generally five years, extended to ten years for fraudulent tax evasion, so zero-rating relief can be clawed back retrospectively. False or forged export documentation also exposes the company to Korea Customs Service penalties and criminal liability under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act, including imprisonment or fines.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Republic of Korea maintains a fully convertible current account for trade, service, and income transactions under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act. Capital account operations are liberalized but remain subject to prior notification, post-transaction reporting, and licensed intermediary execution. There is no mandatory surrender requirement for export foreign currency earnings; residents may retain foreign currency in domestic foreign exchange accounts. Profit remittance by foreign-owned Korean companies is permitted through foreign exchange banks upon submission of audited financial statements, tax clearance certificates, and dividend distribution minutes. For expatriate remittance business, additional friction arises because the entity must obtain a Foreign Exchange Business Operator registration from BOK before onboarding remittance partners; without this license, banking counterparties may freeze inbound clearing funds. Temporary BOK prudential measures can be reintroduced for monetary stability, but the current regime imposes no commission-based conversion cap. Therefore, the blueprint should treat Korean won as freely convertible for operating purposes, while maintaining full documentary trail of each remittance to survive BOK inspection.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no mandatory surrender rule and no fixed conversion ratio for export proceeds in Korea. Under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act, export receipts from overseas sales must be settled and received into a domestic foreign exchange bank account within five years from the export declaration date (goods) or from the service completion date (services), unless an extension is granted by the Bank of Korea; exporters may retain the foreign currency without conversion into Korean won. Mandatory conversion ratio: 0 percent.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Korea (BOK) acting under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act and delegated enforcement rules of the Ministry of Economy and Finance

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low. Offshore digital EMI institutions without a Korean license cannot legally service Korean entities or hold local settlement deposits because the Bank of Korea restricts money transmission and electronic pre-paid payment services to institutions licensed under Korea's Electronic Financial Transactions Act and registered as Foreign Exchange Business Operators. An offshore EMI may only act as an upstream correspondent, never as the onshore account-holding institution, and would require a branch or subsidiary licensed by the Financial Services Commission.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

National corporate income tax: 9% up to KRW 200 million; 19% over KRW 200 million to KRW 20 billion; 21% over KRW 20 billion to KRW 300 billion; 24% over KRW 300 billion, plus 10% local income tax surcharge on the national tax, producing a top aggregate statutory rate of 26.4% for non-resident-owned Chusik Hoesa corporations with a domestic place of business.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

22% computed as 20% national withholding tax plus 2% local income tax surcharge; dividend withholding is triggered by shareholder resolution at the time the dividend is confirmed, and the withholding agent must remit the tax to the National Tax Service by the 10th day of the month following the month in which the dividend is distributed.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: KRW 5,000,000,000 aggregate annual cross-border related-party transactions requires submission of the Korean Local File with the annual corporate return; a consolidated multinational group revenue threshold of KRW 100,000,000,000 additionally requires the Master File; the thresholds are monitored separately for goods, services, intangibles, and financing transactions.

Economic substance scope: South Korea does not maintain a UAE-style Economic Substance Regulations regime, but the Framework Act on National Taxes and the Framework Act on International Tax Adjustment enforce substance-over-form and the OECD principal purpose test; a remittance Chusik Hoesa must show real office premises, resident local employees, substantive decisions by Korean directors, actual execution of the remittance SWIFT and cash-pickup contracts, and core income-generating activities inside Korea, otherwise the tax office may deny treaty benefits, recharacterize the entity as a conduit, and deem the profits to be directly attributable to the foreign owner.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion through sham cross-border income and false return filings

Statutory sanction: Article 3 of the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act imposes imprisonment up to 10 years or a fine up to 3 times the amount of corporate income tax evaded; the Framework Act on National Taxes Article 47-3 imposes a 20% or 40% negligent or fraudulent additional tax; and the National Tax Service may assess the full amount with overdue interest.

Director personal liability: Korean courts will disregard the limited liability shield of a Chusik Hoesa when a director misuses the entity as a sham or fraudulent facade; under Framework Act on National Taxes Article 39, the director can become secondarily liable for unpaid corporate withholding and corporate taxes to the extent of the corporation's unpaid liabilities; under the National Tax Collection Act Article 10-9 a director can be subjected to a departure ban, asset seizure, and travel blacklist; and a director who signs false returns is exposed to personal criminal imprisonment in egregious cases.
Treaty shopping, conduit shell, and abuse of double tax treaty benefits

Statutory sanction: The Framework Act on International Tax Adjustment Article 2, Article 3, and anti-abuse provisions authorize the National Tax Service to deny treaty benefits and reassess 22% withholding tax; additional tax of up to 40% for unfaithful returns plus overdue interest applies; and Korean Supreme Court decisions require the beneficial-ownership interpretation for DTA claims.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly structure a conduit receive personal civil liability for repatriated profits and are subject to statutory officer joint liability; the court may pierce the corporate veil and order the director to return the dividend or royalty to the Korean corporation; a director can be blacklisted from foreign currency transactions, prohibited from serving as a director in the financial or remittance industry, and criminally prosecuted for aiding tax fraud, including imprisonment in aggravated cases.
Unlicensed money transmission and AML/CFT red flags in cross-border remittance rails

Statutory sanction: Under Article 32 of the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act, an unregistered foreign exchange business is criminally sanctioned by imprisonment up to 5 years or a fine up to KRW 300 million; the Financial Information Act AML regime imposes fines of up to KRW 100 million per failure to report suspicious transactions, in addition to seizure of illegal foreign exchange profits; regulatory authorities may revoke the bank license of an affiliated institution and the entity may be dissolved by the court.

Director personal liability: Directors are treated as principals of the corporate crime under Korean criminal law and can be jailed if the board knew of or controlled the unlicensed remittance network; the Financial Services Commission may impose an executive prohibition order, and the Korea FIU may issue an asset freeze and travel-ban recommendation; civil liability includes restitution of all unlawfully collected remittance fees and amounts transmitted through the unlicensed rails, and the corporate veil will be pierced when the company exists only to evade licensing and AML statutes.
