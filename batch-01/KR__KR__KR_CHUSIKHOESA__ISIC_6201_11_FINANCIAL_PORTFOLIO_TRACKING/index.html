Registered Agent & Physical Address Licensed Agent Required

A virtual office or commercial mailbox is not categorically prohibited by the Korean Commercial Act for filing the principal office address, but it is operationally and commercially impermissible for this low-risk fintech software business. The National Tax Service and the Financial Supervisory Service apply a substance-over-form standard, requiring actual business premises where records are kept, clients can be met, IT infrastructure is operated, and employees perform their duties. Korean commercial banks, when opening a corporate account for a foreign-invested Chusik Hoesa, conduct on-site visits and reject virtual-office addresses as a red flag, particularly where the stated business is financial portfolio tracking and payment rails will include Stripe and Apple IAP. A virtual office may be used as a supplementary administrative address, but the registered office should be a physically leased office, at least one member of local staff should be present during business hours, and the company must be able to produce lease agreements, utility receipts, payroll records, and server access logs upon demand. For tax and bank purposes, virtual office and commercial mailbox are therefore not permissible as the sole principal office.

Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

No minimum paid-in capital is imposed by the Commercial Act of the Republic of Korea for a Chusik Hoesa after the removal of the minimum capital requirement. Nevertheless, the entire subscription price for every issued share must be fully paid by the promoters into a domestic commercial bank or securities company account in the name of the company to obtain a statutory payment certificate before incorporation registration. This deposit is not a regulatory blocked escrow account; it is a dedicated incorporation capital deposit account that becomes freely available operating capital only upon successful registration. For the illustrative official state filing fee stated above, a standard paid-in capital of KRW 10,000,000 is assumed; the court filing component and local education surtax vary proportionally with capital.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Value-Added Tax Act Article 21 zero-rates exported goods moved from Korea to a foreign destination under a customs export declaration, and exported services including qualifying digital and information technology services where the place of use or performance is shown to be outside Korea and the recipient is a foreign entity or individual without a Korean tax nexus. For this financial portfolio tracking cloud service, the taxpayer should maintain a contract naming the offshore customer, a service invoice denominated in foreign currency, evidence of actual supply and usage outside Korea, and foreign exchange receipt evidence.

  • Export Customs Declaration, also known as the Export Declaration Certificate, issued through the Korea Customs Service UNI-PASS system with export declaration number and customs clearance seal for goods
  • Commercial Invoice
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill for goods
  • Certificate of Foreign Exchange Receipt or Foreign Currency Inward Remittance Certificate issued by a Korean foreign exchange bank, proving offshore payment proceeds
  • Sales contract or purchase order
  • Electronic tax invoice transmitted to the NTS showing the zero-rated supply
  • For service exports where no customs declaration exists, substitute the customs bundle with the service contract, detailed service report or usage log, foreign exchange receipt, and evidence demonstrating the offshore place of benefit or use

Under Value-Added Tax Act Article 32, VAT-registered business operators must issue electronic tax invoices for all supplies and transmit them to the NTS through HomeTax or a government-approved electronic tax invoice service provider by the day after the supply date. B2C credit/debit card sales are captured automatically from card acquirers and converted into electronic tax invoices by the NTS. There is no continuous fiscal stamping device mandate; anti-fraud control is achieved through mandatory real-time electronic tax invoice transmission, issuance of an NTS digital issuance number, and cross-reconciliation with customs and foreign exchange data.

The NTS audits export zero-rating by cross-checking electronic tax invoices, Korea Customs Service UNI-PASS declarations, and foreign exchange bank receipts. If zero-rating is claimed without valid customs or foreign exchange documentation, the supply is reclassified as taxable at 10% Korean VAT, unpaid VAT is assessed with interest, and additional tax penalties, typically 20% to 40% of the tax amount under the Value-Added Tax Act and Framework Act on National Taxes, are imposed. In cases of intentional or fraudulent false zero-rating, the NTS may apply a 10-year retrospective reassessment period and refer the matter under the Punishment of Tax Evaders Act, which carries imprisonment of up to 10 years or fines of up to five times the evaded tax.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

South Korea operates a liberalized yet heavily monitored foreign exchange regime under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act (FETA). The Korean won (KRW) is fully convertible for current account items, including trade in goods, services, and ordinary business remittances, with minimal formality. In contrast, capital account transactions, such as foreign direct investment, overseas borrowing, and portfolio investment, are subject to ex-ante or ex-post reporting requirements to the Bank of Korea or authorized foreign exchange banks. Since the 2010 reforms, Korea has abolished mandatory surrender requirements for export proceeds; exporters may retain earnings in foreign currency accounts indefinitely, but must report receipt within the statutory reconciliation timeline. Profit remittances, including dividends and liquidation proceeds, are permitted without prior approval once corporate tax and withholding tax obligations are settled and a Foreign Investment Registration Certificate (or its equivalent confirmation) is presented to the paying bank. Non-resident shareholders face statutory withholding rates of up to 22% unless treaty relief is claimed through the appropriate tax residency certificate. The most significant practical hurdle for foreign-owned entities is the requirement to demonstrate the underlying genuine trade or investment purpose for each major FX transaction, as the BOK periodically audits banks for compliance with anti-money laundering and foreign exchange reporting obligations. Additionally, the 'foreign exchange concentration' mechanism restricts pre-announced large conversions above USD 10 million equivalent, requiring simplified but compulsory notice to the BOK. Overall, KRW is not a restricted currency in the official sense, but the documentary burden for capital account movements is substantial, and any deviation from the declared business purpose could trigger civil or criminal penalties for both the entity and its banking counterparty.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No mandatory surrender or fixed conversion ratio applies to export proceeds in South Korea. Under Article 5 of the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act and related Regulations, an exporter must collect foreign exchange proceeds within six months from the date of export clearance unless the MOEF approves an extension. Proceeds must be received into a domestic foreign exchange bank account or a registered overseas account, and the receiving bank must report such receipt to the BOK within the prescribed reporting cycle. The exporter may freely hold, sell, or convert KRW without any statutory percentage requirement, subject to the bank's internal FX transaction documentation procedures.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Korea (BOK) in coordination with the Ministry of Economy and Finance (MOEF) and the Foreign Exchange Administration

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Low. Offshore digital financial institutions and fintech EMI platforms such as Wise, Revolut Business, or Airwallex are not licensed to provide local KRW current accounts or IBAN-equivalent accounts in South Korea. The Banking Act ascribes deposit-taking activities to authorized banks only, and the FSC does not permit non-bank EMIs to use a sponsor-bank arrangement in a manner that effectively creates an independent multi-currency account for a Korean domestic entity. Such EMIs may be used only as ancillary payment gateways for cross-border payables where funds are ultimately routed through a licensed Korean bank account. However, this introduces an unnecessary layer of regulatory friction for a software company that needs to settle local payroll and VAT obligations. Therefore, the only viable compliance path is to open a primary operational account at a Tier-1 domestic bank and to use recognized merchant acquirers for card payment settlement.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

For the initial equity injection from foreign shareholders, the Cusik Hoesa must file a 'Report on Acquisition of Shares by a Foreigner' under the Foreign Investment Promotion Act through the receiving bank (e.g., KB Kookmin or Hana Bank). The recommending bank will issue the 'Certificate of Reporting on Foreign Investment' after verifying the remittance. Stakeholder remittances must use SWIFT MT103 or equivalent with the purpose code '12500 – Foreign Direct Investment in Local Company' or the appropriate BOK Foreign Exchange Reporting purpose code 'K060'. Ample proof of the transaction prior to wiring is essential, including a notarized capital call resolution, a share issuance statement, and the company's corporate registration. The bank will verify the inflow against the foreign investment report and automatically credit the Capital Escrow Account (Invest-Sung Account). For any subsequent injections exceeding USD 10 million, the Bank of Korea's Foreign Exchange Concentration and Administration Office must be notified in advance. The entire capital verification process typically takes 3-5 business days after the subscription amount is cleared, subject to no adverse media hits on the shareholder names.Outbound trade payments, including software royalties and intercompany service charges, should be executed via standard SWIFT MT103 message over the domestic bank's secure corporate e-banking channel. Each payment must be accompanied by an FX purchase application mentioning the appropriate BOK purpose code (e.g., 'D30 – Service Accounting: Software Maintenance' or 'A10 – Trade-Related Sales Commission'). For larger transactional flows exceeding USD 500,000 per occurrence, the bank may request the underlying contract, invoice, and English/Korean translation. For cross-border subscription revenue repatriation to the overseas parent or shareholder, the same MT103 method applies, but withholding tax clearances and treaty benefit certifications must be attached if treaty-exempt dividend rates are claimed. Commercial letters of credit (L/C) are not normally required for a pure software business unless the company is issuing licenses to Korean distributors under a structured off-take arrangement; in such case, a 'Supplier's Facility' L/C at sight or a Documentary Collection (D/P or D/A) can be opened through Hana Bank's or Shinhan Bank's trade finance division, with an underlying master license agreement and IRD (Importer's Remittance/Export Declaration) forms integrated into the bank's trade portal.Forward contracts (plain vanilla FX forwards) with a licensed Korean bank for hedging real commercial transactions denominated in USD/EUR or other foreign currencies, requiring evidence of underlying trade exposure such as invoices or subscription revenue forecasts.Non-deliverable forwards (NDFs) offshore are only permissible for entities with valid underlying exposure and through a domestic bank's offshore affiliate, subject to BOK restriction that no netting arrangement creates a speculative position.Currency swaps are permissible only when the company enters a cross-currency interest rate swap with a Korean bank for the purpose of converting an existing foreign-currency debt facility into KRW, and the notional amount must not exceed the principal of the underlying external borrowing.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Low. Offshore digital financial institutions and fintech EMI platforms such as Wise, Revolut Business, or Airwallex are not licensed to provide local KRW current accounts or IBAN-equivalent accounts in South Korea. The Banking Act ascribes deposit-taking activities to authorized banks only, and the FSC does not permit non-bank EMIs to use a sponsor-bank arrangement in a manner that effectively creates an independent multi-currency account for a Korean domestic entity. Such EMIs may be used only as ancillary payment gateways for cross-border payables where funds are ultimately routed through a licensed Korean bank account. However, this introduces an unnecessary layer of regulatory friction for a software company that needs to settle local payroll and VAT obligations. Therefore, the only viable compliance path is to open a primary operational account at a Tier-1 domestic bank and to use recognized merchant acquirers for card payment settlement.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

National Corporate Income Tax of 24% on taxable income above KRW 300 billion, plus 10% local income tax surcharge, giving a top aggregate statutory rate of 26.4%; lower brackets are 9%, 19% and 21% for income up to KRW 200 million, KRW 20 billion and KRW 300 billion respectively

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

20% statutory withholding tax on dividends paid to a non-resident corporate shareholder, plus 10% local income tax surcharge, making the aggregate statutory rate 22%; treaty rates may reduce the dividend WHT to 5%, 10% or 15% depending on shareholding, treaty and beneficial ownership; a Korean branch paying dividends to its foreign head office is treated separately as branch remittance tax

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: KRW 50,000,000,000 of aggregate cross-border related-party transactions in a fiscal year triggers the mandatory preparation and submission of Master File and Local File transfer pricing documentation; additional simplified documentation or an International Transactions Summary may be required even below this threshold when requested by the National Tax Service

Economic substance scope: Korea does not operate UAE-style Economic Substance Regulations, but Article 14 of the Framework Act on National Taxes incorporates a strong substance-over-form doctrine; a Korean Chusik Hoesa engaged in personal wealth analytics and investment tracking must prove that its Core Income-Generating Activities, including software development, data custody, user contracting, risk management and strategic decision-making, are genuinely performed by Korean employees and management; the National Tax Service and the Financial Intelligence Unit will disregard a paper entity that cannot demonstrate such substance and may apply controlled foreign company rules and treaty abuse defenses

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion by False or Omitted Corporate Income Tax Filing

Statutory sanction: Punishment of Tax Evasion and Tax Offenses Act Articles 10 and 12; Framework Act on National Taxes Articles 47-2 and 47-3; penalties include 20%-40% of underpaid tax, statutory daily late-payment surcharge, criminal fines up to five times the evaded tax and imprisonment for up to five years; the National Tax Service may also issue a demand for restitution of the evaded tax

Director personal liability: Korean Commercial Act Article 401 permits third parties to pierce the corporate veil and hold directors jointly and severally liable for damages caused by intentional or grossly negligent breaches of duty; a director who signs a knowingly false return loses limited liability, may suffer personal asset seizure, be placed on a National Tax Service blacklist, be subject to a travel ban, and be criminally imprisoned
Transfer Pricing Mispricing and Documentation Failure

Statutory sanction: Adjustment of International Taxes Act Articles 5, 6, 11 and 12; the National Tax Service may adjust taxable income to the arm's-length range, impose an additional tax of 20%-40% on the understated tax, and levy fixed administrative penalties for missing or false transfer pricing documentation; unconscious noncompliance also impairs the availability of corresponding adjustments and treaty mutual agreement procedure relief

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly approve non-arm's-length contracts or false documentation may be personally liable for the resulting tax deficiencies under Commercial Act Article 401; a court may disregard the corporate entity if the transactions lack economic substance, exposing the director's personal assets and triggering criminal sanctions under the tax evasion laws, including imprisonment and travel restrictions
Shell Company Abuse and Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: Framework Act on National Taxes Article 14 allows recharacterization of sham transactions; Adjustment of International Taxes Act Article 17 imposes controlled foreign company rules on the Korean beneficial owners; Foreign Exchange Transactions Act violations carry administrative fines and potential criminal penalties; the National Tax Service may assess corporate income tax, local income tax and penalty taxes on the substance owner and cancel any benefit obtained through the shell

Director personal liability: If the corporate form is only a sham, courts may lift the veil and hold directors personally liable for restitution of taxes, interest and penalties; directors face seizure of personal assets, blacklisting by the National Tax Service, travel bans, and criminal imprisonment for intentional fraud or violation of foreign exchange reporting obligations; the limitation of liability characteristic of a Chusik Hoesa is lost when the director personally participates in the misuse of the corporate form
