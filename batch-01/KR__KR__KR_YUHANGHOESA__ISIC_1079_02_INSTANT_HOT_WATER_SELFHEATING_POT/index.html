Registered Agent & Physical Address Licensed Agent Required

South Korean tax and commercial registration law requires a registered principal office address that is a physical location where books, records, and management personnel are accessible. Virtual office arrangements are generally permissible for non-manufacturing service entities, but for a self-heating instant food manufacturer classified under ISIC 1079, the local tax authority and food safety regulator require a production facility and a verifiable place of business. The registered office must match the business registration certificate and the food manufacturing license address for statutory inspections.

Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

No minimum paid-up capital is prescribed for a Yuhan Hoesa; however, all subscribed capital must be fully deposited into a temporary escrow account maintained at a Korean bank prior to application for incorporation, in the name of the company under construction, and a deposit balance certificate must be obtained from the bank. The deposited capital remains blocked until the incorporation registration is completed, and withdrawal prior to registration may invalidate the incorporation and subject the incorporators to personal liability.

Exports of goods are zero-rated for VAT if the exporter provides a customs export declaration (Bill of Entry), Bill of Lading, and proof of foreign exchange repatriation (Inward Remittance Certificate or bank statement). Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

South Korea maintains a hybrid capital and current account convertibility regime under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act. Current account payments meeting genuine trade, service, and dividend remittance obligations are generally convertible on demand, whereas capital account transactions such as inward direct investment, borrowing, and outward investment are subject to duty-to-report requirements and, for certain products, approval from the Ministry of Economy and Finance. A foreign-owned Yuhan Hoesa must document the underlying economic substance and foreign exchange declaration for each capital crossing. There are no mandatory surrender requirements in the sense of a fixed quota to be swapped into Korean won, but export proceeds must be received within statutory collection periods through a domestic foreign exchange bank, and any export receipt that remains in a foreign currency account must be registered and managed under a permitted foreign exchange account. Profit remittance of dividends is permitted if audited retained earnings support the distribution, but the paying bank will require evidence of corporate tax filing, the foreign investment report, and conversion through the licensed domestic bank. Practical hurdles include shortage or mismatch of prior tax/customs clearance and incomplete source-of-funds documentation from overseas investors.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act, export proceeds must generally be collected within six months from the date of the export declaration, unless an extended collection period is approved by the designated foreign exchange bank. There is no hard statutory obligation to convert all export receipts into Korean won; exporters may hold export receipts in deposited foreign exchange accounts up to specified limits, but any conversion to KRW must be executed through a licensed bank and the receipt reported in the schedule of foreign exchange receipts and payments. Goods shipped under wholesale trade or bonded processing require a separate certificate and additional documentary verification.

Central bank supervision: Bank of Korea (BOK); Ministry of Economy and Finance as foreign exchange policy and supervisory authority under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low - Not independently viable. Offshore digital EMIs without a Financial Services Commission license under the Electronic Financial Transactions Act cannot legally open domestic Korean won accounts or handle cross-border transfers independently; they may only support payments through licensed domestic banks. For this Yuhan Hoesa, the required capital escrow, export settlement, and FX purchase/sale functions must be held with a Tier-1 domestic commercial bank. An EMI cannot serve as the sole treasury rail and should be considered only as a supplementary technology layer anchored to a licensed domestic bank account.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Local File is mandatory when Korean domestic intercompany transactions with foreign related parties equal or exceed KRW 5 billion in the fiscal year; Master File is required when the multinational enterprise group's Korean entity reaches KRW 50 billion of foreign-related-party transactions or the group meets consolidated revenue thresholds set by the National Tax Service. Separate disclosure of international transactions is required when aggregate related-party transactions exceed KRW 100 million. Thresholds are updated by NTS; robust documentation is required regardless of threshold if an advance pricing agreement or TP audit is pending.

Economic substance scope: Korea is not an ESR-designated jurisdiction in the UAE sense, but it applies a strict substance-over-form principle through Framework Act on National Taxes Article 14 and the International Tax Adjustment Act. DTA benefits require the recipient to perform the Core Income-Generating Activities (CIGA) in its residence state: substantive decision-making, payroll, office premises, board meetings, and bank accounts. A Yuhan Hoesa that claims treaty relief through a shell holding company fails the CIGA test; the NTS will reallocate income to the Korean entity, deny reduction, and impose late-payment and under-reporting penalties.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Transfer Pricing

Statutory sanction: Framework Act on National Taxes Articles 47-2 and 47-3: 20% unfiled penalty and 40% fraudulent misdeclaration penalty; Corporate Tax Act reassessment; Punishment for Tax Evasion Act Article 3: imprisonment up to three years and a fine up to twenty times the evaded tax.

Director personal liability: Under the Korean Commercial Act Articles 399 and 401, a director who knowingly approves false invoices is jointly and severally liable for damages to the company and creditors. The National Tax Service may impose a departure restriction (travel blacklist) on the director for confirmed tax arrears; if the entity is treated as a sham, courts may pierce the corporate veil and attach the director's personal assets. Criminal imprisonment applies if the director directed or aided the fraudulent transfer pricing.
Shell Company and DTA Abuse

Statutory sanction: International Tax Adjustment Act Articles 4 and 5 (income reallocation), Framework Act on National Taxes Article 14 (substance over form), denial of treaty benefits under OECD MLI and the principal purpose test; administrative penalty of 40% for fraudulent misdeclaration under Article 47-3, plus reassessed tax, daily delinquency surcharges, and civil liability for unpaid withholding.

Director personal liability: Directors of the Korean payor who deliberately participate in the sham arrangement risk joint liability as withholding agents, personal fines, and criminal sanction under the Punishment for Tax Evasion Act. The NTS can issue a travel ban and collect from personal assets where corporate funds were diverted through the shell; foreign directors may also face mutual administrative assistance and extradition in serious cases.
AML and Unreported Cross-Border Currency/Customs Violation

Statutory sanction: Foreign Exchange Transactions Act Article 29: imprisonment or fines for unreported cross-border remittance; Customs Act Article 270: smuggling penalties up to five to ten times the evaded duties; Specific Financial Information Act Article 6: fines and imprisonment for suspicious transaction reporting failures; VAT and corporate tax reassessment on the concealed import costs.

Director personal liability: Directors and responsible compliance officers may be criminally prosecuted under the Specific Financial Information Act and Customs Act; personal asset forfeiture and civil restitution are available. The NTS and customs authorities can impose a travel ban/blacklisting on directors with outstanding liabilities or pending criminal investigation. If the corporate form is used as a facade for money laundering, the veil is pierced and directors become personally liable for unpaid taxes and penalties.
