Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Pass_Through_Eligible: The SCSp is a fiscally transparent partnership without legal personality. Luxembourg income tax law allocates profits on a pro-rata basis to partners, who are taxed in their own hands; the SCSp itself is not subject to corporate income tax (CIT) or net wealth tax. The SCSp may, however, be the taxpayer for municipal business tax (impôt commercial communal, ICC/ICB) where it carries on a commercial, industrial or craft activity. For VAT purposes, the SCSp is a standalone taxable person ('assujetti') in its own right for all its taxable supplies, subject to the small-enterprise exemption.

Available election forms

No domestic check-the-box election exists under Luxembourg law; the SCSp is per se transparent. Foreign partners may make classification elections in their home jurisdictions (e.g., US check-the-box under Treas. Reg. § 301.7701-3), and Luxembourg will respect the resulting foreign treatment only to the extent compatible with the applicable double tax treaty and the ATAD II hybrid mismatch rules. Partners may also apply for an advance tax ruling from the ACD (law of 30 December 2009) to confirm transparency, profit allocation and treaty eligibility, and with the AEDT for an advanced VAT agreement. Hybrid mismatch elections/notifications under ATAD II may be required where the SCSp acts as a reverse hybrid.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

17%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

EUR 35,000 annual taxable turnover (small-enterprise exemption, Article 57 VAT law); EUR 10,000 annual intra-EU distance sales threshold (OSS applicability); non-established foreign suppliers are registrable from the first euro of taxable turnover in Luxembourg; importation of goods triggers VAT registration and accounting obligations irrespective of turnover.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A 0% rate (exemption with credit) applies to goods, including automotive replacement parts and tuning accessories, dispatched or transported by the SCSp to destinations outside the EU under Article 155 of the Luxembourg VAT Law, provided the export is demonstrated by a valid customs export declaration and the physical movement of goods out of EU customs territory is evidenced. Supplies of goods to VAT-registered EU customers are zero-rated as intra-Community supplies under Article 156 subject to a valid customer VAT number and EC sales list reporting. For B2C intra-EU distance sales, VAT is payable in the Member State of destination via OSS; imports of low-value consignments (≤ EUR 150) may benefit from IOSS. Digitally delivered tuning software/services may be zero-rated where the place of supply is established outside the EU under the B2B/B2C place-of-supply rules.

Luxembourg currently imposes no real-time clearance, continuous transaction controls (CTC) or digital stamping for domestic B2B e-invoicing. B2G invoices must comply with EU Directive 2014/55/EU (PEPPOL). Luxembourg is transposing the EU 'VAT in the Digital Age' (ViDA) package: mandatory electronic invoicing for intra-EU B2B supplies is expected from 1 July 2028, with real-time digital transaction reporting via the eTVA/Publientax API (REST/SOAP with eIDAS certificates; accepted formats: UBL, Factur-X, PEPPOL BIS). Until ViDA applies, VAT returns, OSS/IOSS filings, EC sales lists and customs declarations are filed electronically; original commercial invoices must be retained in original form for 10 years.

The AEDT and Luxembourg Customs may claw back incorrectly zero-rated exports by reassessing VAT at 17%, plus late-payment interest of 0.8% per month, and an administrative penalty of up to 10% of the tax amount (minimum EUR 250 per formal documentation failure). Where fraudulent zero-rating or missing-trader fraud is established, criminal prosecution applies with fines of up to EUR 25,000 (or double the tax evaded in cases of repetition) and imprisonment of up to 5 years. The limitation period for reassessment and clawback is extended from 5 to 10 years in cases of fraud. The AEDT may also suspend or exclude the taxpayer from OSS/IOSS schemes, restrict future refund claims, and publish the entity's name as an unreliable exporter.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Luxembourg is a Eurozone member with no exchange-control regime. Capital and current account transactions are fully liberalised under Article 63 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union, subject only to exceptional EU safeguard measures and sanctions screening. There are no restrictions on non-resident ownership of the SCSp, no prior administrative approval for profit remittance from a retail trading entity, and no limitation on converting EUR into foreign currency or vice versa at market rates. Practical hurdles arise solely from BCL balance-of-payments reporting obligations, CSSF transaction monitoring expectations, and the need to evidence economic substance. Dividend or distribution remittances by an SCSp to non-resident partners are permissible, but must be supported by audited or accountant-prepared financial statements, tax certification and a partner resolution; withholding tax at 15% may apply under Luxembourg domestic law where distributions are treated as excessive or where the partnership is not fiscally transparent, although treaty and EU directives may reduce or eliminate this for eligible beneficial owners.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No mandatory surrender requirement exists under Luxembourg or EU law for export receipts. Export proceeds from online sales of automotive aftermarket parts may be received in EUR or foreign currency and retained in ordinary commercial bank accounts or designated foreign-currency accounts without conversion. The SCSp is free to choose the timing and counterparty for conversion to EUR, provided the underlying export transactions are documented by commercial invoices, transport evidence and bank payment purpose codes for BCL external statistics. There is no statutory conversion ratio, timeline or repatriation deadline.

Central bank supervision: Banque centrale du Luxembourg (BCL), acting in cooperation with the Commission de Surveillance du Secteur Financier (CSSF) for banking and AML/CFT supervision, and with the Luxembourg Ministry of Finance for monetary and external statistics policy.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Medium feasibility with strict structural limits. Luxembourg-licensed Electronic Money Institutions (EMIs) and EU/EEA passported EMIs are workable for merchant collection of online card payments, including Stripe and PayPal settlement, and can provide SEPA IBANs for low-value trade flows. However, an offshore or non-EEA EMI is not a prudent primary banking rail for a Luxembourg SCSp: it is not treated as a CSSF-regulated credit institution, may expose the partnership to sanctions screening and source-of-funds weaknesses, and often prevents the bank from obtaining a satisfactory full audit trail. EMIs should be used only as sub-acquirers or customer-payment front-ends, with funds swept daily to the SCSp's credit institution account in Luxembourg or another fully regulated EU bank.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Capital contributions from partners should be routed via SEPA Credit Transfer for EUR or via SWIFT MT103 for non-EUR currency, using an explicit payment purpose description and internal reference such as 'SCSP CAPITAL CONTRIBUTION / PARTNERSHIP AGREEMENT Cl. X'. The MT103 should carry the ISO 20022 external purpose code 'INVS' for a capital investment or 'TRAD' where the payment represents pre-export trade-related working capital; a free-format remittance line should identify the contributing partner, the SCSp and the fiscal year. The receiving bank will require a signed capital account ledger, the notarised partnership deed and, where applicable, evidence of the partner's board approval. The BCL will receive the transaction through the bank's balance-of-payments reporting, so the SCSp must confirm the underlying commercial purpose and refrain from mixing personal and corporate flows. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Medium feasibility with strict structural limits. Luxembourg-licensed Electronic Money Institutions (EMIs) and EU/EEA passported EMIs are workable for merchant collection of online card payments, including Stripe and PayPal settlement, and can provide SEPA IBANs for low-value trade flows. However, an offshore or non-EEA EMI is not a prudent primary banking rail for a Luxembourg SCSp: it is not treated as a CSSF-regulated credit institution, may expose the partnership to sanctions screening and source-of-funds weaknesses, and often prevents the bank from obtaining a satisfactory full audit trail. EMIs should be used only as sub-acquirers or customer-payment front-ends, with funds swept daily to the SCSp's credit institution account in Luxembourg or another fully regulated EU bank.

Stage 06

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

The Luxembourg SCSp is fiscally transparent; no corporate income tax is levied at the entity level. For a Luxembourg-resident corporate unitholder, the headline IRC rate is 15% for fiscal years commencing on or after 1 January 2025, plus 7% solidarity surcharge (1.05 percentage points) and municipal business tax (6.75 percentage points in Luxembourg City), producing an aggregate statutory rate of 22.80%.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

15% domestic Luxembourg withholding on dividends from a Luxembourg company. Because the SCSp is tax transparent, distributions by the SCSp to unitholders are not treated as Luxembourg-source dividends subject to the 15% withholding, provided no Luxembourg company has distributed profits through the SCSp in a non-transparent manner. The underlying Luxembourg issuer will withhold 15% on dividends paid to the SCSp; corporate non-resident unitholders may claim 0% under the EU Parent-Subsidiary Directive if they hold at least 10% of the share capital for 12 months, or a treaty-reduced rate.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Local File documentation should be prepared where aggregate related-party transactions (auto-parts purchases, platform fees, financing, management and royalty payments) exceed EUR 1,000,000 in a fiscal year; Master File is appropriate where the worldwide group revenue exceeds EUR 750,000,000, and Country-by-Country notification is mandatory above that threshold. Luxembourg substance practice may require a lighter benchmarking memorandum even below the EUR 1,000,000 watermark when the SCSp claims DTA benefits or charges intercompany markups.

Economic substance scope: Luxembourg does not apply Gulf-style Economic Substance Regulations, but the SCSp must have real commercial substance. For the e-commerce activity this means actual administrative presence in Luxembourg, local decision-making on procurement and pricing, active management of inventory and logistics, bank accounts and books kept in Luxembourg, and payroll and office infrastructure. A pure mailbox SCSp that merely invoices auto-parts sales is disregarded under Luxembourg GAAR, Article 56 LIR (transfer pricing), EU ATAD abuse doctrine and the OECD Principal Purpose Test.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and false declaration

Statutory sanction: Administrative tax increases of 50% to 100% of evaded tax, late-payment interest at 0.8% per month, criminal fines up to EUR 100,000, and imprisonment up to 5 years under Luxembourg tax and criminal law.

Director personal liability: The general partner (gérant) assumes unlimited liability for SCSp obligations; where the general partner is a legal entity, its managing directors can be jointly liable for fraudulent misrepresentations, travel restrictions may be imposed, and personal bankruptcy or veil-piercing is available against the beneficial owner who orchestrated the evasion.
Artificial shell or mailbox arrangement

Statutory sanction: The Luxembourg tax administration may reclassify all profit as taxable in the real management jurisdiction, deny treaty benefits under the OECD MLI Principal Purpose Test, disallow cost deductions, impose 10% to 50% penalties, and apply 15% withholding on deemed distributions if the arrangement is treated as non-transparent.

Director personal liability: Directors of the corporate general partner risk personal liability for repayment of taxes, interest and penalties if the shell arrangement was designed to circumvent creditors or tax enforcement; passports may be confiscated in cross-border tax-fraud proceedings.
Transfer pricing manipulation

Statutory sanction: Tax adjustment to the arm's-length price under Article 56 LIR, additional assessment of corporate income tax and municipal business tax, 10% to 50% penalties, mandatory interest, and termination of intellectual property cost-sharing arrangements if the SCSp lacks substance.

Director personal liability: The gérant or directors who knowingly signed or caused the inaccurate pricing may be personally assessed for restitution if the company is dissolved or insolvent, and could be prosecuted for tax fraud leading to imprisonment.
AML or KYC non-compliance by e-commerce payment intermediaries

Statutory sanction: CSSF administrative fines under Luxembourg AML law can reach EUR 1,000,000 or 10% of turnover, plus criminal fines; the SCSp can have its payment-processing account terminated if the beneficial owners are undisclosed.

Director personal liability: Compliance officers and managing directors face disqualification, travel bans and imprisonment up to 5 years for intentional money-laundering facilitation; courts may lift the corporate veil to collect civil damages.
Failure to file or non-cooperation with Luxembourg tax authorities

Statutory sanction: Default assessments computed to the maximum possible tax, 10% automatic penalty and 5% per month incremental penalty up to 50%, 9.6% per annum late-payment interest, and listing in the Luxembourg debt-enforcement register preventing cross-border settlements.

Director personal liability: Directors and legal representatives may be personally liable under Article 710-13 of the Luxembourg Companies Act if the SCSp was stripped of assets before tax liabilities were satisfied; a director ban may be imposed by the criminal court.
